# CI-JEPA: A Counterfactual Analysis of Latent Representations in Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures for Self-Supervised Learning 

Mintu Dutta 1, Ritesh Vyas 2, and Mohendra Roy 1⋆

Abstract. Self-supervised visual representation learning learns useful features without man-ual annotations during representation training. The image-based joint-embedding predictive architecture (I-JEPA) predicts latent representations of masked image regions, but its objec-tive does not explicitly model responses to specified visual interventions. We introduce CI-JEPA, a counterfactual intervention-aware extension that learns to predict the representation change ∆Z = ZCF −Z between an original image and a modified counterpart. We assess repre-sentation robustness through selective sensitivity: stronger responses to task-relevant semantic changes than to nuisance changes. Experiments on Flowers102 use flower-center occlusion as a candidate semantic intervention and background blur and tint as candidate nuisance inter-ventions. With frozen-encoder linear probing, CI-JEPA achieves a best validation accuracy of 78.14%, compared with 77.55% for both the pretrained ViT-B/16 and the I-JEPA base-line, a gain of 0.59 percentage points. The reported mean L2 representation changes are 4.42 for center occlusion, 3.48 for background tint, and 2.83 for background blur. This ordering is consistent with relative semantic selectivity for the evaluated interventions, rather than complete nuisance invariance. The accuracy comparison is complementary and does not es-tablish improved robustness over the baselines. These controlled image modifications provide a framework for studying intervention-induced changes in JEPA representations; they do not establish causal feature discovery or robustness to all visual changes. 

Keywords: Self-supervised learning · I-JEPA · Counterfactual interventions · Explainable AI · Vision Transformer · World models

Motivation for the Counterfactual Block I-JEPA predicts target-region representations from visible context [1]. This objective encourages semantic prediction, but does not explicitly supervise how a representation should respond to a specified visual intervention. Strong recognition accuracy alone therefore cannot establish whether the learned features reflect the object or incidental correla-tions. Evidence for this concern comes from Xiao et al. [2], who showed that object-recognition mod-els can classify images using backgrounds alone and can change their predictions when backgrounds are replaced while foreground objects are retained. These findings motivate testing background dependence; they do not establish that I-JEPA exhibits the same behavior on Flowers102. Our central question is whether an encoder responds differently to changes in potentially discrim-inative object information and changes in nuisance background information. We use flower-center CI-JEPA 3

occlusion as a candidate semantic intervention and background blur and tint as candidate nuisance interventions. We hypothesize that the former produces a larger representation change than the latter. This distinction is an experimental assumption rather than a universal property of flowers: center information may vary in importance across classes, and intervention magnitude or editing artifacts may also influence the measured response. The counterfactual block makes this question part of the learning objective. Paired original and modified images provide a target change, ∆Z , while the intervention-conditioned predictor learns to estimate that change. Unlike simply including modified images as additional training samples, this objective explicitly relates an intervention to its effect in latent space. It also avoids assum-ing that every modification should leave the representation unchanged, since removing relevant object information may legitimately change it. Counterfactual visual explanations have used spa-tial replacements to examine changes in class predictions [3]; here, we instead train a predictor of intervention-induced representation changes. The primary motivation for using counterfactuals in our experiments is to assess the robustness of the learned representations. We define robustness as selective sensitivity: the representation should respond to changes in semantic information while remaining relatively stable under nuisance changes. Thus, greater sensitivity to semantic counterfactuals than to nuisance counterfactuals is the desired behavior under our evaluation criterion. The main contributions of this work are as follows: (1) we add an intervention-aware counterfac-tual branch to I-JEPA; (2) we generate several controlled counterfactual versions of flower images; (3) we measure how each intervention changes the learned representation; (4) we compare nuisance changes, such as background modifications, with meaningful changes, such as flower-center occlu-sion; and (5) we assess representation robustness through the relative sensitivity to semantic and nuisance counterfactuals. 

## 2 Background 

There is a substantial body of research on model explainability [4,5,6,7], as well as on causal and interventional representation learning [8,9]. Counterfactual learning [3] examines how a model would respond if part of its input were changed while other information remained the same. In computer vision, this is done by creating a modified version of an image and comparing the model outputs for the original and modified images. Several studies have generated counterfactual examples to improve model explanations and test robustness. For example, “Counterfactual Visual Explanations” [3] generated counterfactual expla-nations by replacing selected spatial regions with regions from a distractor image to change the pre-dicted class. “Generating Visual Explanations” [10] generated class-discriminative natural-language explanations of visual recognition decisions. Causal representation learning methods have also used interventions to examine how changes in underlying factors affect learned features [11,12]. Our work is related to these approaches but has a different focus. We apply controlled visual interventions directly to an I-JEPA-based representation learning framework. Rather than using counterfactual images only to explain the final class label, we measure how each intervention changes the internal representation. We also compare two types of changes: nuisance interventions, such as background modifications, and semantic interventions, such as covering the center of the flower. 4 M. Dutta, R. Vyas, and M. ## 3 Proposed Methodology 

3.1 Overview 

Our approach starts with the original I-JEPA architecture and adds a counterfactual branch. The original image is processed by an encoder to produce the representation Z. We then generate a counterfactual version of the image. The same encoder produces a new representation, ZCF , for the counterfactual image. The difference between these two representations indicates how strongly the intervention affected the model: ∆Z = ZCF − Z. The overall architecture, illustrated in Figure 1, integrates the original and counterfactual processing paths so that the predictor learns both masked-region representations and intervention-induced representation changes.

The counterfactual loss in Equation 9 supplies a complementary training signal: it measures how accurately the predictor models the change caused by each intervention, whereas the original objective measures masked-region prediction. Retaining both objectives is intended to preserve useful semantic features while making intervention responses predictable. The intervention embedding and binary mask provide conditioning information for this additional task; their contribution to robustness requires empirical evaluation rather than following automatically from the architecture. The counterfactual branch, shown in Figure 2, is designed to estimate the counterfactual repre-sentation ZCF . Learning Objective The original I-JEPA loss teaches the model to predict hidden representations. We add a second objective that teaches the model to predict how the representation should change after an intervention. The total loss becomes: 

Ltotal = LJEPA + λLCF . (10) This allows the model to learn both semantic prediction and intervention-aware representations. 

Evaluation Methods We trained three models: a pretrained Vision Transformer (ViT), the orig-inal I-JEPA with ViTs as the context and target encoders, and our proposed I-JEPA with coun-terfactual block. We kept all configurations, including image size, patch size, embedding dimension, learning rate, and dropout, consistent across the models. After training, we froze each encoder and trained a linear classifier to compare model performance [14]. We measure the representation distance between each original image and its counterfactual version. Smaller distances indicate lower sensitivity; however, their interpretation depends on the type of intervention. Low sensitivity to nuisance changes is desirable, whereas a stronger response to semantic changes indicates that the representation retains that information. We therefore evaluate robustness through the combination of semantic sensitivity and nuisance stability. Robustness Assessment and Limitations The reported mean L2 changes are 4.42 for center occlusion, 3.48 for background tint, and 2.83 for background blur (Table 2). The larger response to the semantic counterfactual than to the nuisance counterfactuals is consistent with the selective-sensitivity criterion used in our robustness assessment. However, the nuisance responses remain nonzero, so the results indicate relative selectivity rather than complete nuisance invariance. Using the reported mean L2 changes, the SIR is approximately 4 .42 /2.83 = 1 .56 for center oc-clusion relative to background blur and 4 .42 /3.48 = 1 .27 relative to background tint. These are ratios of the reported mean effects, not means of per-image ratios. Intervention effects are not re-ported for the baseline encoders, so these results do not establish improved selective sensitivity over the baselines. Differences in intervention strength, edited area, and editing artifacts may also con-tribute to the observed ordering. The reported best validation accuracies do not constitute held-out test results, and the 0.59-percentage-point gain should not be interpreted as statistically significant without repeated-run uncertainty estimates. 

## 4 Conclusion 

This paper introduced a counterfactual intervention-aware extension of I-JEPA to study the robust-ness of learned representations. Our experiments use counterfactuals as controlled tests of whether a representation responds more strongly to semantic changes than to nuisance changes. Under our operational criterion, a more robust representation retains sensitivity to semantic information while remaining relatively stable under nuisance variations. The counterfactual block predicts intervention-induced changes, while representation distances and the Selective Intervention Ratio assess the de-sirability of the measured response pattern. CI-JEPA 9

The reported representation changes are greater for flower-center occlusion than for background blur or tint, consistent with the desired semantic-over-nuisance ordering. This supports relative selectivity for the evaluated interventions, rather than a guarantee of robustness to all visual changes or proof of causal feature discovery. Classification accuracy is complementary to this assessment and does not replace it. Future work will compare intervention sensitivity across baseline models, control intervention strength and edited area, and extend the evaluation to concept-level interventions and additional datasets.
