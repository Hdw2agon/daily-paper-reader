# Retraction-Based Gradient Projection Algorithms on Manifolds 

## Conglong Xu, Hao Wu ∗

Department of Mathematics, The George Washington University 

Abstract 

We introduce a framework for retraction-based convex optimization on Riemannian manifolds, which includes a notion of retraction-specific convex sets and retraction-based gradient projection algorithms. The standard theory of gradient projection algorithms generalizes easily to this framework. Within this framework, we establish convergence results for retraction-based gradient projection algorithms with various stepsize rules. As an application, we use our framework to study the weighted low-rank approximation. We also provide numerical validation of our convergence results on the image completion task. 

## 1 Introduction 

In this paper, we extend the theory of gradient projection algorithms from Euclidean spaces to Riemannian manifolds. More precisely, we introduce a notion of retraction-specific convex sets on manifolds in Section 2 and establish retraction-based gradient projection algorithms on manifolds and their convergence in Section 3. This provides a more general framework for convex analysis on manifolds than the existing theory of geodesic convexity. In Section 4, we apply our algorithms to the weighted low-rank approximation and numerically validate their convergence on the image completion task. 

Related Work and Our Contributions. The existing theory of geodesic convexity in differential geometry has motivated recent efforts to extend the theory of convex optimization from Euclidean spaces to Riemannian manifolds (Bécigneul and Ganea 2019; Nguyen et al. 2019; Alimisis et al. 2021; Martínez-Rubio 2022; Weber and Sra 2023b,a; Criscitiello and Boumal 2023; Hu, Wang, and Abernethy 2023; Fan, Yang, and Vemuri 2023; Han et al. 2024; Yao, Chen, and Yang 2024; Wang et al. 2024; Roux, Martínez-Rubio, and Pokutta 2025; Cheng and Weber 2026; Carrillo et al. 2026; Hirai, Nieuwboer, and Walter 2026; Vishnoi 2018). Our notion of retraction-specific convex sets explicitly encodes convexity on manifolds via their tangent bundles and generalizes the existing notion of geodesically convex sets. The theory of gradient projection methods on Euclidean spaces is a classical topic in optimization. Rosen’s gradient projection methods for linearly and nonlinearly constrained nonlinear programming (Rosen 1960, 1961), along with the constrained minimization work of Levitin and Polyak (Levitin and Polyak 1966), established the early theoretical framework for the projection-based feasible direction methods on Euclidean spaces. The studies on Armĳo’s sufficient-decrease rule (Armĳo 1966) and the feasible-direction convergence (Topkis and Veinott 1967) provide the backtracking line-search framework adapted in this paper. The expository work in (Bertsekas 2016) provides a detailed survey on the theoretical framework of the gradient projection methods on Euclidean spaces, which inspired our theoretical framework. The studies of Euclidean gradient projection methods led to numerous applications on, for example, gradient projection methods for linearly constrained problems (Calamai and Moré 1987), active-set and bound-constrained quadratic programming methods (Moré and Toraldo 1991), spectral projected-gradient methods (Birgin, Martínez, and Raydan 2000, 2014), and projected Barzilai-Borwein methods (Dai and Fletcher 2005), among other problems. But attempts to extend these methods to Riemannian manifolds are scarce. Some recent works (Mahler et al. 2024; Xiong et al. 2026) mentioned “gradient projection onto manifolds”. However, the algorithms they established are applications or extensions of the orthogonal projection of Euclidean gradients onto tangent spaces in some machine learning tasks, which are unrelated to convex optimization on manifolds. The works in (Absil, Mahony, and Sepulchre 2008; Absil and Malick 2012) systematically introduce the projection-like retractions on Riemannian manifolds and their use in matrix-manifold optimization as computationally efficient approximations of exponential maps. The work in (Boumal 2023) gives a comprehensive treatment of retraction-based Riemannian optimization methods, including their applications in geodesically convex settings. These expository works on Riemannian optimization motivated the retraction-based algorithm designed in this paper. Moreover, the studies of the convergence and complexity of retraction-based Riemannian first-order methods in (Bento, Ferreira, and Melo 2017) partly inspired our convergence results for the Riemannian gradient projection methods.

4.2 Numerical Validation 

We provide experimental results for Algorithm 1 with the Armĳo Stepsize Rules 3.2 and 3.3 to validate our convergence results in Proposition 3.7. We used the image completion task (modeled as WLRA) as our experimental setting and drew data from the training splits of the MNIST and grayscale CIFAR-10 datasets (LeCun et al. 1998; Krizhevsky 2009). To save computational resources 3, we randomly sampled 6000 images from the MNIST training split and 5000 images from the CIFAR-10 training split. The samples were balanced across all image categories in both datasets. We followed the steps below to simulate the missing data in the image completion task: 1. We flattened every sampled image to a column vector and concatenated all the flattened vectors to form a matrix of size 

784 × 6000 (for MNIST) or 1024 × 5000 (for CIFAR-10). We used this matrix as the data matrix A.

## 5 Conclusion and Final Remarks 

In this paper, we establish a framework for retraction-based convex optimization on Riemannian manifolds, which includes a notion of retraction-specific convex sets and retraction-based gradient projection algorithms. We provide convergence results for the algorithms within this framework. We applied our algorithms to study the weighted low-rank approximation and numerically validate our convergence results. 

Limitations. Wen and Yin (Wen and Yin 2013) introduced the Cayley transform as an iterative scheme for preserving the orthogonality of iterates on the Stiefel manifold. Recent studies used the Cayley transform to design optimization algorithms on the Stiefel manifold for training large-scale neural networks (Li, Li, and Todorovic 2020; Kume and Yamada 2024). However, the Cayley transform is not a retraction and therefore falls outside the scope of our framework. The convergence analysis of stochastic gradient projection methods for constrained non-convex programming problems on Euclidean spaces is an emerging topic in the recent deep learning literature (Bianchi and Jakubowicz 2013; Ghadimi, Lan, and Zhang 2016; Davis and Drusvyatskiy 2019; Alacaoglu and Lyu 2023; Zheng and Lamperski 2026). However, the algorithms in this paper are deterministic. Extending our framework to Cayley transform and stochastic gradient projection algorithms on manifolds could be interesting directions for future research.

For the “Feasible Direction” routine, the mean difference is −2.463 × 10 −4 (t107 = −0.9209 , p = 0 .1796 ). For the “Projection Arc” routine, the mean difference is −2.206 × 10 −4 (t107 = −0.8556 , p = 0 .1971 ). Both mean differences are negative, but both p-values exceed the significance threshold. Thus, the pooled long-run analysis does not provide statistically significant evidence that either main routine has lower final RMSE than the benchmark routine. This aggregate result does not rule out the configuration-specific advantages shown in Figures 1, 2, and 3. 

C.5 Our Choice of RMSE as the Primary Evaluation Metric 

The root mean square error (RMSE) is widely used to evaluate accuracy in matrix-completion tasks (Mazumder, Hastie, and Tibshirani 2010; Zhou and Tao 2013; Hsieh and Olsen 2014; Ganti, Balzano, and Willett 2015; Hastie et al. 2015; Bhaskar 2016; Wang, Zhang, and Gu 2017; Liu, Mao, and Wong 2020; Elmahdy et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2021; Salakhutdinov and Mnih 2007, 2008). We choose RMSE as our primary evaluation metric for two additional reasons. First, Proposition 3.7 states that every limit point of the sequence generated by Algorithm 1 is stationary when using Armĳo Stepsize Rules 3.2 or 3.3. It does not provide an asymptotic convergence-rate guarantee. Accordingly, our experiments compare completion accuracy through RMSE rather than infer empirical convergence rates from objective values. 28 Moreover, Problems 4.2 and 4.3 have different objective functions. Their objective values are therefore not directly comparable across routines. In particular, the benchmark objective (the objective function of Problem 4.2) includes the regularization term 

λ∥x∥2, whereas the main-routine objective (the objective function of Problem 4.3) does not. Thus, the benchmark objective can differ from the main-routine objective at initialization, and its value varies with the regularization parameter λ. RMSE applies the same definition to all three routines and allows a direct comparison on a common scale. 

## D Reproducibility Details 

D.1 Datasets and Preprocessing 

The experiments use the training splits of MNIST and CIFAR-10. For the sampled experiments, the data artifacts contain 6000 balanced MNIST images and 5000 balanced CIFAR-10 images. The full-training Stage 1 experiments use all 60000 MNIST training images and all 50000 CIFAR-10 training images. Each image is flattened and stored as one column of a matrix. The resulting sampled matrices have sizes 784 × 6000 for MNIST and 1024 × 5000 for grayscale CIFAR-10. The full-training matrices have sizes 784 × 60000 for MNIST and 1024 × 50000 for grayscale CIFAR-10. Each serialized data artifact is a dictionary containing the complete matrix M_full , the masked matrix M_masked , and the binary weight matrix W.We use masking rates 0.25 , 0.5, and 0.75 for the sampled datasets and masking rate 0.5 for both full-training datasets. The weight is zero at a masked entry and one otherwise. The masked matrix is obtained by multiplying the complete matrix by the weight matrix. We use random seed 42 for dataset sampling and masking. All routines for a fixed dataset, retraction, rank, and regularization value receive clones of the same truncated-singular-value-decomposition initialization. 

D.2 Grid Searches and Long Runs 

The grid searches use ranks p ∈ { 32 , 64 , 128 } and regularization values λ ∈ { 10 −2, 10 −4, 10 −6} with the QR and polar retractions. For each routine and retraction-p-λ configuration, the Armĳo grid is 

(β, σ, s ) ∈ { 0.5, 0.75 , 0.9} × { 0.25 , 0.5, 0.75 } × { 0.3, 0.5, 1.0}.

Thus, each routine has 27 Armĳo grid points per configuration. Every grid-search run uses 200 iterations, maximum backtracking count 200, Armĳo tolerance 10 −12 , CPU execution, and float32 arithmetic. For the main routines, the constraint radius is computed as ∥A ⊙ W ∥F /√λ.For each routine and configuration, successful grid points are ranked by final RMSE and then by final unregularized WLRA loss. For the sampled-dataset routine comparisons reported here, the selected Armĳo parameters seed the 1000-iteration convergence runs. The full-training tables report only Stage 1 grid-search results. If a selected long run fails, the runner records the failed attempt and tries the next successful source-grid candidate. The raw experiment records retain failed runs. The table generator excludes failed records from best and mean RMSE and reports their number separately.
