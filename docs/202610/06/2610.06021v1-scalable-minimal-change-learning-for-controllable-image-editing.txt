## Abstract 

Image editing aims to modify specific attributes of an image while preserving all other aspects. In practice, however, applying even simple editing instructions to existing methods often leads to unintended changes. We identify the central issue: a fundamental causal principle of minimal change , which requires that an intervention alter only the intended attributes in the output while leaving all others invariant, has not been explicitly incorporated as an optimization objective. To encourage minimal change, prior work rooted in causal representation learning typ-ically imposes an L1 regularizer on the latent difference between pre- and post-edit representations. However, this strategy does not scale to modern image editing models: L1 regularization fails to induce true sparsity and instead tends to shrink all latent differences uniformly; sparsity in the latent space does not translate to localized change in the output of nonlinear deep image generation models; and the multi-domain or counterfactual supervision it requires is generally unavail-able. To effectively leverage the minimal change principle for controllable image editing, we cast it as a reinforcement learning objective. Rather than constraining latent representations, we design rewards that directly capture minimal change over intervention outcomes and optimize them explicitly during training. To produce these rewards reliably, we further introduce an agentic reward model that leverages the chain-of-thought reasoning ability of vision-language models without requir-ing human annotations. The idea is that instead of emitting a single, unreliable scalar score, the model reasons explicitly about two complementary failure modes, unimplemented changes and unintended changes, and produces this supervision automatically. Across four benchmarks, ARRO improves average EditScore from 5.21 to 5.88. A region-aware evaluation on 600 examples further shows an 8.4% reduction in off-target pixel change relative to the base editor. Controlled reward and SFT comparisons, blinded human evaluations, and transfer to OmniGen2 pro-vide complementary evidence for the reward design and its applicability beyond one backbone. ## 1 Introduction 

Image editing aims to modify specific attributes of an image while preserving all other aspects. Recent instruction-based editors [Brooks et al., 2023, Zhang et al., 2023, Sheynin et al., 2023, Black Forest Labs et al., 2025, Wu et al., 2025a,b, Deng et al., 2025, Zhang et al., 2025] have made impressive progress on visual quality and instruction following. Yet even simple instructions still trigger widespread, unintended changes: “add glasses” subtly alters the subject’s hair and skin; “change the car’s color” redraws the background; “remove the sign” shifts the lighting of the entire scene. This is not a cosmetic issue but a fundamental one: it determines whether a generative model can be trusted as a controllable editing tool, rather than a high-fidelity sampler that happens to follow instructions most of the time.

minimal change , which requires that an intervention alter only the intended attributes in the output while leaving all others invariant, has not been explicitly incorporated as an optimization objective in modern image editing models. Minimal change is widely applied in causal representation learning [Schölkopf et al., 2021, Peters et al., 2017, Perry et al., 2022, Lopez et al., 2023]. Existing methods (e.g., [Yang et al., 2021, Shen et al., 2022, Komanduri et al., 2022]) operationalize it by imposing an L1 regularizer on the latent difference between pre- and post-intervention representations, encouraging interventions on a single latent factor to leave the remaining factors unchanged. This strategy is difficult to adapt effectively to modern image editing models, due to three obstacles. (O1) L1 does not reliably induce sparsity: it tends to shrink all latent coordinates uniformly toward small values rather than driving most of them to exactly zero [Shabrina et al., 2025], so latent edits remain dense, just quieter. (O2) Latent sparsity does not translate to output locality: modern decoders are large, deeply nonlinear neural networks with a latent-to-image Jacobian that is far from axis-aligned, so even a sparse change in the latent space can propagate into broad, unintended modifications across the output image [Shen et al., 2020, Karras et al., 2020]. (O3) The required supervision does not scale: causal representation learning methods rely on multi-domain datasets with attribute labels or paired counterfactual examples, which are expensive or generally unavailable. We address all three obstacles by reformulating minimal change as a reinforcement learning (RL) objective on intervention outcomes rather than latents. Designing rewards that directly capture minimal change on the output dissolves (O1) and (O2) simultaneously: there is no L1 approximation to optimize, and the decoder’s nonlinearity is no longer a problem, because the reward is computed directly on the output rather than on the latent that produces it. Concretely, the supervised dataset contains only source-instruction pairs (xsrc , c ); during RL, the current policy samples candidate edits 

xi, which are then assigned minimal-change rewards ri according to how well they implement the instruction while leaving everything else intact. 2To address (O3) , a minimal-change reward has to be reliably designed without human annotation. A natural choice would be to ask a vision-language model (VLM) for a single editing quality score, as in recent reward-modeling work [Gong et al., 2025, Luo et al., 2025]. However, directly prompting a VLM for a numerical score is unreliable. First, the score is poorly calibrated: scalar VLM ratings are noisy and high-variance, with thresholds that shift across images, so the same numerical score can mean different things on different inputs [Li et al., 2026]. Second, the score is uninformative about the type of failure: a minimal-change reward must capture two distinct aspects, whether the instructed edit was carried out and whether everything else was left alone, but a single number collapses both into one signal, so the policy cannot tell which aspect it is failing on. To make the minimal-change reward more reliable, we design an agentic reward model: rather than asking the VLM for a numerical score, we leverage its chain-of-thought reasoning ability to perform a structured audit of the edit. We decompose the minimal-change reward into two complementary components: unimplemented changes , where the instructed edit is missing or incomplete, and 

unintended changes , where modifications appear outside the instructed scope. For each component, the VLM reasons over the editing triplet of source image, instruction, and edited output, and produces a list of identified issues. To stabilize the agentic reward model, we further introduce a rubric refinement loop that enforces consistency within each GDPO group (the set of candidate edits sampled for a given source-instruction pair). An LLM first merges the candidates’ audit lists into a single deduplicated rubric. The VLM then performs a second-pass evaluation in which, for each candidate, it selects the applicable items from this shared rubric, and the two reward signals are computed from the selected items. On top of FLUX.1 Kontext, our method lifts average EditScore from 5.21 to 5.88 across four bench-marks (MinEval, MagicBrush, AnyBench, and Emu-Edit). A masked pixel-difference evaluation on 600 examples measures 8.4% less off-target change than the base editor. Under fixed GDPO training, replacing ARRO’s rewards with EditScore Prompt-Following and Consistency rewards yields 3.78 average EditScore, while ARRO achieves 5.88 . On MinEval, ARRO-GDPO also exceeds reward-guided Top-10 SFT ( 5.669 vs. 4.629 EditScore). Blinded human comparisons and transfer to OmniGen2 provide further evidence. These results support supervising the effect of an intervention. 

Contributions. (1) We diagnose three reasons why latent-space sparsity fails to deliver minimal change in modern generative editors ineffective L1 regularization, decoder nonlinearity, and the lack of scalable supervision and recast minimal change as a reinforcement learning objective on intervention outcomes, optimized end-to-end over a flow-matching editor. (2) We design an agentic reward model that, unlike scalar VLM judges, reasons over the editing triplet to produce decomposed efficacy and locality rewards from raw images alone counting unimplemented and unintended changes, respectively stabilized by a group-level rubric refinement step that anchors all candidates to a shared, deduplicated set of canonical issues. (3) We developed MinEval, a dataset of automatically generated edit prompts and source images covering atomic changes across six edit types, with disjoint training and held-out evaluation splits. MinEval is designed to facilitate research into controllable image editing. (4) On top of FLUX.1 Kontext, A RRO improves average EditScore from 5.21 to 5.88 across four benchmarks and reduces masked off-target pixel change by 8.4% on a 600-example evaluation. Reward-pipeline, EditScore-reward, and Top-10 SFT controls, human evaluations, and OmniGen2 transfer probe the source and breadth of these gains (Sec. 4; App. C.1). 

## 2 Related Work 

Instruction-based Image Editing. Following Brooks et al. [2023], recent models like FLUX.1 Kontext [Black Forest Labs et al., 2025] treat locality as an emergent property of pretraining. However, unintended background drift persists in current benchmarks [Qian et al., 2025, Jiang et al., 2026]. Post-training approaches address this via preference optimization [Wallace et al., 2024, Zhao et al., 2025] or online RL [Liu et al., 2025, Li et al., 2026] using learned reward models [Luo et al., 2025, Gong et al., 2025]. We depart from this line in two ways: (1) we argue human preference rewards often conflate aesthetics with adherence rather than penalizing non-minimal changes, and (2) our agentic VLM reward is zero-shot, bypassing the need for expensive, learned reward-model training. 

Flow Matching and Sampling Efficiency. Research on generative flow matching has improved few-step synthesis through model-aligned training couplings [Lin et al., 2025b] and sub-mode 3conditioning for diverse one-step generation [Lin et al., 2026]. In a distinct, discrete-diffusion setting, parallel-in-time sampling accelerates the sequential generation process [Yao et al., 2026]. These works address transport, diversity, and sampling cost; our objective concerns the scope of changes made by an instruction-conditioned editor. 

The Minimal Change Principle. The principle that interventions should perturb only targeted mechanisms originates in causal inference [Pearl, 2009, Schölkopf et al., 2021]. In generative modeling, this is often operationalized through structured latent spaces [Yang et al., 2021, Komanduri et al., 2024] or training-free latent constraints [Kamenetsky et al., 2025, Shabrina et al., 2025]. While we share the conceptual goal of sparse interventions, we argue that enforcing minimal change on a latent passed through a non-linear decoder is a brittle proxy. Instead, we optimize for minimal change directly in the output space via visual auditing. 

Task-specific representations and metrics. EditCLIP [Wang et al., 2025] learns representations of source-to-edit transformations for exemplar-based editing and evaluation; NearID [Cvejic et al., 2026] uses matched-background distractors to learn identity representations; StyleID [Yun et al., 2026] learns a facial-identity metric from perceptual judgments across stylizations. These methods use supervision tailored to a predefined representation or metric. ARRO instead constructs instruction-specific, decomposed reward signals from candidate edits without per-instruction human labels; the resulting issue annotations can also support offline candidate selection. We do not claim that these representation-learning tasks are interchangeable with online editing rewards. 

Safety and Asset Protection. Adjacent work examines visual-instruction jailbreaks of image-to-video models [Zheng et al., 2026], conflict-aware safety guidance for text-to-image diffusion [Xiang et al., 2026], protection of 3D Gaussian splatting assets from instruction-driven editing [Hong et al., 2025], and defenses against image-to-video generation and fine-tuning-based video customiza-tion [Huang et al., 2026]. These studies address misuse and unauthorized modification; our focus is preserving untargeted content when carrying out an authorized image edit. 

Agentic VLM Rewards. Standard VLM-based rewards typically provide scalar scores [Rocamonde et al., 2023, Gong et al., 2025]. Recent work has introduced structured reasoning or binary checklists to improve interpretability [Li et al., 2026, Wu et al., 2025c]. Our approach sits at the intersection of agentic VLMs [Wu et al., 2025d] and RL; rather than a global similarity score, our reward is derived from a structured audit of “unimplemented” versus “unintended” changes. This provides a granular, per-region optimization signal that makes locality a directly optimizable property. Due to the limited space, the extended related work is provided in Appendix A. 

## 3 Method 

We cast minimal change as a reinforcement learning objective in the output space of a generative editor: given a source image xsrc and an editing instruction c, the policy πθ should realize the instructed effect while leaving every other aspect of the output unchanged. This re-frames controllable image editing away from representation-level sparsity, which constrains how the model encodes factors, and toward outcome-level optimization, which constrains what the output looks like after the intervention. An outcome-level reward must therefore observe two complementary failure modes at once: unimplemented changes , where the instructed edit is missing or incomplete, and unintended changes , where modifications appear outside the instructed scope. ## 4 Experiments 

4.1 Setup Training data. We use the ∼10K-prompt training split of MinEval , a dataset we construct by a three-stage VLM pipeline over MagicBrush [Zhang et al., 2023] source images, covering six atomic 7edit types ( add , remove , move , replace , appearance_change , action_change ) and rejection-sampled with the un-tuned base editor to drop unrealizable and trivial prompts; full pipeline, prompts, and checklist are in App. E. 

Evaluation benchmarks. We evaluate on four complementary benchmarks: (1) the held-out 600-prompt split of MinEval (in-distribution; 6 atomic edit types, disjoint from training), (2) Mag-icBrush [Zhang et al., 2023] for human-curated edits from a different pipeline (535 prompts), 

(3) AnyBench [Yu et al., 2024] for breadth across 18 edit types with fine-grained per-type analysis (900 prompts), and (4) Emu-Edit [Sheynin et al., 2023] for out-of-distribution robustness at scale (3,589 prompts; 8 task types). Per-type analysis is concentrated on AnyBench, which is the only benchmark with edit-type labels at this granularity. 

Metrics. The primary metric is EditScore-Overall (ES) [Luo et al., 2025], a Qwen3-VL-8B +LoRA edit-quality judge on [0 , 10] (↑). We complement it with two off-the-shelf preference scorers from different model families: HPSv2 (HPS) [Wu et al., 2023], a CLIP-H based score on [0 , 1] (↑), and 

ImageReward (IR) [Xu et al., 2023], a BLIP-based real-valued score ( ↑). To measure preservation more directly, we also report OffTarget-L1 (↓), the mean absolute source-to-edit pixel difference outside a SAM 2 target mask on 600 randomly selected examples from the four benchmarks; its definition and scope are in App. C.1. The three EditScore sub-scores (PF, Cons, PQ) are in App. C.4. 

Baselines. We compare against six external instruction-edit models InstructPix2Pix [Brooks et al., 2023], MagicBrush [Zhang et al., 2023], OmniGen2 [Wu et al., 2025b], UniWorld-v1 [Lin et al., 2025a], ICEdit [Zhang et al., 2025], BAGEL [Deng et al., 2025] spanning ∼1B–20B parameters and UNet/DiT/MoT families (sizes annotated in Table 1). Our backbone reference is the un-tuned FLUX.1 Kontext-dev [Black Forest Labs et al., 2025] (12B), which isolates the gain attributable to RL fine-tuning. All methods use identical inference settings (Sec. 4.2). 

4.2 Implementation Details Training. We fine-tune FLUX.1 Kontext-dev [Black Forest Labs et al., 2025] (12B) with Mix-GRPO [Li et al., 2025] as the RL backbone, training for 230 steps ( ≈300 GPU-hr on 8×A100-80GB). The reward is produced by Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B-Instruct [Qwen Team, 2025] as the audit/verification VLM ΦV and Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507 [Qwen Team, 2025] as the rubric-aggregation LLM 

ΦL, both served via vLLM [Kwon et al., 2023]. Full hyperparameters are in App. D. 

Reward-generation cost. For a group of 16 candidates, audit, aggregation, and verification took 16.06 seconds and processed 61,950 tokens under the hardware and serving configuration in App. C.1. This measures reward generation rather than end-to-end training throughput. 

Evaluation. All methods are evaluated under identical inference settings (resolution, denoising steps, CFG, seed; details in App. D); metrics follow Sec. 4.1.

Summary. Each ablation exposes a distinct failure mode. Merged scalar (2) : collapsing the dual channels before group normalization drops EditScore by ∼1.5, as the higher-variance signal washes out the other, confirming per-channel normalization is essential. Efficacy only (3) : without locality pressure, the policy over-edits and corrupts non-target context ( −1.12 ES vs. Base). Locality only (4) : by step ∼170 the policy reward-hacks to high-frequency noise, |O i| → 0, and r(2) saturates even though every instruction is unimplemented. The dual-channel design (5) closes both shortcuts: r(1) 

anchors semantic validity so neither over-editing nor empty outputs is rewarded. 

Reward construction and optimization controls. With GDPO and the training configuration fixed, EditScore(PF, Cons) rewards yield 3.777 average ES across the four benchmarks, versus 5.880 for ARRO rewards. Removing aggregation while retaining verification reduces the four-benchmark average ES from 5.880 to 4.961 , supporting the value of a shared, consolidated rubric. In a separate MinEval-only comparison, raw audit counts yield 3.2779 ES, ARRO Top-10 SFT yields 4.6289 , and ARRO-GDPO yields 5.6690 ; these values are not four-benchmark averages. In a controlled CLEVR diagnostic with all requested recolorings implemented, ARRO assigns zero unintended changes for 5–50 requested edits, whereas EditScore Consistency varies despite zero ground-truth unintended changes (App. C.1). Detailed protocols and metrics are in App. C.1. 

Human validation and backbone transfer. Two blinded annotators checked 60 candidate groups (960 candidates) and 565 final issues: they jointly confirmed correct merging for 560 /565 issues, visual support for 553 /565 , and no evident omitted issue in 58 /60 groups. In a separate blinded pairwise study, ARRO wins more comparisons than it loses against EditScore(PF, Cons)-GDPO (Table 4). Applying the same reward pipeline to OmniGen2 with its FlowGRPO optimizer improves ES on all three unseen benchmarks (Table 5) and wins more human comparisons than the original OmniGen2 (Table 4). Study protocols and full transfer metrics are in App. C.2 and App. C.3. 

## 5 Conclusion 

We recast minimal change for instruction-based image editing as an outcome-level RL objective with separate rewards for unimplemented and unintended changes. On FLUX.1 Kontext-dev, A RRO 

improves average EditScore from 5.21 to 5.88 across four benchmarks and reduces masked off-target pixel change by 8.4%. Reward and SFT controls, human evaluations, and OmniGen2 transfer support the value of structured reward construction and direct optimization. 

Limitations and broader impacts. Reward quality is bounded by the VLM’s perception, espe-cially for subtle differences, ambiguous or compositional instructions, and candidate-specific issue assignment. An imperfect judge can introduce systematic errors or be exploited during RL; the expert study validates the evaluated configuration rather than all possible auditors. OffTarget-L1 depends on segmentation and captures pixel change rather than full semantic correctness. We demonstrate a sec-ond rectified-flow editor, OmniGen2, but do not establish transfer to diffusion backbones or quantify 10 multi-seed variability. More controllable editing could also facilitate deceptive image manipulation; deployment requires provenance and misuse safeguards beyond the reward mechanism studied here.

## A Related Work (Detailed) 

Instruction-based image editing: pretraining. The dominant paradigm for instruction-based image editing trains a conditional generative model on large-scale (source, instruction, edit) triplets. InstructPix2Pix [Brooks et al., 2023] pioneered this direction by synthesizing paired training data from a language model and a text-to-image model, enabling a forward-pass diffusion editor to follow free-form instructions without per-example optimization. Subsequent work scaled this recipe along two axes: stronger language understanding through multimodal LLM backbones [Huang et al., 2024, Li et al., 2023], and stronger visual backbones through diffusion-transformer architectures and rectified flow, most notably FLUX.1 Kontext [Black Forest Labs et al., 2025] and Qwen-Image-Edit [Wu et al., 2025a], which explicitly target locality and minimal background drift as a design goal. Across this line of work, locality is treated as an emergent property of data quality, conditioning design, and model capacity; it is not optimized for directly. As reported by recent benchmarks [Jia et al., 2025, Qian et al., 2025, Jiang et al., 2026], even the strongest pretrained editors still leak edits into the background, shift identity, or introduce unintended attribute changes, motivating a post-training stage that targets these failures explicitly. 

Instruction-based image editing: post-training. A second line of work post-trains pretrained editors using preference or reward feedback. Diffusion-DPO [Wallace et al., 2024] adapts direct preference optimization to diffusion models, with follow-up work extending it to richer or per-region preference signals [Zhao et al., 2025, Dunlop et al., 2025, Benarous et al., 2025]. On the online-RL side, Flow-GRPO [Liu et al., 2025] and DanceGRPO [Xue et al., 2025] bring group-relative policy optimization to flow-matching generators, and GDPO [Liu et al., 2026] extends this to multiple rewards. For the editing setting specifically, EditScore [Luo et al., 2025], OneReward [Gong et al., 2025], RewardDance [Wu et al., 2025c], SpatialReward [Long et al., 2026], and ThinkRL-Edit [Li et al., 2026] train dedicated reward models to drive online RL fine-tuning. Our work differs from this line on two fronts. First, human preference is not minimal change. Prior rewards fit human preference data, which mixes many factors at once: visual quality, instruction adherence, aesthetic appeal, and overall plausibility. Whether the edit altered things outside the intended scope is only one of these factors, often the least salient one to a human rater glancing at the result. A reward trained to predict human preference therefore has no reason to penalize unintended changes specifically, especially when the edit otherwise looks plausible. We target minimal change directly instead. Second, prior rewards are not scalable. Each of EditScore, OneReward, RewardDance, SpatialReward, and ThinkRL-Edit is a learned reward model, requiring tens to hundreds of thousands of annotated 15 preference pairs and a training pipeline of its own. Our reward requires no preference data and no reward-model training: an off-the-shelf VLM is prompted to audit each edit, and the two reward components for minimal change are read off directly from its output. 

Minimal change in causal representation learning. The principle that an intervention should perturb only the targeted mechanism originates in causal inference, where it underpins Pearl’s 

do -calculus and the structural-causal-model account of counterfactuals [Pearl, 2009, Peters et al., 2017]. In representation learning, this principle is formalized as the sparse mechanism shift hy-pothesis [Schölkopf et al., 2021, Perry et al., 2022, Lopez et al., 2023]: distribution shifts across environments correspond to sparse changes in a small number of causal mechanisms, and exploiting this sparsity yields identifiability of the latent causal factors [Lachapelle et al., 2024, Xu et al., 2024]. Within deep generative models, CausalVAE [Yang et al., 2021], DEAR [Shen et al., 2022], SCM-VAE [Komanduri et al., 2022], and more recent flow-prior variants [Jin et al., 2026, Komanduri et al., 2024] operationalize minimal change by structuring the latent space around a learned causal graph and pairing it with weak label or counterfactual supervision, so that interventions can be directed at individual latent factors. A parallel, training-free line of work in StyleGAN-era image editing makes the same intuition explicit by adding L1 or sparse-autoencoder constraints to the latent edit at inversion time [Shabrina et al., 2025, Kamenetsky et al., 2025]. Our work follows the same conceptual goal but disagrees on where the constraint should live: we argue that minimal change is fundamentally a property of the output of an intervention, and that enforcing it on a latent that is then passed through a deeply nonlinear decoder is a brittle proxy. 

Minimal change for image generation and editing. The same principle has been brought to image-level tasks via counterfactual image generation under structural causal models [Komanduri et al., 2024, Rasal et al., 2025, Nie et al., 2025, Jing et al., 2026] and StyleGAN-era disentangled face editing with axis-aligned latents or training-free L1/layer-masking constraints [Shabrina et al., 2025, Kamenetsky et al., 2025]. These methods deliver strong locality on narrow domains with attribute labels or counterfactual pairs, but inherit the same three obstacles we identify in Sec. 1 when scaled to free-form instructions; we sidestep all three by measuring minimal change on the generated image and optimizing it end-to-end via an agentic reward.

## E Training Data Construction 

This appendix expands the three-stage data pipeline summarized in Sec. 4.1. Stage (i) synthesizes atomic instructions, stage (ii) filters them through a structured VLM audit, and stage (iii) executes them with the un-tuned base editor and rejects instructions whose candidate score distributions are degenerate. 

E.1 Atomic Edit Types 

Every instruction is constrained to one of six atomic edit types, each with a fixed surface pattern and a closed list of allowed verbs: • add — “ add/insert/put/place <object> to/at <location> ”. • remove — “ remove/delete/erase <object> ”. • move — “ move/relocate <object> from <src> to <dst> ”. • replace — “ replace/substitute/swap <old object> with <new object> ”. • appearance_change — “ change/make/paint <object> ’s <attribute> to <value> ” (color, texture, material). • action_change — “ make/have <animate object> <action> ” (action must be species-appropriate). Instructions whose leading verb conflicts with the declared edit type (e.g., a remove -style verb under 

add ) are rejected at parse time before any VLM call. 

E.2 Instruction Generation and Validation 

For each source image xsrc and edit type t, the VLM is invoked in one of two modes. When a draft instruction c0 is available (e.g., from a prior pass), the validation prompt Pval asks the VLM to audit c0 against a fixed checklist and, when fixable, return a corrected instruction. When no draft is available, or after 5 failed validation rounds, the generation prompt Pgen asks the VLM to propose a fresh instruction from scratch and self-audit it under the same checklist. To improve diversity, the generation call is launched in parallel with 5 different temperatures {0.6, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9, 1.0} and the first candidate that passes the checklist is kept. Both prompts share the same JSON output schema; the validation variant adds an explicit instruction field for the corrected wording, the generation 23 variant a generated_instruction field. We show the validation variant below; the generation variant is identical except that the <instruction_to_validate> block is removed and Step 0 is replaced by “select a single, high-confidence target object”. 

Pval (xsrc , c 0, t ) (instruction-filtering prompt) 

Role: You are validating an image editing instruction. Decide whether the instruction is accurate, complete, and feasible for the given source image. 

Inputs: 

• Source image: {xsrc }

• Instruction to validate: {c0}

• Edit type: {t} (one of add , remove , move , replace , appearance_change ,

action_change ). • Required pattern for t: as defined in App. E.1. 

Step 1: Single-object check. Does the instruction target exactly one object? Reject phrasings such as “and”, “both”, “all”, or plural targets. 

Step 2: Object verification. Look at the source image and decide whether the referenced object actually exists and is correctly identified (i.e., not confused with a similar-looking object). 

Step 3: Completeness check. Verify that all slots required by the edit type are filled (e.g., replace 

requires both old and new object; move requires both source and target locations; add requires both object and location). 

Step 4: State-change check. Compare the current state of the target with the state requested by the instruction. If they already match, the edit is trivial and must be rejected. 

Step 5: Feasibility check. Decide whether the requested change is physically plausible; for 

action_change , additionally check that the action is appropriate for the species of the target. 

Output format. Return a single JSON object and nothing else: 

{"parsed_instruction": { "action": "<verb>", "target": "<single object>", "details": "<...>" }, "single_object_check": {"targets_single_object": true|false, "violation_found": "<...>"}, "object_verification": {"object_exists": true|false, "object_correctly_identified": true|false, "what_i_actually_see": "<...>"}, "completeness_check": {"all_required_fields_present": true|false, "missing_fields": ["<...>"]}, "state_verification": {"current_state": "<...>", "requested_state": "<...>", "states_are_different": true|false}, "feasibility_check": {"physically_possible": true|false, "species_appropriate": true|false|null, "issue": "<... or ’none’>"}, "overall_result": { "feasible": true|false, "instruction": "<original if valid, corrected if fixable, else empty>", "failed_checks": ["<names of any failed checks>"], "reason": "<brief summary>" }}

An instruction is accepted only if every check returns true and the instruction field, after pipeline-level verb-vs-edit-type re-check, is non-empty. Otherwise the failed-check names are fed back to the VLM as a recheck context for up to 5 rounds, after which the pipeline falls back to parallel generation as described above. Instructions that never pass either route are discarded. 24 E.3 Per-edit-type guidance injected into Pval and Pgen 

Both Pval and Pgen inject two type-specific blocks under the placeholder “Required pattern for t”:a type-specific guidance block (what makes a valid instruction of this type) and an evaluation-alignment block (which verifiable criteria the downstream agentic reward model will check). The six blocks below are summaries of the strings we actually inject for each t.

Edit type add 

Pattern. Add [specific object with attributes] to/in [precise location] . Allowed verbs: add, put, place, insert .

Type-specific guidance. Introduce a new object that does not yet exist in the image. Specify (i) the object: type, color, size (e.g., “a large red balloon”); (ii) a precise location with confirmed empty space. Reject if the candidate location lacks empty space sufficient for the object’s size, since adding the object would otherwise displace existing content and create side effects. 

Evaluation alignment. The downstream reward model checks: presence (the new object appears), 

quantity (count matches; always state quantity explicitly), position (at the specified location), attributes 

(color/size/type match), and addition ̸ = replacement (existing objects must remain). Use add/place ,never replace/swap . Prefer empty-space targets so the evaluator can confirm no displacement. 

Edit type remove 

Pattern. Remove the [color/feature] [object] [position disambiguator] . Allowed verbs: remove, delete, erase .

Type-specific guidance. Remove an object that clearly exists and can be uniquely identified. Confirm the object is visible, add a disambiguator when multiple similar objects exist, and verify the identification is correct (not a similar-looking object, e.g., sheep mistaken for bull). 

Evaluation alignment. The downstream reward model checks: true removal (the object must be completely invisible; blurring, painting-over, or covering counts as failure) and disambiguation (the evaluator must be able to locate the exact target). Use remove/delete/erase , never hide/cover/obscure 

(which imply the object is still present). Add enough disambiguators (color, position, size) when multiple similar objects exist. 

Edit type move 

Pattern. Move the [object] from [current position] to [different target position] . Allowed verbs: move, shift, relocate .

Type-specific guidance. Relocate an existing object to a clearly different position. Verify (i) current position (where the object is now ) and (ii) target position (where it should go , must be different from current). Use canonical position equivalences ( LEFT =“left side”, CENTER =“middle”, etc.) when checking that source ̸ = destination. 

Evaluation alignment. The downstream reward model checks: destination (object present at the new position), source clear (gone from the original; no residual or ghost), no duplication (object must not appear at both positions), identity (same object preserved). Always specify both “from [source]” and “to [destination]” so the evaluator can verify both positions; vague phrasing such as “move the cup over” fails because the evaluator cannot confirm source-clear. 

25 Edit type replace 

Pattern. Replace the [existing object] with [specific new object] . Allowed verbs: 

replace, substitute, swap .

Type-specific guidance. Both the old and the new object must be specified explicitly. The old object must be visible in the source image; the new object should be similar in size/shape so it can fit naturally at the same location without major adjustments to the surrounding scene. 

Evaluation alignment. The downstream reward model checks: A gone (old object completely absent), 

B present (new object appears at A’s original location), and no coexistence (A and B must not both be visible; coexistence = failure). Name the old object precisely so the evaluator can confirm absence. Use “replace A with B”, never just “add B” (which implies A stays). 

Edit type appearance_change 

Pattern. Change the [object]’s [attribute] from [current value] to [new value] 

(color, material, texture). Allowed verbs: change, modify, turn, make .

Type-specific guidance. Modify a single appearance attribute of an existing object. Verify (i) the object exists, (ii) its current attribute value is identified accurately, and (iii) the target value is different from the current one. Reject when current value already equals target (e.g., “change the stop sign to red”), or when the named attribute does not exist on the object (e.g., “change the saddle” on an unsaddled horse). 

Evaluation alignment. The downstream reward model checks: identity preserved (object keeps its shape, position, and category), attribute matches (the specified attribute reaches the target value), and 

only the specified attribute changes . Be specific about which attribute is being changed (“color from red to blue”, not “make it different”); vague instructions cause false failures because the evaluator cannot determine the intended boundary. 

Edit type action_change 

Pattern. Make the [specific subject] [new action] instead of [current action] .

Allowed verbs: make, have, change .

Type-specific guidance. Change a subject’s pose, action, or expression to something different and 

species-appropriate . Use species-appropriate action vocabularies (humans: sit/stand/walk/wave/lie down; quadrupeds: lie down/walk/turn head/graze, but not “stand up” since they are already standing on four legs; birds: perch/fly/spread wings/hop). Reject when current action equals target (e.g., “make the person sit down” on someone already sitting). 

Evaluation alignment. The downstream reward model checks: correct subject (the right person/animal performs the action; disambiguate when multiple subjects exist), action visible (target action is clearly visible in a static frame), subject identity preserved . Choose actions that are visually verifiable in a single frame; avoid temporally extended actions like “start walking” or “begin to speak” that require motion to verify. 

E.4 Edit-Level Rejection Sampling 

The instructions that survive App. E.2 are then filtered at the edit level. For every (xsrc , c, t ) triplet we sample a group of N =16 candidate edits G={xi}Ni=1 from the un-tuned base editor (FLUX.1 Kontext-dev) at the training resolution of 720 ×720 , with T =25 denoising steps and i.i.d. noise seeds; this matches the GDPO group used during RL fine-tuning (Sec. 4.2). Each candidate is then scored by the same three-step agentic reward pipeline used at training time: audit, group-level rubric aggregation, and rubric-anchored verification (Eqs. (3)–(6)), yielding the same two reward channels we optimize against:

that is, the negative counts of verified unimplemented and unintended issues for candidate xi.To align with the GDPO advantage estimator, which is identically zero on a group whose rewards are constant, we filter on the variation of these two channels within G. Concretely, we run a two-pass filter: 26 • Pass 1 (unimplemented channel). Drop the group if more than half of its candidates have |U i|=0 ,i.e., if #{i : |U i|=0 } > N/ 2. When a majority of candidates already realize the instruction c in full, the channel-1 reward r(1)  

> i

is dominated by a single mode at 0, the within-group variance of the unimplemented signal collapses, and GDPO receives little advantage signal from this channel. Such groups are dropped regardless of their channel-2 distribution. • Pass 2 (unintended channel). Among groups that pass Pass 1, drop those for which |O i| is also constant across i, or for which std( |O 1: N |)<σ min , or for which the per-group mean of |O i|

exceeds a maximum tolerated count μmax . The first two conditions ensure the locality channel actually carries a within-group signal; the last condition discards prompts on which the base editor changes so much of the source image that the auditor would have to reason over a long, heterogeneous list of differences, which keeps per-item judgments focused and low-variance. For the runs reported in Sec. 4 we use σmin =0 and μmax =999 , so that within Pass 2 only the constant-across-i test on |O i| is active (the std and mean tests are inert at these settings). In this default configuration the filter discards two kinds of groups: those whose unimplemented channel is dominated by zeros (Pass 1, majority-success), and those whose unintended channel is constant across the group (Pass 2, locality-channel constancy). Both are degenerate cases for the group-relative advantage estimator: in the first, the efficacy channel carries little ranking signal because most candidates already realize c in full; in the second, the locality channel literally has zero within-group variance and contributes a zero advantage. Tightening σmin or μmax yields the more aggressive variants used in our preliminary tuning runs but did not change the qualitative ranking. The retained triplets form the ∼10K atomic-edit training set used for RL fine-tuning. 

## F Prompt Templates 

We instantiate the three model invocations in Sec. 3 with the following prompt templates. Each template is a fixed string with placeholders (in {curly braces} ) for inputs. All templates instruct the model to return a JSON object with a fixed schema, which we parse to obtain the required outputs in Eqs. (3), (4), and (5). The audit prompt Paudit and the verification prompt Pverify are designed to share the same reasoning structure: both ask the VLM to parse the instruction, compare the source and edited images, and identify changes belonging to the two failure modes. They differ only in the output mode. The audit is open-ended, asking the VLM to generate the lists eUi and eOi from scratch. The verification is closed-form, asking the VLM to select, from each fixed group rubric, the items present in xi. This shared structure ensures the two stages apply the same notion of "unimplemented" and "unintended" change, and the rubric refinement loop only varies what the VLM is asked to produce , not how it is asked to look .

F.1 Audit prompt Paudit 

The audit prompt is given to the VLM ΦV together with the source image xsrc , the candidate edit xi,and the instruction c, and produces the raw lists ( eUi, eOi).

Paudit (xsrc , c, xi)        

> Role: You are a careful visual auditor. You will be shown two images and a natural-language editing instruction. Your task is to identify two kinds of discrepancies between the instruction and what the edited image actually shows.
> Inputs:
> •Source image: {xsrc }
> •Edited image: {xi}
> •Editing instruction: {c}
> Step 1: Parse the instruction. Identify what the instruction explicitly asks to modify, what the resulting state should be, and what should remain unchanged.

27 Step 2: Compare the two images. Examine the source and edited image side by side and note all visible differences. For each difference, decide whether it falls inside or outside the scope of the instruction. 

Step 3: Generate two lists. 

1. Unimplemented changes ( eUi): aspects of the instruction that the edited image fails to realize, including complete omission, partial execution, or incorrect application. Return an empty list if the instruction is fully realized. 2. Unintended changes ( eOi): visible modifications that fall outside the scope of the instruction. Include only changes you can actually see; do not speculate. Return an empty list if no such changes are present. Each entry must be a short, self-contained, visually verifiable description of a single change. Do not assign numerical scores. Do not summarize. 

Output format. Return a single JSON object and nothing else: 

{"reasoning": "<your step-1 and step-2 analysis>", "unimplemented_changes": [ "<short description>", ... ], "unintended_changes": [ "<short description>", ... ]}

F.2 Aggregation prompt Pagg 

The aggregation prompt is invoked once per candidate group. It receives the two audit collections in separate fields, applies the instruction filter only to unintended changes, and returns both canonical rubrics in one response. 

Pagg (c, eU1: N , eO1: N )

Role: You are a rubric editor. Given an editing instruction and two collections of free-form issues identified across N candidate edits, construct separate, deduplicated checklists for unimplemented and unintended changes. Keep the two failure modes distinct. 

Inputs: 

• Editing instruction: {c}

• Raw unimplemented issues: candidate-indexed lists { eUi}Ni=1 .• Raw unintended issues: candidate-indexed lists { eOi}Ni=1 .

Step 1: Filter against the instruction. From the unintended collection only, remove descriptions of changes explicitly requested or directly implied by c. Do not apply this filter to unimplemented issues, which describe missing aspects of c.

Step 2: Consolidate each collection separately. Merge descriptions of the same underlying issue, split descriptions that conflate distinct issues, normalize each entry to one atomic visually checkable failure, and drop vague, speculative, or non-visual entries. For example, “the bow was not removed” and “the bow is still visible” form one unimplemented issue; “the wheels became silver” and “the rims changed color” form one unintended issue when they describe the same change. 

Step 3: Produce two rubrics. Write each issue in neutral form so that it can be checked against any candidate. Assign IDs within each rubric. Do not merge an unimplemented issue with an unintended issue. 

Output format. Return a single JSON object and nothing else: 

{"reasoning": "<filtering and consolidation rationale>", "unimplemented_rubric": [ {"id": 1, "issue": "<canonical unimplemented issue>"}, ... 

28 ], "unintended_rubric": [ {"id": 1, "issue": "<canonical unintended issue>"}, ... ]}

The two returned arrays define RUG and ROG , respectively. 

F.3 Verification prompt Pverify 

The verification prompt is invoked once per candidate. It receives both group rubrics and returns separate Boolean verdicts for their issues in one response. As in the audit prompt, the VLM first parses the instruction and compares the source and edited images; verification then selects from the fixed rubrics rather than generating new issues. 

Pverify (xsrc , c, xi, RUG , ROG )

Role: You are a strict visual verifier. Given a source image, an editing instruction, an edited image, and two checklists of canonical issues, decide which issues are visibly present. Keep unimplemented and unintended changes separate. 

Inputs: 

• Source image: {xsrc }

• Edited image: {xi}

• Editing instruction: {c}

• Unimplemented checklist: RUG , with issue IDs and descriptions. • Unintended checklist: ROG , with issue IDs and descriptions. 

Step 1: Parse the instruction. Identify what c asks to modify, the required result, and what should remain unchanged. 

Step 2: Compare the images. Examine the source and edited image side by side. Determine which requested aspects were realized and which visible differences fall outside the scope of c.

Step 3: Select from both checklists. For each unimplemented item, mark true only if the named aspect of c remains absent, partial, or incorrect. For each unintended item, mark true only if that off-target change is visible. When in doubt, mark false . Do not add issues or alter their wording. 

Output format. Return a single JSON object and nothing else: 

{"reasoning": "<instruction and image comparison>", "unimplemented_verdicts": [ {"id": 1, "present": true}, ... ], "unintended_verdicts": [ {"id": 1, "present": false}, ... ]}

The issues marked true in the two verdict arrays form Ui ⊆ R UG and Oi ⊆ R OG . Consequently, one candidate group needs N audit calls, one aggregation call, and N verification calls, for 2N + 1 

reward-generation calls. 29
