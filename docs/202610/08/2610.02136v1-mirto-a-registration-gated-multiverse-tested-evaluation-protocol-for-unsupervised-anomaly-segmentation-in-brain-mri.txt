Abstract. Unsupervised anomaly detection (UAD) methods for brain MRI are ranked by a single score, yet that score rests on choices that are rarely reported: how each anomaly map is aligned with the reference, how and on which data the threshold is set, and which false-positive budget, metric, aggregation and lesion definition are used. We present MIRTO, an eval-uation protocol that makes these choices ex-plicit and measures their effect. It gates the ge-ometry of every comparison with a registration check and label-free diagnostics of known power, sets thresholds on validation data alone and re-ports the false-positive volume actually realised on test, repeats each comparison over 15,552 defensible evaluation pipelines, and attaches paired subject-bootstrap intervals with multi-plicity control. Applied to four UAD methods trained on the same healthy data and tested on 312 BraTS 2020 subjects, MIRTO showed that an axis-order mismatch between stored maps and the reference lowered a diffusion model’s voxel AUROC from 0.873 to 0.583 whilst barely moving its slice-level AUROC. Within each met-ric, the method explained at least 0.95 of the variance in voxel AUROC and AUPRC and 0.77 in Dice, but only 0.14 in lesion sensitivity, where the lesion definition and hit criterion domi-nated. A Dice advantage that was significant at validation thresholds vanished at equal realised false-positive burden, and an exact identity at-tributes it to threshold transfer. A training-free change to REFLECT’s latent aggregation raised Dice at equal burden by 0.052. Nine hypotheses were tested against explicit criteria; because the same cohort served to develop the protocol, all inference is exploratory.

1 Introduction 

Unsupervised anomaly detection (UAD) in brain MRI learns what healthy anatomy looks like and flags whatever departs from it, so that pathology can be localised with-out lesion annotations [9,40]. The field has moved quickly, from variational and context-encoding autoencoders, in-cluding our own StRegA [9,21], to diffusion models con-ditioned on the input [11,54,14,15], rectified flows that correct an image in one step [12], and frozen foundation-model features [24,31]. New methods are usually intro-duced with a table in which they rank first. Such a table is the end of a long chain of decisions, most of which a reader never sees. Every anomaly map lives on the grid of its own preprocessing and must be brought into the space of a common reference; a thresh-old has to be chosen, on some data, to meet some false-positive budget; Dice can be averaged over subjects, taken as a median, or pooled over voxels; a lesion must be de-fined before it can be counted as found; and the models were trained on particular healthy data with particular budgets. Each decision is defensible on its own. Together they span a large space of analysis paths, and it is well documented outside imaging that a reasonable change of path can change the conclusion [60,58,59]. In biomedical image analysis, rankings of challenge entries are known to be fragile to the metric and the aggregation [45,56], and choices made with sight of the test data bias the es-timate of performance [62,20]. UAD adds failure modes of its own. Thresholds are often tuned on the test cohort; slice-level detection scores reward any map that tracks how much brain a slice contains; and simply thresholding FLAIR intensity has been reported to give better Dice than several learnt models [48]. We asked two questions, and framed each component of the answer as a hypothesis to be tested. How much of a UAD leaderboard is decided by the evaluation rather than by the model? And can a protocol make that depen-dence visible, catch the errors it hides, and still say some-thing definite about the models? 1.1 Related work 

UAD methods for brain MRI. Reconstruction-based methods train a generative model on healthy scans and read anomalies from the residual between an input and its pseudo-healthy reconstruction. Autoencoders and their variants were surveyed by Baur et al. [9]. In our ear-lier work, StRegA, we combined a compact context-encoding VAE with pre- and post-processing steps cho-sen for clinical data, and reported a Dice of 0.642 on BraTS T2 tumours against 0.522 for the best base-line [21]. Denoising diffusion probabilistic models [33] im-proved reconstruction fidelity but tend either to copy the anomaly or to hallucinate healthy tissue that differs from the subject. Patched diffusion [10], automatic mask-ing and re-sampling (AutoDDPM) [14], implicit guidance (THOR) [15], masked diffusion (MAD-AD) [13], cold-diffusion restorations (DISYRE) [51] and aggregated nor-mative diffusion (ANDi) [29] each address this dilemma differently. Kascenas et al. showed that the spatial scale and magnitude of the training noise largely determine how well denoising autoencoders and diffusion models de-tect anomalies, and that a denoising autoencoder trained with coarse noise is competitive with far more complex models [37]. Two lines condition the denoiser on the in-put itself: cDDPM conditions on a learnt encoding of the image so that the reconstruction keeps the subject’s inten-sity characteristics [11], and UCAD-CDM (here UCCD) conditions on a contrastive encoder trained to be invari-ant to the anomaly factor [54]. REFLECT replaces it-erative sampling by a rectified flow [44] in the latent space of a VAE, transporting an abnormal latent to the healthy manifold in essentially one Euler step [12]. Feature-based methods compare deep features with those of healthy references: AnomalyDINO uses frozen DI-NOv2 [53] patch features and nearest-neighbour distances to a memory bank [24]; Dinomaly and Dinomaly2 re-construct foundation-model features [31,30]; SCRD4AD uses scale-aware contrastive reverse distillation [41]. Iter-Mask3D refines masks iteratively in 3D at test time [43]. We study REFLECT, UCCD, cDDPM and Anomaly-DINO, which cover one-step transport, two forms of con-ditioned diffusion and a training-free foundation-model memory bank. 

How UAD is evaluated. Comparative studies have repeat-edly found evaluation to be the weak link. Meissen et al. showed that thresholding FLAIR scans gives better Dice and precision-recall curves than several learnt anomaly detectors on tumour and multiple-sclerosis data [48]. La-MIRTO 3

gogiannis et al. re-ran a diverse set of methods in a common framework on several datasets and found that feature-modelling methods, many from industrial inspec-tion, set the state of the art [40]. Two broad benchmarks unify methods across medical domains: BMAD assem-bles six datasets from five domains, amongst them brain MRI, and fifteen algorithms [6], and MedIAnomaly com-pares thirty methods on seven datasets of five modali-ties, for both image-level classification and pixel-level seg-mentation [18]. Bercea et al. argued that the quality of the normative reconstruction, which the usual metrics ig-nore, governs downstream behaviour [16]. A recent multi-centre benchmark selected thresholds on separate valida-tion scans, reported Dice from 0.03 to 0.65 across algo-rithms and found biases with scanner, lesion size, age and sex [28]. Myles et al. recast UAD as a Bayesian inverse problem and derived several existing scores as instances of one energy [50]. These works compare methods under a fixed protocol, or improve the scoring rule; none quanti-fies how much of the resulting ranking the protocol itself determines, nor checks the geometric correctness of the comparison. 

Validation methodology. Maier-Hein et al. showed that challenge rankings change with the metric and the ag-gregation [45], and the Metrics Reloaded consortium catalogued metric pitfalls and recommendations [46,56], including the dependence of overlap metrics on struc-ture size [61]. Multiverse analysis [60] and specification curves [59] evaluate a conclusion over all defensible anal-ysis paths; the many-analysts design shows how widely reasonable paths diverge [58]. Selection on the evaluation data inflates estimates [62,20]. Across medical imaging, Varoquaux and Cheplygina traced how biases enter at every step from data to evaluation [63]; Christodoulou et al. found that more than half of the MICCAI 2023 seg-mentation papers did not assess performance variability and that only one reported confidence intervals [23]; and a Bayesian analysis of published comparisons estimated a probability of a false outperformance claim above 5% for 86% of classification and 53% of segmentation papers [22]. In machine learning generally, data sampling, initialisa-tion and hyperparameter choice add enough variance to change benchmark conclusions [17]. MIRTO brings these ideas to UAD and adds what segmentation by threshold-ing needs: an explicit operating point. 

Distribution-free control of operating points. Conformal risk control (CRC) chooses a threshold on calibration data so that the expected value of a bounded, monotone loss on a new exchangeable case is at most α [3]; risk-controlling prediction sets (RCPS) [8] and Learn-then-Test [2] give control with high probability over the calibration set in-stead; see [1] for an introduction. We use the loss “this scan exceeds its false-positive budget”. For such an in-dicator loss CRC reduces to split-conformal calibration, whose coverage conditional on the calibration set follows a known Beta law [64,1]; we use that law to state how often a small calibration set misses its target. Covariate-adjusted ROC analysis [36] provides the tool we use to separate anatomy from detection in slice-level scores. 

1.2 Contributions 

Our contribution is an evaluation protocol, the exact ac-counting that makes what it reports interpretable, and one method-level improvement evaluated with it; the ex-periments validate the protocol and apply it. 1. Registration gate and label-free diagnostics. 

A gate that accepts correct registrations and rejects flips, transposes and two-slice offsets, and five dis-cordance diagnostics computable without extra la-bels, two of which detect injected in-plane errors with power 1.000 at a false-alarm rate of 0.05 in 30 subjects (Section 2.3). 2. Operating points that account for threshold transfer. Validation-only thresholds, realised rather than nominal burden, a threshold-transfer gap with an exact decomposition of any contrast into a map term and a transfer term (Identity 1), and a conformal per-scan burden guarantee, for which we apply the known training-conditional law of split-conformal cal-ibration to state, from the calibration size alone, how often a calibration set misses its target (Lemma 1; Section 2.4). 3. Exact accounting of post-processing and met-rics. Elementary identities, applied to UAD, that split a post-processing gain at equal burden into an effect at fixed threshold and a threshold relaxation, state exactly when deleting a component raises Dice (Identities 4 and 5), and decompose pooled Dice and pooled AUROC so as to explain when subject-level and pooled rankings disagree (Identities 2 and 3). Their value lies in what they let a reader attribute, not in their difficulty. 4. An evaluation multiverse. Ten axes of evaluation choices, flip shares read together with the size of each difference, per-metric variance shares with bootstrap intervals, and the training data as an eleventh axis (Section 2.7). 5. Latent aggregation for REFLECT. A training-free change to how REFLECT combines its latent channels, which raises Dice at equal burden in two independently trained models (Sections 2.9 and 3.10). We apply the protocol to four UAD methods and two model-free baselines. We do not claim that any method is best, nor that the inference is confirmatory: the 312 test subjects served both to develop the protocol and to test its hypotheses, so every test is exploratory (Section 2.8). 4 N. With an indicator loss, CRC se-lects an order statistic of the calibration scores exactly as split-conformal prediction does, and 1 − ρ is then the coverage conditional on the calibration set, whose Beta law is the classical training-conditional result of the con-formal literature [64,1]. We state it because, applied to the per-scan burden, it turns a marginal guarantee into a prediction that a user can make before seeing any test data. For n = 24 and α = 0 .10 , j = 1 and ρ ∼ Beta(2 , 23) ,whose mean is 0.080 , whose 97.5th percentile is 0.211 , and which puts only 70 .8% of its mass at or below α. CRC therefore controls the average over calibration sets whilst leaving roughly three calibration sets in ten above the target; a guarantee that holds with high probability for the calibration set in hand requires RCPS or Learn-then-Test [8,2], at the price of a stricter threshold. We com-pare this prediction with 1,000 random calibration splits in Section 3.3. Figure 3c illustrates the construction.

3.1 Registration and geometry 

Hypothesis. A mapping error that permutes voxels within slices should push per-subject voxel AUROC below 0.5 and scramble its ranks across subjects, whilst leaving slice-level scores almost unchanged, because a slice that contains tumour still does so after any in-plane permu-tation. If so, label-free diagnostics can detect such er-rors without extra labels. H7 (diagnostic power): rank stability and the anti-location rate each detect in-plane flips and transposes with power of at least 0.95 at a false-alarm rate of 0.05 in 60 subjects. A secondary hy-pothesis predicts that UCCD’s voxel AUROC falls un-der antero-posterior and left-right flips whereas Anomaly-DINO’s slice-level AUROC does not fall under an antero-posterior flip. 

A mapping error that standard metrics miss. Each method stores its maps in its own axis order, crop and resolution, so any comparison of methods with differ-ent preprocessing must undo these steps, and an er-ror in doing so is easy to make and hard to see. Such errors arose in practice in assembling this bench-mark. A mapping that read the stored cDDPM maps in the wrong axis order and ignored their field-of-view crop gave a voxel AUROC of 0.583; with the correct mapping the same maps gave 0.873, a change of +0 .290 [0 .277 , 0.302] , with +0 .535 [0 .517 , 0.553] in MIRTO 11 

AUPRC and +0 .520 [0 .504 , 0.537] in Dice at the valida-tion threshold at 20 cm 3 (Appendix D.1, Table 14). Slice-max AUROC moved by only +0 .112 [0 .099 , 0.125] (me-dian +0 .083 ). This is the pattern the hypothesis predicts: a slice-level score is close to blind to the error that matters most for segmentation. The error is of the kind the reg-istration gate is designed to reject (it fails in-plane flips and transposes on synthetic volumes; Section 2.3), and the label-free diagnostics flag it directly: under the wrong mapping 24% of cDDPM test subjects were anti-located (per-subject voxel AUROC < 0.5). Two further instances of the same risk occurred with REFLECT. A second ex-port of its maps with the same kind of axis error left 26% of test subjects anti-located and lowered voxel AU-ROC from 0.936 to 0.616 whilst slice AUROC stayed at 0.915 (with the correct mapping, 2 of 312 subjects were anti-located); and resampling REFLECT maps by sim-ple zooming instead of with the mapped transform gave the published map a Dice of 0.089 instead of 0.548, which invalidated a comparison of score rules made with that resampling. Each error produced a plausible table. That is the case for gating geometry rather than assuming it. 

The registration gate on correct pipelines. The gate passed 1,342 of 1,344 method–subject pairs (312 test and 24 validation subjects for each of four methods; Fig. 4a). Every method’s applied transform was the cohort-wide overlap maximiser amongst the 56 candidates (criterion ii-a). The one subject that failed, for UCCD and cD-DPM, which share a reference, failed only the overlap floor (iii) with D = 0 .845 ; its centroid error was within 3 mm and no alternative did better, so it is a label-fidelity exception, not a misregistration. Excluding it changed the interval-excludes-zero status of 5 of 588 contrasts, each with a bound within 0.0013 of zero, and moved no esti-mate by more than 0.008. A fixed overlap threshold of 0.95 would have failed 61 (REFLECT), 63 (AnomalyDINO) and 242 (UCCD, cDDPM) correctly registered subjects (Appendix Table 15): their own references had been re-sampled inside the method’s preprocessing (median la-bel fidelity 0.966) or their tumour extended beyond the 100-slice field of view of UCCD and cDDPM (45 of 336 subjects). Across all twenty method–training-set combi-nations the same subject was the only failure. 

Diagnostics where the truth is known. We injected con-trolled corruptions into the two pipelines whose geometry had been verified independently (Fig. 4b; Appendix Ta-ble 16). In-plane flips and the transpose cost UCCD 0.34 to 0.50 Dice and 0.20 to 0.26 voxel AUROC, yet changed its slice-max AUROC by at most +0 .012 . A corrupted map can even improve a standard metric: an antero-posterior flip raised AnomalyDINO’s slice-max AUROC by +0 .181 and the transpose by +0 .099 . Rank stability and the anti-location rate detected every in-plane flip and transpose with power 1.000 at a false-alarm rate of 0.05 already at n = 30 , which supports H7 (Table 17); the sec-ondary hypothesis on flips held as well. They behave dif-ferently elsewhere: the anti-location rate stays near zero under through-plane shifts and reference dilation, so it is specific to localisation errors, whereas rank stability re-acts to almost any perturbation of the per-subject rank-ing (power 1.000 for a two-slice shift that moved AUROC by only +0 .003 ) and is better read as a change detector. Permuting 5% of subject identifiers was not detectable at 

n ≤ 312 , and the Jaccard bound was never violated. Fur-ther robustness checks (excluding the failing subject, re-stricting to tumours inside every field of view, a through-plane offset check, spatial randomisation and a geometry factorial) are reported in Appendix D.1.

3.7 Lesion-level detection 

Hypotheses. If no sub-floor fragment is ever hit, the lesion floor changes pooled sensitivity only through its denom-inator, by the ratio |L f |/|L 0| (Appendix A). H4 (con-trast dependence): detection rises with T2 contrast and with oedema fraction in every residual model. Secondary hypotheses predict that UCCD finds fewer than 10% of small lesions and that the three residual models miss largely the same lesions (Jaccard index of the missed sets of UCCD with REFLECT and with cDDPM at least 0.70). The lesion definition rescaled pooled sensitivity about elevenfold through the denominator alone. REFLECT hit 287 lesions under both definitions; 287 /472 = 0 .608 with the 50 mm 3 floor and 287 /5,473 = 0 .052 without, a ratio of 5,473 /472 = 11 .6 that is identical for every method because the 5,001 sub-floor fragments were never hit. Subject-mean sensitivity moved less (0.765 against 0.173). A sensitivity computed over all components is not a sen-sitivity in any clinical sense, and a lesion-level metric is uninterpretable without its connectivity and floor. At the validation threshold the residual models found nearly every large lesion and almost no small one: large-lesion sensitivity was 0.915 to 0.997 and small-lesion sen-sitivity 0.036 to 0.073, at most 12 of 165 small lesions, as the secondary hypothesis predicted. The three resid-ual models missed largely the same lesions (Jaccard in-dex of the missed sets 0.805 to 0.938), which supports the second secondary hypothesis. Most misses carried ev-idence that the threshold did not select: of the 472 above-floor lesions, 123 to 137 were missed with in-lesion scores above the subject’s healthy-brain median, 9 to 17 had no such evidence, and 12 to 35 were selected but delineated with IoU < 0.10 (Fig. 11a). Lesion volume dominated de-tection in every model (Firth-penalised log-odds 4.8 to 6.0 per standard deviation of log volume for the resid-ual models; Fig. 11b), whereas in the Firth fit T2 con-trast added an effect whose interval excluded zero only for REFLECT ( +1 .98 [1 .22 , 2.82] ) and AnomalyDINO (+0 .29 [0 .03 , 0.55] ); for cDDPM only the GEE interval excluded zero (Appendix Table 47). H4 was therefore not supported; it could be tested only in part, because oedema fractions are not available in the binary labels. Label-informed upper bounds that respect the per-subject bud-get (Appendix D.7, Table 45) place the remaining head-room mainly in delineation: perfect boundaries for de-tected lesions would add 0.40 to 0.46 Dice at 20 cm 3, an optimal threshold per lesion 0.12 to 0.16, and an optimal threshold per subject 0.03 to 0.07.

The registration gate and the label-free diag-nostics catch such errors with measured power and leave correct pipelines alone; the obvious alternative, a Dice threshold on the method’s own reference, would have re-jected up to 242 correctly registered subjects per method. The threshold-transfer component turns a familiar complaint, that thresholds are tuned on the test set, into something measurable. Identity 1 shows that the gap be-tween test-tuned and validation-tuned Dice is not a fixed optimism to be subtracted but a product of a burden mismatch and a local slope, which differs from method to method. A comparison at validation thresholds reflects what a user would obtain but confounds map quality with threshold transfer; a comparison at equal burden isolates map quality but cannot be reproduced in prac-tice. MIRTO reports both, and the identity says exactly how they relate. Conformal risk control adds a per-scan guarantee, which is closer to what matters for an indi-vidual patient than a cohort mean. The known training-conditional law of Lemma 1 adds the caveat that a 24-subject calibration set honours the guarantee only on av-erage, with a spread that follows from the calibration size alone, and the empirical splits followed that law closely (shares within 2.8 percentage points, 97.5th percentiles within 0.015). A user who needs the guarantee for the calibration set in hand should use RCPS or Learn-then-Test [8,2] and accept a stricter threshold. The multiverse quantifies what the previous compo-nents illustrate, and it is most informative metric by met-ric. Pooled over metrics, the evaluation axes carry most of the variance, but mainly because metrics have differ-ent scales, and we do not lean on that share. Within a metric the picture is sharper. Rankings by voxel AUROC are a property of the method. Dice is dominated by the method in variance, chiefly because one method is far be-hind, yet the order of the three residual models is decided by the evaluation, with leads of several Dice points in each direction and significant contrasts on both sides. Lesion sensitivity is mostly a property of the evaluation. Read-ing flip shares together with the size of each difference is what separates these cases from mere closeness: UCCD and cDDPM swap places often because they are close, and their significant difference at validation thresholds is a matter of threshold transfer. Lesion-level claims deserve the least confidence unless the lesion definition, the hit criterion and the floor are stated, and our denominator identity shows that the floor alone can change sensitivity elevenfold without a single hit changing. 

Recommendations. For authors of UAD methods we sug-gest a short list, which the checklist released with the code expands. Score every method against one reference on one grid, and report a registration check. Choose thresholds on data that are not the test set, and report the realised false-positive burden as a distribution (median and 95th percentile, not only the mean). Report subject mean, me-dian and pooled Dice side by side, and when two meth-ods swap places across evaluation choices, report by how much each leads. State the lesion definition in full. Do not report marginal slice AUROC without an anatomy con-trol. Report paired intervals with a declared multiplicity correction. And train competing methods on the same healthy data, or say plainly that they were not. 

Relation to other benchmarks. Recent benchmarks have improved UAD evaluation by enlarging and diversifying the test data [28], by examining the normative recon-struction [16], or by unifying many methods and datasets within one framework [40,6,18]. MIRTO is complemen-tary. It does not propose a new dataset; it asks how much of any benchmark’s conclusion survives a change of de-fensible evaluation choices, and it provides the identities needed to say why a conclusion changes when it does. The same machinery could be wrapped around any of those benchmarks. It also answers, for UAD, the concerns raised for medical imaging AI in general: that performance vari-ability and confidence intervals are rarely reported [23], MIRTO 21 

that many outperformance claims are probably false [22], and that biases enter at every step of evaluation [63]. MIRTO adds a source of uncertainty that intervals over subjects do not capture, the choice of evaluation pipeline, and shows how large it is. 

Limitations. The evidence comes from a single cohort and a single contrast. BraTS 2020 whole-tumour labels are large, conspicuous lesions, and evaluation on T2 alone limits what can be said about small or subtle pathology; the small-lesion results here are a floor, not a characteri-sation. The 312 test subjects served both to develop the protocol and the choice of training set and to test the hypotheses, so all inference is exploratory; explicit deci-sion criteria keep a hypothesis from being bent to fit its outcome, but they cannot remove the influence of other analyses of the same cohort. One trained model exists per method and training set, so training-seed variance, which can be large enough to change benchmark con-clusions [17] and is the most likely competing explana-tion for small between-method differences, is unknown. A common training set means the same source data, not the same optimisation budget, and IXI-HH+Guys is not the best training set for every method: on test it cost RE-FLECT 0.055 and UCCD 0.037 Dice relative to their best training sets (Appendix Table 60), so the comparison of methods should not be read as each method’s best attain-able result. The injection study validated the diagnostics on two pipelines; their power on other architectures is assumed rather than shown. Validation-threshold inter-vals do not include the uncertainty of fitting a threshold on 24 subjects, which a nested resampling would add. The covariate-adjusted slice estimators failed their own control, so the borrowed-map contrast, not the adjusted AUROC, is the defensible slice-level measure. Finally, the training-free improvement to REFLECT is shown for one method and two trained models, and the specific winning aggregation did not replicate. 

Future work. The next step is a pre-registered confirma-tory evaluation on an untouched cohort, with the training set and every threshold rule fixed by its protocol. Nested resampling of the validation threshold, several training seeds per method and a second pathology with smaller lesions would close the main gaps listed above. 

5 Conclusion 

A UAD leaderboard is a statement about models and evaluation jointly. In our study the method decided rank-ings by voxel AUROC, whereas the evaluation decided the order of the three strongest models in Dice and in lesion sensitivity. MIRTO separates the two. It gates the geom-etry of the comparison with tests of known power, holds models to operating points chosen without the test set and reports what those operating points actually deliver, evaluates every conclusion over a multiverse of defensi-ble pipelines, reading how often an order flips together with how large each difference is, and attaches paired, multiplicity-adjusted intervals to each claim. Elementary identities explain why a contrast changes between valida-tion thresholds and matched burden, why post-processing appears to help and why pooled and subject-level rank-ings disagree, and a known conformal result states how often calibration on a small set will miss its target. Ap-plied to four current UAD families, the protocol exposed a geometric error that standard metrics missed, showed which apparent differences were artefacts of threshold transfer, and still supported some definite statements for this cohort and training set: REFLECT had the highest voxel AUROC, the two conditioned diffusion models gave higher Dice at small budgets, no method found more than 12 of the 165 small lesions, and a training-free change to REFLECT’s latent aggregation raised its Dice at matched burden in two checkpoints. Reporting evaluation choices with the same care as architectures would make UAD comparisons much easier to interpret.

Within IXI-HH+Guys the methods kept their own training protocols: REFLECT trained on all 504 IXI T2 volumes of the pool (stage 1, 200 epochs; re-flow, 50 epochs); UCCD on 324 training and 81 validation volumes (encoder and diffusion model taken at their best validation loss); cDDPM on 361 training and 92 validation volumes (best validation loss at epoch 1,426 of 1,600, 2,854 optimisation steps); AnomalyDINO built its memory bank from 50 reference volumes with slice stride 2. Deviations and disclosures. (1) The stated description of the training data names all three IXI sites; our IXI T2 data contain no subjects from the third site (IOP), so IXI-HH+Guys comprises the Hammersmith and Guy’s sites, identically for all methods. (2) The canonical arrays are a through-plane-cropped copy (98–149 slices) of the 240 ×

240 × 155 originals; no tumour voxel is affected. (3) The contrast-dependence hypothesis could be tested only in part, because the available labels are binary. (4) The pruning trade-off was judged from point estimates, as no interval was computed for it.
