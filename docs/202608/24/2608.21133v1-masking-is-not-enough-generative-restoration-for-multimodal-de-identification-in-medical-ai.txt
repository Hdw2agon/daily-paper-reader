## Abstract 

Medical image-text data can expose protected health information (PHI) through both visible image content as well as accompanying text, creating a barrier to privacy-preserving medical AI systems. This risk is especially prominent in multi-modal systems, where images, questions, reports, and clinical context may enter training, evaluation, or inference pipelines. Existing medical vision-language benchmarks primarily emphasize task utility, while de-identification methods are often evaluated separately from downstream reasoning. We introduce ClinX, an end-to-end multimodal PHI sanitization framework for medical image-text data. ClinX detects visible identifiers with optical character recognition (OCR), constructs binary PHI masks, and applies ClinX-PRISM, a no-skip generative restoration module with privacy-oriented post-processing for burned-in identifier suppression. In parallel, text-side PHI is reduced through progressive de-identification levels: regex masking, context-aware masking, and rewrite-based sanitization. We eval-uate ClinX in medical visual question answering (MedVQA), jointly measuring PHI leakage and downstream utility across image-side, text-side, and combined de-identification settings. Results show that OCR-only masking is not sufficient as a standalone solution, and restoration-based sanitization better preserves clinically relevant visual context while sharply reducing recoverable PHI.

## 1 Introduction 

Multimodal medical AI systems are increasingly being integrated into real-world clinical workflows, including diagnostics assistance, clinical documentations, patient triage, and as decision-supporting tools [Poon et al., 2025]. A central challenge in deploying these medical AI systems is that they often depend on sensitive patient data containing the protected health information (PHI), such as patient names, dates of birth, medical record numbers, hospital identifiers, and other details that can reveal an individual’s identity [Poon et al., 2025, Faustini et al., 2026]. The risk is especially more prominent when hospitals export the medical imaging data for research or AI model development. Even after the Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) metadata has been removed, the image pixels themselves may still contain burned-in annotations, including patient name, date of birth, medical record number, scan dates, and hospital identifiers rendered into the images directly [Macdonald et al., 2024]. Similar leakage can also appear in scanned reports, free-text clinical notes, structured clinical queries, and image overlays. As a result, data that appears at the file or metadata level may still expose the patient’s identity through visible text or associated language. This is not just a technical limitation, but also a regulatory concern. Under the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) de-identification guidance, identifiers such as names, dates, and medical record numbers must be removed or properly managed before the data can be used for the research, or secondary analysis [U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2012, Electronic Code of Federal Regulations, 2000]. Meeting this standard requires a systematic framework for identifying information across the full multimodal data pipeline. Recent progress in medical AI has increasingly moved beyond the single-modality prediction and diagnosis towards the multimodal visual-language systems that connect the medical images with the questions, instructions, report understanding, and clinical contexts [Chen et al., 2024]. Medical vision-language models (VLMs) and multimodal medical large language models (LLMs) have shown growing promise for tasks such as image-grounded reasoning, report understanding, instruction following, and clinical question answering [Li et al., 2023, Moor et al., 2023a, Jiang et al., 2025, Sellergren et al., 2026, Huang et al., 2025]. Within this broader direction, medical visual question answering (MedVQA) has become a focused benchmark for the evaluation of the model’s ability to answer the natural-language questions grounded in medical imagery. Datasets such as VQA-RAD, PathVQA, and SLAKE have supported evaluation across the radiology, pathology, and knowledge-enhanced medical image understanding [Lau et al., 2018, He et al., 2021, Liu et al., 2021]. However, most of this work has emphasized reasoning accuracy, answer correctness, and model capability, while privacy risk in the paired image-text input has received much less systematic research attention. In parallel, PHI protection has been studied through separate de-identification efforts, including DICOM metadata removal, OCR-based detection of burned-in text [Macdonald et al., 2024, Rempe et al., 2025], as well as de-identification of free-text clinical notes [Neamatullah et al., 2008, Stubbs and Uzuner, 2015]. These methods are essential to ensure the PHI does not leak in any way, shape, or form, but they are particularly evaluated as individual pre-processing or privacy-preserving steps rather than as a part of a downstream medical vision-language reasoning pipeline. As a result, it remains unclear how PHI removal from the image side and the question side jointly affects both privacy protection and model utility. This gap is central to MedVQA, where the image and the question may each contain clinically useful information while also serving as potential challenges for PHI leakage. To address this gap, we introduce ClinX, an end-to-end multimodal PHI sanitization framework for the privacy preserving medical AI. ClinX is designed to operate before the medical image-text data are used for the model training, evaluation, or downstream inference, reducing the risk that patient identifiers are exposed to the learning and inference pipeline. Given PHI-contaminated multimodal inputs, ClinX produces sanitized image-text pairs through two coordinated branches. The image-side branch detects the visible identifiers with the optical character recognition (OCR), matches candidate text against PHI patterns, constructs the binary PHI mask, and then applies the ClinX-PRISM, a generative restoration and identifier suppression module that removes the visible PHI while preserving the surrounding clinical image structure. The text-side branch applies progressively stronger de-identification, moving from the regex-based masking, to context-aware masking, and finally a generative rewrite to sanitize the text from any PHI, and ensure the structure of the text is intact. Together, these two branches prevent the PHI-bearing visual and textual context from being passed directly into the multimodal medical AI models while preserving the clinical signal needed for learning and reasoning. In this paper, we instantiate and evaluate ClinX in the MedVQA setting, 2where sanitized image-question pairs are tested for both privacy protection and downstream answer utility. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows: • We introduce ClinX, an end-to-end multimodal PHI sanitization framework for medical image-text data before training, evaluation, or inference. • ClinX-PRISM extends image-side de-identification beyond OCR-only masking through mask-conditioned no-skip generative restoration with privacy-oriented post-processing for burned-in identifier suppression. • PHI leakage and answer utility are jointly evaluated in MedVQA across image-side, text-side, and combined multimodal de-identification settings. We have organized the remainder of this paper as follows. Section 2 reviews related work. Section 3 presents ClinX framework. Section 4 describes the experimental setup and reports the results from the experiment. Section 6 concludes the paper. 

## 2 Background and Related Work 

Medical AI has increasingly shifted from the single-modality prediction towards multimodal vision-language systems that connect clinical images with questions, reports, instructions, and broader clinical contexts [Moor et al., 2023b, Hartsock and Rasool, 2024, Chen et al., 2024]. Recent medical vision-language models (VLMs) have demonstrated strong performance on biomedical image conversation, few-shot medical VQA, radiology reasoning, and instruction following over medical images [Li et al., 2023, Moor et al., 2023a, Sellergren et al., 2026, Jiang et al., 2025, Huang et al., 2025]. As image-text reasoning becomes central to medical AI, it also increases the number of channels through which protected health information can enter training, evaluation, or inference pipelines [Chen et al., 2025]. Compared with just the task utility and model downstream performance, this privacy risk has received far less systematic attention. 

Medical visual question answering (MedVQA) offers a focused setting for studying image-text reasoning in clinical contexts. Datasets such as VQA-RAD [Lau et al., 2018], PathVQA [He et al., 2021], SLAKE [Liu et al., 2021], and PMC-VQA [Zhang et al., 2024] have supported evaluation across radiology, pathology, and knowledge-enhanced medical image understanding. These datasets are primarily utility-centered, measuring answer accuracy, reasoning ability, and closed-ended question performance. They are also typically released in the pre-de-identified form and do not include the PHI annotations or controlled PHI-bearing image-text pairs, making them unsuitable for the systematic evaluation of the pipeline to detect, remove or preserve the utility after the PHI sanitization. As a result, there is no standard MedVQA benchmark for studying privacy leakage and answer utility together under the realistic multimodal PHI contamination. 

Clinical de-identification has addressed PHI protection through a largely parallel line of work. Under the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) Privacy Rule, de-identified health information must not identify an individual or provide a reasonable basis for doing so, with Safe Harbor and Expert Determination serving as the main regulatory pathways [Electronic Code of Federal Regulations, 2000, U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2012]. In medical imaging, de-identification has commonly focused on removal of Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) metadata, using optical character recognition (OCR) to detect the burned-in text, and mask visible identifiers, which could be the candidate for the PHI, before masking or redaction [Macdonald et al., 2024, Rutherford et al., 2021]. Similarly, in clinical text approaches, de-identification has been studied through rule-based system, named-entity recognition, sequence labeling, and neural rewriting to suppress patient-identifying information from medical text records [Neamatullah et al., 2008, Stubbs and Uzuner, 2015]. These methods are well established within their respective domains, but they are usually evaluated as standalone preprocessing steps. This separation is limiting for the multimodal medical AI, where an image may be sanitized while the paired text still contains the PHI, or the text may be sanitized while the image still exposes the burned-in identifiers. 

Masking mechanism is one of practical ways for suppressing visible PHI, but it is not always sufficient for downstream image-text reasoning. OCR-based blackout masking can remove the surface-level identifiers, yet it may also disrupt the nearby visual structures that may be useful for the medical diagnosis and interpretation [Macdonald et al., 2024, Rutherford et al., 2021]. Foundational and widely used approaches include generative adversarial networks, context encoders, partial convolutions, DeepFill, and LaMa [Yu et al., 2018, 2019, Liu et al., 2018, Suvorov et al., 2022, Pathak et al., 2016]. ## 3 ClinX 

In this section, we introduce ClinX, our multimodal PHI sanitization pipeline for medical VQA. Specifically, our approach is designed to deal with instances where both images and questions may potentially contain PHI before being fed into the downstream model. In this section, we first introduce the threat model as well as the specific controlled environment of PHI-augmented image-question pairs. Next, we provide details on the image side of our pipeline, which leverages OCR localization and the use of our no-skip generative restoration module called ClinX-PRISM. Lastly, we explain our question de-identification procedure as well as our evaluation protocol. 

Threat model We evaluate the potential for data misuse within a downstream data-use setting where medical image-question pairs containing PHI could become exposed to the model developers, VLM inference API, benchmark evaluators, or automatic extraction engines prior to de-identification. The adversary is not presumed to have access to the synthetic manifest utilized in our evaluation process, yet could exploit optical character recognition (OCR), string matching, or vision-language inference to extract any visible or textual identifiers from the published inputs. Any synthetic manifest identifier that could potentially be extracted from the sanitized medical image or question through the respective leakage evaluator is considered to be recovered. This threat model does not claim cryptographic privacy, rather it just targets practical PHI exposure through burned-in image text and question-side identifiers before downstream MedVQA inference. 

3.1 ClinX Overview 

Our framework, ClinX, is designed for medical VQA settings in which protected health information (PHI) can appear in both the image and the accompanying questions. Given an image-question pair that is contaminated with PHI, ClinX generates a sanitized image and a sanitized question, which can then be fed into the downstream medical VLM. There are two goals we aim to achieve with our design: suppress recoverable PHI and preserve the clinical information essential for the correct answer generation. Let us consider D = {(xi, q i, a i)}Ni=1 be a medical VQA dataset, where xi is the image, qi is the question, and ai is the ground-truth answer. ClinX-PRISM: Privacy-Oriented Generative Restoration. Direct OCR redaction removes visible identifiers using the masking, thus preserving the privacy itself, but it also leaves a hard occlusion in the image. This is undesirable, particularly for medical images. It is so because the region surrounding a burned-in overlay may still carry useful visual context for downstream reasoning and diagnosis. We therefore introduce ClinX-PRISM, short for Privacy-preserving Restoration via Inpainting and Spatial Masking , a privacy-oriented generative restoration module that replaces the localized PHI region while preserving the rest of the medical image unchanged. In ClinX, we therefore treat generative restoration not simply as image reconstruction, but as a privacy-oriented 

sanitization process that removes recoverable identifiers while preserving clinically relevant image 5context. During the inference of the image-question pair, ClinX-PRISM receives only the PHI-overlaid image ˜xi and its PHI mask mi. The clean image xi is used only during paired restoration training, where it provides supervision before synthetic PHI overlay. It is never available to the sanitizer at evaluation time and is not included as a main image condition itself, thus preventing any change of leakage of PHI. We designed the ClinX-PRISM around a simple but important hypothesis, which is that the high-fidelity inpainting and privacy-preserving inpainting should not necessarily use the same information-flow pattern. In standard restoration, skip connections are often useful because they transfer high-resolution encoder features directly into the decoder, which we denote using g-skip notation. In PHI sanitization, however, the same pathway can be risky because encoder features are computed from the PHI-contaminated input; thus, the direct feature reuse may carry identifier-correlated strokes, edges, or local texture fragments into the restored region itself. We therefore treat skip removal as a privacy-oriented architectural constraint rather than only a reconstruction choice. This hypothesis is evaluated through the g-skip versus ClinX-PRISM comparison, where both models share the same training objective and differ primarily in generator information flow along with post-processing. Optimization and Image Conditions. In ClinX, We use an adversarial inpainting backbone as our evaluation requires the deterministic, mask-conditioned restoration over a large Cartesian grid of image and text privacy settings. Inpainting with a prompt conditioned diffusion model is another plausible choice since both approaches have been proven to be successful for the purpose of re-construction, but it introduces additional prompt design, stochastic sampling, and reproducibility concerns. 3.3 Progressive Query De-Identification Pipeline 

In the case of question text data, ClinX performs text-level de-identification using increasingly powerful de-identification steps, including pattern-based and context-aware masking, as well as rewrites. Finally, both the sanitized images and texts are combined and validated through the downstream MedVQA. In ClinX framework, we use the question-side branch to sanitize the PHI through four privacy levels, Ltext = {L0, L 1, L 2, L 3}. At L0, the PHI-augmented question is retained, which is used to isolates the effect of image-side sanitization. At L1, explicit surface-form identifiers are masked with regex-based rules, including names, dates, record numbers, phone numbers, email addresses, and hospital identifiers. Following L1 sanitization, at L2, context-aware masking is applied to suppress residual PHI that may appear near anchors such as “Patient,” “Name,” “DOB,” “MRN,” “ID,” or “Hospital.” This stage expands sanitization from direct string matching to context-sensitive PHI removal. Finally, for L3, we generate rewrite-based de-identification manifests using an offline large language model (LLM). We instruct the model to remove all of the remaining PHI like names, dates, record numbers, hospital identifiers, locations, and other identity-bearing phrases if any, while preserving the medical question being asked. In practice, we preserve the clinical finding, anatomy, imaging modality, disease cue, and question intent whenever they are present, and remove demographic or institutional details that are not needed to answer the VQA itself. 3.4 Joint Multimodal Evaluation Design 

In ClinX, both image-side and question-side sanitization are tested simultaneously. For every image-question pair in ClinX, the Cartesian product Cimg × Ltext is used, which connects every image condition to every text privacy level. This approach enables us to study the effect of image sanitization in isolation from combined multimodal sanitization, since all L1≤x≤3 de-identify the question and only L0 preserves PHI-augmented questions to isolate image-side sanitization. Since the manifest 

zi governs both the image as well as the question modality, this is not merely a post hoc grid of pre-processing choices. It measures how image restoration and question de-identification interact under a shared privacy process. A separate text-only ablation study is also performed where the image is left unchanged, but only the question is manipulated across different privacy levels. This way, we can better understand the effect of question-side sanitization independent of image restoration, and help us better interpret the privacy-utility tradeoff in MedVQA. ## 4 Experiment and Evaluation 

In this section, we present the experimental setup and evaluation results of ClinX. We first describe the datasets, privacy settings, medical VLMs, training details, and evaluation metrics used in our 7experiments. We then report the performance of ClinX, analyze the effectiveness of the proposed text de-identification mechanism, and provide a detailed privacy protection analysis. 

4.1 Experimental Setup Datasets. We evaluated ClinX on PHI-augmented versions of three popular MedicalVQA datasets PathVQA, VQA-RAD, and SLAKE. Augmentation was done by injecting the synthetic PHI into both the image and question modalities using the shared manifest described in Section 3. The test split contained 6,719 PathVQA examples, 451 VQA-RAD examples, and 1,061 SLAKE examples. The training split was used only to train the restoration models, the validation split was used for checkpoint selection, and the test split was reserved for MedVQA and privacy evaluation, to ensure there is no data leakage. 

Privacy Conditions. On the image side, we evaluated four primary conditions: Cimg =

{raw , ocr , g-skip , PRISM }. where, raw is the PHI-overlaid image, ocr is just OCR-guided black-out masking, g-skip is the skip-connected restoration baseline, and PRISM denotes ClinX-PRISM, the proposed no-skip restoration pathway with mask-guided post-processing. For OCR leakage analysis only, we also report PRISM-flat , a no-postprocessing diagnostic export of the same no-skip generator, which is excluded from utility comparisons. On the text side, we evaluated 

Ltext = {L0, L 1, L 2, L 3}, corresponding to the PHI-augmented question, regex masking, context-aware masking, and rewrite-based de-identification. Finally, we evaluated the full Cartesian product 

Cimg × Ltext .

Base Medical VLMs. We evaluated four medical VLMs: LLaVA-Med-7B [Li et al., 2023], MedGemma-1.5-4B [Sellergren et al., 2026], MedVLThinker-3B [Huang et al., 2025], and HuluMed-4B [Jiang et al., 2025]. 

Training Configurations. Both restoration variants were trained with the same PatchGAN-style adversarial objective, using 10 epochs, batch size 6, learning rate 10 −4, input resolution 224 × 224 ,and BF16 mixed precision on 2 × NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs. 

Performance Metrics. Utility was measured using exact match (EM), token-level F1 (F1), yes/no exact match (Yes/no EM), and LLM-as-a-Judge scores with Llama 3.1-8B [Grattafiori et al., 2024] and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 [Jiang et al., 2023]. Privacy leakage was measured using OCR-based PHI token recall and character overlap between injected PHI strings and OCR-recovered text. Imple-mentation details for ClinX-PRISM and the LLM-as-a-Judge prompting and scoring protocol are provided in Appendix C. 

4.2 Overall Privacy–Utility Tradeoff 

In Table 1, we observe an evident privacy-utility tradeoff across the evaluated sanitization settings. First, OCR-guided blackout masking drastically decreased the amount of recoverable PHI compared to the raw baseline conditions but performed comparably to and in some cases just under the performance of the raw baselines when considering EM and F1 scores. This result highlights the inadequacy of directly applying blackout masking to maintain MedVQA utility downstream. On the other hand, the restore conditions allowed us to keep high utility levels while keeping OCR leakage close to zero. When comparing among the three levels of text sanitization L0 − L3, g-skip and 

PRISM were able to outperform both raw and ocr when it comes to lexical metrics, especially under stricter text-side privacy settings. These results support the main premise of ClinX, that effective multimodal PHI sanitization should suppress identifiers while preserving clinically useful image context. Dataset-specific and model-wise results are provided in Appendix A.

4.4 Effect of Progressive Query De-Identification 

Utility stayed relatively constant between L0 and L2, indicating that both regex- and context-based identification removal does not change the clinical nature of the questions itself. Utility is most affected at L3, when de-identification through rewrites makes changes to the question’s structure itself. ocr conditions. This result indicates that, as sanitization becomes more restrictive on the question side, the importance of maintaining clinical information on the image side increases. 

4.5 OCR-Based Privacy Protection 

The results shown in Table 1(b) confirmed that raw images without any protection contained consider-able amounts of PHI that were recoverable via OCR engines, with token recall rates higher than 37%. Both methods of protection drastically decreased token recall rates, demonstrating that both masking and restoration-based sanitization effectively reduced OCR recoverability. Within restoration-based sanitization, the g-skip variant provided the least amount of leakages among all of the options, while the post-processed version of PRISM still yielded slightly worse results. The supplementary analysis of PRISM-flat suggested that this phenomenon was related to the processing phase rather than the no-skip backbone. 

## 5 Discussion 

Our proposed framework, ClinX treats the PHI sanitization in MedVQA as a multimodal privacy-utility problem, rather than simply as image redaction and anonymization. This importance of this distinction becomes evident from the findings, where OCR-blackout masking effectively removes visible identifiers from the image, yet it fails to restore the local visual context that can be useful for reasoning in downstream tasks. In contrast, restoration-based sanitization performed consistently 9better by offering a better balance by reducing OCR-recoverable PHI, while also preserving more of the image evidence needded for the downstream reasoning. Additionally, a comparison of g-skip and 

PRISM highlights the importance of the no-skip architecture. The no-skip pathway is not necessarily a universal improvement in terms of the increase in accuracy when compared to the skip-connected baseline. However, it offers a comparable performance while leveraging a privacy-driven restoration pathway that prevents direct feature reuse from the PHI-contaminated input. Nevertheless, the ClinX framework offers no complete guarantees in terms of privacy, since leakage detection was limited on OCR-based recovery and synthetic PHI. Additional privacy audits and studies involving actual PHI patterns remains to be conducted in the future. Qualitative examples and additional limitations of the privacy evaluation are provided in Appendix D. 

## 6 Conclusion 

In this paper, we presented ClinX, a multimodal PHI sanitization framework for MedVQA that jointly addresses both image-side and text-side identifiers. Across datasets, privacy settings, and evaluated medical vision-language models, restoration-based sanitization substantially reduced the OCR-recoverable protected health information than blackout-masking methods, while consistently retaining higher utility. This paper demonstrates that privacy-preserving MedVQA should be evaluated as a joint multimodal problem, and not merely the redaction of images alone. Future work will extend ClinX toward stronger privacy audits, non-text PHI, and broader clinical multimodal settings.

A.2 Auxiliary PRISM-flat No-Postprocessing Leakage Retest 

We have provided the main results in Table 1(b), which includes an auxiliary no-postprocessing variant of ClinX-PRISM, denoted PRISM-flat . This variant uses the same no-skip restoration backbone, as that of the primary PRISM condition, but doesn’t include the final mask-guided post-processing stage. We introduced this diagnostic control to separate two factors that are coupled in the primary ClinX-PRISM output: the no-skip restoration architecture and then, the final rendered post-processing stage. In the primary comparison, PRISM showed slightly higher residual OCR leakage than g-skip . We do not interpret this result as direct evidence that the no-skip architecture is intrinsically less private. A second explanation is that the post-processing stage can introduce faint boundary, grain, or even compression like artifacts that OCR engines occasionally parse as text-like structure. From the 

PRISM-flat retest, we can probe this distinction by retaining the same no-skip generator while removing just the final post-processing stage at evaluation time. Under both OCR engines, which were EasyOCR and RapidOCR, PRISM-flat produced compari-tively lower residual leakage than the primary PRISM output. This supports our interpretation that the modest OCR disadvantage of the PRISM architecture is more plausibly associated with the final rendered output than with the no-skip restoration backbone alone. On the other hand, PRISM-flat 

is not treated as a primary condition because it was evaluated only for OCR leakage and not for downstream MedVQA utility. Its role was strictly limited to just the diagnostic, as it helps to explain the leakage behavior of the post-processed ClinX-PRISM output without changing the primary evaluation set overall.

A detection was treated as PHI if it matched or partially overlapped with a manifest identifier and satisfied the OCR confidence threshold. The binary PHI-only mask mi was then constructed by projecting matched bounding regions into image space and then we applied a small dilation to cover boundary pixels around the rendered text, to ensure that the PHI is fully covered, and the bounding box is seamless. This strategy differs from generic text masking, as only OCR detections corresponding to manifest-defined identifiers were masked, which ensured that only the PHI was masked and not other supporting information of the image itself. B.3 Progressive Question De-Identification Details 

On the Question-side, the de-identification followed four privacy levels, where L0 retained the PHI-augmented question and served as the weakest text-side privacy condition. L1 applied regex-based masking to explicit surface-form identifiers, including names, dates, medical record numbers, phone numbers, email addresses, and hospital identifiers. L2 extended this process with context-aware masking, targeting identifier-like spans near anchors such as “Patient,” “Name,” “DOB,” “MRN,” “ID,” and “Hospital.” And finally at L3 level of text deidentification, we applied rewrite-based de-identification offline using the Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct [Yang et al., 2025]. The rewrite prompt instructed the model to remove any identity-bearing information if present at all while also preserving the medical question being asked. In practice, the rewrite was considered valid when the clinical finding, anatomy, imaging modality, disease cue, and question intent were preserved while demographic, institutional, or record-specific identifiers were removed, else we used the original question as the replacement, if the LLMs failed to preserve the core structure of the medical question being asked in level L3. Rewrites were generated just before the downstream VQA evaluation and were not adapted using the predictions of the evaluated VLMs. 

## C Implementation Details 

C.1 ClinX-PRISM Architecture and Optimization 

In ClinX, the PRISM module uses a no-skip, mask-conditioned encoder-decoder generator for the image-side PHI restoration. The PHI-overlaid image, denoted by ˜xi is then encoded through a convolutional downsampling path, while the PHI mask mi is used as a spatial conditioning signal for the SPADE residual blocks in the bottleneck and decoder. Unlike the skip-connected restoration baseline, ClinX-PRISM does not concatenate encoder features into the decoder. This design forces reconstruction of the PHI region through the mask-conditioned bottleneck rather than through direct low-level feature reuse from the PHI-contaminated input, ensuring that the output does not contain any residual information from the masked region. The generated output, denoted by ˆyi is not used for replacing the complete image. It is only used for generating the pixels within the PHI mask; outside of the mask, the original input or pixels from the PHI-overlaid image is retained. Next, the primary PRISM condition executes the post-processing stage within the mask as discussed in the main body of the paper. The secondary PRISM-flat diagnosis is a variation where no post-processing stage takes place. For the case of adversarial supervision, we use the PatchGAN architecture for the discriminator network. C.2 LLM-as-a-Judge Prompting and Scoring Protocol 

LLM-as-a-Judge scoring was applied as an additional form of evaluation besides exact match and token-level F1 scoring. Each LLM-as-a-Judge was presented with the query, the ground truth short answer, and model prediction, after which it returned either a positive or negative binary score based on whether the predicted answer was clinically consistent with the ground truth. Image, privacy condition, LLM name, and PHIs were not presented to each judge. D.2 Limitations of the Privacy Evaluation 

While ClinX aims to mitigate exposure of PHI in the medical datasets through visible image burned-in text and question-side identifiers, but no explicit and formal cryptographic privacy protection is provided. The image-side masks are contingent on OCR detection, and any missed identifier that stays out of the PHI mask will be left in even after sanitization. Residual OCR leakage after sanitization is thus reported instead of assuming perfect PHI localization. The proposed benchmark employs synthetic PHI injection to avoid handling and exposure of actual PHI present in the real medical dataset, in order to conduct repeatable evaluation of privacy loss. The advantage of this approach is in providing a controlled framework for evaluation, however it may fail to account for some visual and linguistic variances present in real medical images. Similarly, the leakage measures are contingent on OCR recall, with the focus on token-level recovery rather than more comprehensive re-identification risks from non-textual visual content, linguistic characteristics, institutional style, and other demographic shortcuts. We evaluated ClinX-PRISM module using an adversarial inpainting backbone and a skip-connected restoration ablation. We did not include diffusion-based inpainting models in the primary comparison. Diffusion models may improve perceptual restoration quality, but they introduce additional prompt design, stochastic sampling, computational cost, and reproducibility considerations.
