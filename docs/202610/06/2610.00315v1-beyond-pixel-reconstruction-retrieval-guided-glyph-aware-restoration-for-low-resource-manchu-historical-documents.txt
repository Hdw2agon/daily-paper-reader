Beyond Pixel Reconstruction: Retrieval-Guided Glyph-Aware Restoration for
Low-Resource Manchu Historical Documents
Ting Huang1,*, Dongdong Wang2,*, Mingqiu Liang1, Siyang Lu1,†
1School of Computer Science and Technology, Beijing Jiaotong University, Beijing, China
2College of Design, Construction, and Planning, University of Florida, Gainesville, FL, USA
Abstract
Restoring low-resource historical documents, such as Manchu
historical documents, is critical for preserving cultural her-
itage but remains challenging due to severe data scarcity
and complex degradation patterns. Although recent diffusion-
based restoration methods have substantially improved visual
reconstruction quality, the scarcity of annotated Manchu his-
torical documents necessitates transferring pretrained models
from natural-image or other writing-system domains. Such
transferred models provide limited script-specific knowledge,
while their pixel-centric optimization objectives fail to ex-
plicitly preserve legitimate Manchu glyphs, let alone re-
cover the correct ones. We tackle this challenge by refor-
mulating historical document restoration from pixel recon-
struction to glyph-aware recovery, leveraging a script-specific
glyph inventory together with the semantic reasoning ca-
pability of a large vision-language model, rather than di-
rectly synthesizing missing pixels. Accordingly, we propose
a Coarse-to-Fine Retrieval-Guided Glyph-Aware Restoration
(CRGR) framework, in which a vision encoder retrieves struc-
turally similar candidates and a large vision-language model
identifies the most appropriate glyph. This retrieval-before-
restoration paradigm enables glyph-aware restoration without
task-specific training while promoting the recovery of legiti-
mate Manchu glyphs. We further establish a Glyph-oriented
Evaluation Protocol, demonstrating that faithful restoration
should be evaluated beyond image fidelity by explicitly as-
sessing glyph validity, identity preservation, and structural
consistency. Extensive experiments demonstrate that CRGR
achieves the highest glyph structural similarity (GSS = 0.9986)
and superior restoration accuracy on intact content (GRA =
0.9600), validating retrieval-before-restoration as an effective
paradigm for improving glyph fidelity inlow-resource Manchu
historical document restoration.

Introduction
Historical document restoration is a fundamental task in
cultural heritage preservation and digital humanities, aim-
ing to recover degraded historical documents for subsequent
archival conservation, scholarly analysis, and digital dissem-
ination (Pal et al. 2017). Recent advances in deep learning
have significantly improved historical document restoration
across various degradation scenarios (Zhu et al. 2024). How-
ever, the effectiveness of existing methods largely depends on

the characteristics of target writing systems, including train-
ing data availability, glyph diversity, and document degra-
dation patterns. Existing restoration methods have achieved
promising results on high-resource scripts,where relatively
large-scale datasets and representative training samples are
available. In contrast, many under-resourced writing systems,
including Manchu, Khitan, and Jurchen, contain only lim-
ited historical collections and exhibit substantial variations
in glyph styles. Furthermore, long-term aging, insect dam-
age, ink fading, and physical deterioration introduce complex
degradation patterns, making restoration particularly chal-
lenging for low-resource scripts. Consequently, restoration
failures frequently arise not only from missing visual details
but also from inaccurate recovery of glyph structures.
Existing restoration methods mainly formulate histori-
cal document restoration primarily as a pixel reconstruction
problem. Their objective is to infer missing contents from sur-
rounding image contexts and generate visually plausible re-
sults (Suvorov et al. 2022). This pixel-centric formulation im-
plicitly assume that visual similarity is a reliable surrogate for
glyph correctness. However, this assumption becomes unreli-
able for low-resource historical scripts containing highly sim-
ilar glyph structures. Achieving robust restoration under this
paradigm typically requires abundant annotated data, power-
ful pretrained models, or task-specific adaptation, which are
difficult to obtain for many endangered writing systems. Con-
sequently, visually plausible reconstructions may still contain
incorrect glyph structures or distorted stroke patterns. Since
restoration errors can propagate to downstream tasks such as
optical character recognition, historical interpretation, and
linguistic analysis, preserving glyph structures and visual
integrity is more important than pixel-level similarity alone.
To address these challenges, we reformulate historical doc-
ument restoration as a retrieval-guided glyph recovery prob-
lem and propose Coarse-to-Fine Retrieval-Guided Glyph-
Aware Restoration (CRGR), taking bilingual Manchu–
Chinese woodblock-printed historical documents as a rep-
resentative low-resource restoration benchmark. CRGR first
employs a vision encoder to retrieve Top-K structurally sim-
ilar glyph candidates from an intact glyph gallery, followed
by fine-grained candidate verification using a frozen vision-
language model. Directly applying VLM verification over
the entire glyph inventory can introduce substantial com-

Related Work
2.1
Visual Reconstruction
Most existing historical glyph restoration methods formulate
restoration as a visual reconstruction problem, where miss-
ing contents are inferred from surrounding image contexts
and optimized for pixel-level fidelity (Pathak et al. 2016;
Yu et al. 2019). Early deep learning approaches mainly
rely on context-aware image inpainting to recover visually
plausible contents, represented by adaptive attention-based
models such as AOT-GAN (Zeng et al. 2023). To better
handle large missing regions, large-receptive-field restora-
tion methods, such as LaMa (Suvorov et al. 2022), exploit
Fourier convolutions to capture long-range contextual de-
pendencies and improve structural continuity. More recently,
diffusion-based restoration methods, such as Unified Diffu-
sion Transformer (UDT) (Kim et al. 2025), have demon-
strated remarkable generation capability by progressively re-
fining corrupted regions through iterative denoising. Mean-
while, document-oriented restoration frameworks, such as
TextDoctor (Lu et al. 2025), DocRevive (Purkayastha et al.
2026), and GSDM (Zhu et al. 2024), further incorporate
textual structures, degradation-aware priors, and document
layouts to improve restoration quality on degraded historical
documents. Despite their strong visual reconstruction capa-
bility, these methods primarily optimize appearance consis-
tency and implicitly assume that visually plausible restora-
tion preserves glyph structures. This assumption becomes
unreliable for low-resource historical scripts containing vi-
sually similar glyph structures, where visual realism does not
necessarily guarantee structural correctness.
2.2
Structure-aware Restoration
To improve glyph integrity during restoration, recent stud-
ies introduce explicit structural constraints beyond conven-
tional image reconstruction. Stroke-aware restoration meth-
ods preserve character topology by modeling stroke con-
tinuity and structural dependencies, while Transformer-
based approaches, such as LWTFormer (Ruan et al. 2026),
further capture long-range relationships among character
components. Diffusion-based structural restoration has also

glyph generation constraints (Li et al. 2024). In paral-

data. Preserving glyph structures alone cannot address the
challenge of distinguishing visually similar glyph patterns.
2.3
Retrieval-guided and Vision-language
Restoration
Recent studies have begun moving beyond purely pixel-level
image generation by incorporating glyph retrieval and vision-
language models to improve glyph discrimination during his-
torical document restoration. These methods can be broadly
categorized according to how glyph retrieval and vision-
language reasoning are incorporated into the restoration pro-
cess. Early efforts focused on learning more discriminative
glyph representations. Restoring Ancient Ideograph jointly
optimizes image restoration, character recognition, and char-
acter prediction within a unified multi-task framework, en-
abling more discriminative glyph feature learning (Duan,
Wang, and Su 2024). Subsequent studies introduced cross-
modal representation alignment to jointly model visual glyph
structures and auxiliary textual information for historical in-
scription restoration (Liu et al. 2025). Benefiting from re-
cent advances in multimodal learning, large vision-language
models have also been introduced into historical document
restoration. EpiAgent (Zhu et al. 2026) introduces mul-
timodal reasoning for historical document understanding,
while UniHIR (Zhang et al. 2026) further integrates unified
multimodal modeling for historical inscription restoration.
Despite these advances, most existing methods rely on abun-
dant annotated data, task-specific adaptation, or end-to-end
multimodal optimization. Many VLM-augmented restora-
tion approaches aim to synthesize pixel content directly and
require domain fine-tuning. Such requirements are difficult
to satisfy for low-resource historical documents. However,
existing methods generally do not explicitly decouple can-
didate retrieval from candidate verification. Consequently,
existing approaches either optimize pixel generation or rely
on task-specific adaptation, while retrieval-guided semantic
restoration utilizing authentic glyph references has not been
sufficiently explored for low-resource historical documents.
3
Methodology
3.1
Framework Overview
Following the retrieval-guided formulation, CRGR consists
of two consecutive stages: coarse glyph retrieval and candi-
date verification. The first stage retrieves a compact set of
structurally similar glyph candidates using a vision encoder,
while the second stage performs fine-grained candidate ver-
ification using a frozen vision-language model (VLM) to
identify the most appropriate reference glyph (Radford et al.
2021). The selected reference glyph is then inserted into
the corrupted region to complete document restoration. The
overall architecture is illustrated in Fig. 1, and the data flow
of the proposed framework is summarized in Table 1.
3.2
Glyph Knowledge Base
Unlike generative restoration methods that synthesize miss-
ing content from learned priors, our framework restores dam-
aged glyphs by retrieving valid references from an authentic
glyph knowledge base. The knowledge base is constructed
offline from intact historical historical documents and serves
as the search space for subsequent retrieval and verification.
Specifically, each intact glyph is first localized and cropped
using a pretrained glyph detector. The extracted glyphs are
then normalized to a unified resolution while preserving their
glyph identities. Collectively, these glyph instances form a
reference gallery G = {g1, g2, . . . , gN}, where each gi de-
notes an authentic glyph observed in historical documents.
The knowledge base is fixed during inference and is shared
across all restoration samples. Since every restored glyph
is selected from G rather than synthesized pixel by pixel,
the proposed framework naturally constrains restoration to
valid historical glyphs, reducing the possibility of generating
unsupported glyph forms.
3.3
Coarse Glyph Retrieval
Given a damaged glyph, the first stage of CRGR retrieves a
compact set of structurally similar reference glyphs from an
intact glyph gallery using discriminative visual representa-
tions. A vision encoder is employed to extract hierarchical
glyph features from both damaged glyphs and intact reference
glyphs. These representations encode hierarchical visual pat-
terns of glyph structures, providing discriminative features
for retrieval.

3.4
VLM-based Candidate Verification
Visual similarity alone is insufficient because structurally
similar glyphs may still exhibit subtle structural differences
that are difficult to distinguish. The VLM compares the dam-
aged glyph with each retrieved candidate under a predefined
prompt and estimates their matching scores. The candidate
with the highest matching score is selected as the reference
glyph for restoration. The VLM receives the masked glyph
and each retrieved candidate as input and outputs a matching
score under a fixed prompt template.

3.5
Glyph-oriented Evaluation Protocol
Existing restoration metrics, such as PSNR, SSIM, and
LPIPS, primarily assess image fidelity and cannot distinguish
structurally similar glyphs with different semantic identi-
ties. As a result, visually plausible but semantically incorrect
restorations may still receive high level scores. To address
this limitation, we establish a Glyph-oriented Evaluation
Protocol that explicitly evaluates restoration quality from the
perspective of glyph correctness rather than pixel similarity.
Our protocol consists of two stages. First, a pretrained
glyph detector localizes individual glyph instances from
restored historical documents and extracts discriminative
glyph representations. Second, the detected glyphs are eval-
uated using three complementary metrics that jointly assess
glyph identity preservation and structural fidelity: Glyph
Restoration Accuracy (GRA), Cross-category Confusion
Rate (CCR), and Glyph Structure Similarity (GSS).

4
Experiments
4.1
Experimental Setup
Dataset.
To the best of our knowledge, no public bench-
mark targets retrieval-guided glyph restoration; therefore,
we construct a benchmark of scanned Manchu-Han bilin-
gual woodblock-printed documents from multiple sources.
We evaluate CRGR under full-page and patch-level restora-
tion settings. For patch-level evaluation, each page is divided
into 3×3 sub-regions, and patches containing masked glyphs
are retained. A pretrained YOLO detector constructs an intact
glyph gallery, yielding over 15,000 reference glyph instances.
The gallery serves as the retrieval database for CRGR.
Baselines.
We compare CRGR with five representa-
tive restoration approaches covering different restoration
paradigms, including CNN-based, GAN-based, diffusion-
based, and document restoration methods. We select publicly
available methods with released pretrained models and re-
producible inference pipelines. Following the official imple-
mentation setting, LDM adopts a 512 × 512 input resolution
for full-page restoration (Rombach et al. 2021).

Evaluation Metrics.
We primarily evaluate restoration
performance using the proposed glyph-oriented metrics, in-
cluding Glyph Restoration Accuracy (GRA), Cross-category
Confusion Rate (CCR), and Glyph Structure Similarity
(GSS). These metrics are reported on both masked and in-
tact regions to evaluate glyph recovery and preservation of
original document contents. In addition, image-level met-
rics, including PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, RMSE, and F1-score
for foreground reconstruction, are additionally reported.
Implementation Details.
The proposed CRGR consists of
a coarse glyph retrieval stage and a VLM-based candidate
verification stage. We adopt Swin V2 as the visual encoder
for glyph feature extraction and candidate retrieval, and em-
ploy a frozen InternVL model for candidate verification (Liu
et al. 2021; Chen et al. 2024). The VLM receives the damaged
glyph and each retrieved candidate as input and produces a
matching score under a fixed prompt template. The candi-
date with the highest verification score is selected from the
Top-K retrieved candidates as the reference glyph. We set
K = 5 in all experiments. All experiments are conducted
on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU (24 GB) using the
PyTorch framework. All backbone models utilize pretrained
weights without task-specific fine-tuning. Ablation studies
further analyze the effects of different retrieval backbones,
VLM verification models, and candidate numbers.
4.2
Comparison with Representative Restoration
Methods
Qualitative Comparison.
We first compare CRGR with
representative restoration methods under the same masking
setting. Figure 2 presents qualitative comparisons on full-
page document restoration. Specifically, LaMa and AOT-
GAN may produce noticeable artifacts in masked regions, in-
cluding local appearance inconsistencies and distorted stroke
patterns, possibly due to the absence of explicit glyph-level
constraints. Diffusion-based approaches, such as LDM and
GSDM, generally achieve smoother visual reconstruction;
however, the restored glyph appearances may still differ from
the original glyph structures in some cases. PixelHacker fur-
ther improves document-level restoration quality (Xu et al.
2025); however, such reconstruction-based approaches may
still face challenges when distinguishing visually similar his-
torical glyphs. In contrast, CRGR performs reference-based
glyph restoration by retrieving intact glyph instances from
a gallery of historical documents and verifying candidate
consistency with a frozen vision-language model. Instead
of synthesizing missing pixels, CRGR directly restores cor-
rupted regions using verified reference glyphs. This strategy
effectively preserves original glyph structures, printed stroke
characteristics, and document layout while avoiding incor-
rect character reconstruction.

Effect of VLM-based Verification.
We further evaluate
the contribution of vision-language verification by compar-

VLM-based verification further improves candidate discrim-

4.4
Failure Analysis
Although CRGR achieves strong restoration performance,
failure cases may still occur in challenging scenarios where
the reference evidence is insufficient for accurate glyph iden-
tification. We analyze representative failure cases to in-
vestigate the limitations of the proposed retrieval-guided
paradigm. As shown in Fig. 4, failures can be categorized
into three scenarios: (1) gallery coverage limitation, where
the correct glyph is absent from the reference gallery and
therefore cannot be retrieved; (2) insufficient structural in-
formation, where severe degradation removes discriminative
glyph cues and makes candidate retrieval unreliable; and
(3)ambiguous glyph identity, where visually similar glyphs
introduce uncertainty during candidate verification. These
cases indicate that CRGR is mainly limited by reference
coverage and the availability of discriminative structural ev-
idence, rather than the restoration mechanism itself.
5
Conclusion
This paper presented CRGR, a retrieval-guided glyph-aware
restoration framework for low-resource historical document
restoration. Instead of treating restoration as unconstrained
pixel generation, CRGR reformulates the problem as coarse-
to-fine glyph retrieval followed by VLM-based candidate
verification. This formulation enables identity-aware and
structure-preserving restoration using authentic historical
references rather than synthesized glyphs. Experimental re-
sults demonstrate that the proposed retrieval-and-verification
paradigm improves glyph restoration fidelity while pre-
serving intact document contents. More broadly, our study
suggests that low-resource historical document restoration
should move beyond unconstrained pixel generation toward
retrieval-guided semantic restoration, where authentic ref-
erences and multimodal reasoning jointly improve glyph
structural preservation across diverse ancient writing low-
resource historical document restoration scenarios.
