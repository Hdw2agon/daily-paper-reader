ABSTRACT 

Deep generative networks have recently achieved unprece-dented performance in precise image and video editing using sophisticated textual prompts. However, the effectiveness of such models heavily depends on access to very large super-vised and annotated image datasets, which can be very difficult to obtain. This is particularly true for satellite instruments, which very rarely overlap with labelled data, and suffer from domain shifts in the rare occasions they do. In this paper, we investigate the potential of flow matching models for un-supervised domain adaptation of satellite radiometer images. Our main contribution is a novel unsupervised method that achieves precise domain alignment by leveraging parts of the deterministic ordinary differential equations in flow match-ing models, conditioned on different satellite instruments. A key strength of our approach is its ability to preserve essen-tial information while adapting across any domains since the perturbations are in theory bijective. Extensive experiments conducted on the GPM-Core constellation show the benefit of our conditional domain adaptation, particularly in improving rain precipitation estimation from radiometer imagery. 

Index Terms — Unsupervised Domain Adaptation, Flow Matching, Microwave Imagery, Precipitation Estimation. 

1. INTRODUCTION 

Precipitation estimation from satellite imagery holds tremen-dous potential for both scientific and societal applications, as vast amounts of data are continuously acquired worldwide. Moreover, unlike ground-based radar observations — which are often sparse or entirely absent in many developing coun-tries — satellite constellations provide near–real-time, global-scale coverage of the Earth’s surface. This capability is crucial not only for improving water resource management, but also for strengthening our ability to predict extreme hydrological events, such as major floods. This is particularly true for the Global Precipitation Measurement (GPM) mission [1], an international collaboration that provides unprecedented spa-tiotemporal coverage of global precipitation through a con-stellation of twelve satellites, primarily equipped with passive microwave radiometers. Within this constellation, the GPM Core Observatory (GPM-Core) plays a vital role, as it uniquely carries a dual-frequency precipitation radar (DPR) capable of directly estimating raining rates (RR), alongside the GPM Mi-crowave Imager (GMI), which measures brightness tempera-tures (TB). This contrasts with the other satellites in the con-stellation, which are equipped only with passive microwave radiometers, and can measure only brightness temperatures. This unique feature of the GPM-Core satellite opens up promising opportunities for deep learning approaches that ex-ploit the combined DPR and GMI measurements to enhance precipitation estimation. This is particularly true for the meth-ods [2, 3], employing deep neural networks to predict rain rates using the GPM-Core satellite brightness temperatures, and which have shown the capability to outperform classi-cal approaches like GPROF [4]. Among these methods, the strength of DRAIN [2] lies in its convolutional architecture, which exploits the spatial structure of 2D images rather than relying on independent per-pixel estimates as in GPROF — and consequently it often achieves superior performance. This approach is particularly compelling given that the swath of the DPR measurements on the GPM-Core satellite is nearly four times narrower than that of the GMI, enabling rain rate esti-mation over much larger spatial domains. However, despite those very impressive performances, the DRAIN algorithm does not generalize to other satellites from the constellation and achieves much poorer results when using the correspond-ing brightness temperatures. This is generally explained by the different orbit characteristics of the employed instruments such as channels, angle of view, or pixel resolutions. Al-though GPM-Core shares overlapping orbits with those other satellites, training a supervised domain-transfer algorithm is challenging, as these very scarce overlaps occur almost exclu-sively near 60 ◦ latitude north and south, making the model prone to geographical biases. Additionally, varying temporal offsets of up to 15 minutes exist between acquisitions, making those observations suitable only as pseudo ground truths. To tackle this challenge of non-colocated data in satellite imagery, more general strategies often turn to unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA), which has the key advantage of not requiring corresponding ground truth images. These ap-proaches aim to transform the content of an image to match arXiv:2610.01890v1 [cs.CV] 1 Oct 2026 a target distribution by leveraging deep generative neural net-works. Traditionally, such methods have heavily relied on Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) [5, 6], and in par-ticular CycleGAN [7], which allows for indirect supervision through a cycle-consistency reconstruction loss. This is the case for the methods in [8], which use CycleGANs along with the DRAIN [2] to predict raining rates from the DMSP-F18 satellite of the Defense Meteorological Satellite Program. More precisely the images obtained with its SSMI/S mi-crowave imager are modified so as to very closely match the distribution of those from GMI. Then, the DRAIN algorithm is run on those adapted images to obtain raining rates. By doing so, very promising results are obtained in [8], and ul-timately, the only drawback of the method is the notoriously unstable training of GANs [5] — which makes it very chal-lenging for this method to be extended to other satellites from the constantly evolving GPM constellation. In order to address these aforementioned issues, we present a novel unsupervised domain adaptation method, based on the modern Flow Matching [9, 10, 11] paradigm so as to adapt brightness temperatures from the F18 satellite to those of the GPM instrument. The main advantage of this approach resides in the bijective nature of flow based models, which possess the remarkable ability of ”ideally” noising and denoising an im-age while preserving the most salient physical and structural information of the original image. Extensive experiments showcase the benefits of our approach, matching or outper-forming other state-of-the-art approaches while being much easier to train. 

2. RELATED WORK 

Deep generative models have widely been explored for un-supervised domain adaptation. Among them, GAN-based approaches [5], particularly CycleGAN [7] and related image-to-image translation methods [6], have historically achieved strong results. However their main drawback is the training instability and lack of diversity frequently observed with adversarial training objectives [5]. More recently, with the emergence of diffusion models [12, 13], novel interesting approaches have emerged as very competitive alternatives. Those include approaches like Flow Matching [9, 10, 11], which are inspired by continuous normalizing flows [14] and retain the bijective properties inherited from normaliz-ing flows [15], while enabling simulation-free training and significantly faster sampling. Within this line of work, some very recent approaches go beyond the standard Gaussian assumption by allowing arbitrary source and target distri-butions [11, 16, 17], thereby enabling the learning of a pushforward mapping directly between different image distri-butions. Recent related works also investigate unsupervised domain alignment through structured latent decompositions with the flow matching objective [18]. Alternatively, some works exploit the bijective nature of neural networks for un-supervised domain adaptation, most notably [19] employs normalizing flows with adversarial losses, and [20] reverses ordinary differential equations (ODEs) for diffusion models. The work in [20] is closely related to our approach but differs in three key aspects: (i) it trains a separate model for each modality rather than relying on conditional generative models; (ii) it integrates the entire ODE solver to map images fully to Gaussian noise, whereas we integrate only a portion of the trajectory to optimally add noise while preserving image content, and this also differs from stochastic partial noising strategies such as [21]; and (iii) it adopts diffusion instead of the more recent flow-matching paradigm, which is known to yield straighter trajectories, faster integration, and improved image quality [9]. Building on these advantages, we explore conditional flow-matching–based generative models as an alternative for unsupervised domain adaptation, motivated by their stable training, invertible bijective mappings, and well-defined likelihoods. Although this study considers a limited set of satellites, these properties make them promising for an extension to additional sensors in the GPM constellation.

EXPERIMENTS Datasets. The training dataset consists of all GMI and F18 observations acquired in 2018. Following [2, 8] only fre-quencies useful for precipitation estimation are kept. These correspond to 36.6GHz and 89.0GHz for GMI, and 37.0GHz and 91.6GHz for the SSMIS/S instrument. Both vertical and horizontal polarisations are included, resulting in input images with four channels; whose main differences are summarized in Table 1. To ensure consistency between sensors, polar regions are excluded from the SSMI/S data (latitudes above 70 ◦N or below 70 ◦S), so as to match the spatial coverage of GMI. The test dataset contains all overlaps between the GMI and F18 satellite images from the year 2019, that have more than 50 pixels with rain rates higher than 3mm/h. This corresponds to roughly 18 × 10 6 adapted, collocated pixels from the F18 to the GMI domain. Evaluation Metrics. To assess the performance of our method, we employ the DRAIN framework [2] to estimate raining rates from the adapted F18-to-GMI images. Those are then compared with RRs obtained from the original GMI images. To ensure pixels between these images are matched, a KDTree algorithm is applied to find the nearest neighbors within a region of 10km, before calculating distance metrics such as the mean error (ME), mean absolute error (MAE), and the root mean squared error (RMSE). Besides precipitation estimation, we also perform a qualitative assessment of the adapted images. 

Implementation Details. Experiments are conducted using a class-conditional DiT [22], trained for 50 epochs using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 1 .0 × 10 −4 and a batch size of 64. The model uses patch embeddings of size 4, 12 attention heads across 12 transformer layers, and a hidden dimension of 384, resulting in a total of 32.91M parame-ters. During training, input images are randomly cropped to a resolution of 128 × 128 and augmented with random horizontal and vertical flips. In practice, when performing domain adaptation, we set the time variables 𝑡 0 and 𝑡 1 from Eq. 8, to respectively 0.0 and 0.5. For the ODE solver, we use the default Dormand-Prince method from the torchdiffeq library [14]. 

Baselines and Comparative Methods. For the baseline, we use the non-adapted F18 images as direct input to our generative model. For the comparative methods, we evaluate against three approaches: (i) stochastic diffusion noising (as in [21]), where Eq. 2 is applied with random Gaussian noise to perturb samples at 𝑡 = 0.5; (ii) full ODE noising (as in [20]), which is a special case of our formulation with 𝑡 1 = 1.0in Eq. 8; and (iii) CycleGAN (from [8]), which also focused on adapting F18 images to the GMI domain.

5. CONCLUSION 

In this paper, we presented a novel approach for unsuper-vised domain adaptation using conditional flow matching. The strength of our method lies in its ability to perform precise, content-aware modifications through invertible and gradual transformations governed by conditional ODEs. Our experiments demonstrate the benefits of this unsupervised do-main adaptation approach, highlighting its effectiveness in two-domain scenarios — and showcasing great promise for multiple domains. As future work, we aim to extend this method to handle multiple satellite instruments both within and beyond the GPM-Core constellation, further exploring its potential for large-scale multi-domain adaptation.
