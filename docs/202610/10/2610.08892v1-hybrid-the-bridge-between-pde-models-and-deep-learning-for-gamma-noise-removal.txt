# Hybrid++: The Bridge between PDE Models and Deep Learning for Gamma Noise Removal 

Mahipal Jetta [0000 −0002 −9621 −2264] and Sujato Dutta 

Mahindra University 

Abstract. Multiplicative gamma noise is one of the dominant noise fac-tors in Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) and medical ultrasound images. They are dependent on pixel level noises due to which they are highly varying across the image and harder to handle as compared to addi-tive noise. The denoising methods to address this noise currently in-clude classical partial differential equation (PDE) methods which are good for interpretation but lack the restoration ability as compared to the state-of-the-art models while deep convolutional networks like DnCNN achieve high performance but at the cost of transparency due to which practitioners are skeptical to use them in high-risk critical fields like medicine. This paper presents Hybrid++, a novel trainable nonlinear reaction-diffusion architecture that addresses both the con-cerns - staying interpretable while offering performance close to huge black-box models. It combines a fully learnable PDE initialization with a 3-stage reaction-diffusion network having 64-channel multiscale filter banks, 4-layer Squeeze-and-Excitation attention-based influence func-tions and a 64-dimensional noise level embedding. It uses a two-phase training strategy, stage-wise optimization followed by joint end-to-end re-finement which enables co-adaptation of all learnable parameters. On the FoE benchmark, Hybrid++ substantially improves over classical PDE, BM3D and the original TNRD baselines. In the severe-noise setting L=1, it comes within 0.23 dB PSNR of a separately trained DnCNN while us-ing only about 8% of its parameters. We therefore position Hybrid++ not as a universal state-of-the-art image restoration backbone, but as a compact, physically structured reaction-diffusion model for multiplica-tive gamma noise. 

Keywords: Gamma noise · Image Denoising · Partial Differential Equa-tions (PDE). 

# 1 Introduction 

Multiplicative gamma noise is a significant challenge to deal with in imaging systems such as Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) and medical ultrasound where the noise is signal-dependent and cannot be handled by standard additive-noise denoising models. Classical methods like partial differential equations (PDEs) [11] try to address this by adapting smoothing strength to local image gradients and intensity. However, they rely on fixed, handcrafted parameters and struggle arXiv:2610.08892v1 [eess.IV] 6 Oct 2026 2 M. Jetta, S. Dutta 

to recover complex features from the noisy images. On the other hand, deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs) such as DnCNN [17] achieve remarkable performance in denoising but operate as black box mod-els which can’t be easily interpreted and lack transparency. They also require training hundreds of thousands of parameters. Traditional non-local methods like BM3D [3], are effective for additive Gaussian noise but require ad-hoc adap-tations (e.g. homomorphic transforms) for multiplicative noise and often fail to produce optimal results. Trainable Nonlinear Reaction Diffusion (TNRD) [2] was one of the breakthroughs offering a middle ground by unfolding the de-noising process as a sequence of interpretable diffusion stages with learnable parameters. However, the original TNRD was meant mainly for additive noise and its compact parameterization limits its capacity for handling the more chal-lenging multiplicative noise. More recently, image restoration has been strongly influenced by transformer and diffusion-based architectures. Models such as SwinIR [8] and Restormer [16] demonstrate the effectiveness of windowed or efficient self-attention for general image restoration and denoising, while re-cent diffusion-transformer restoration methods [14] further improve degrada-tion modeling through generative priors and coarse-to-fine refinement. In the SAR despeckling literature[10], CNN, contrastive-learning [4], transformer, and diffusion-based methods have also been explored for removing speckle from real or simulated SAR images. These methods are important modern base-lines for broad image restoration. However, our focus is different, we design our main architecture Hybrid++ as a compact, interpretable extension of the PDE/reaction-diffusion family for multiplicative gamma noise, rather than as a large generic restoration backbone. Our main contributions are: 

– A fully differentiable PDE initialization module whose parameters ( α, β) are optimized via backpropagation in place of traditional grid search. 

– Parallel multiscale filter banks (32 filters of 3×3 and 32 filters of 5×5) pro-ducing a 64-channel feature representation that captures both fine textures and gradients while also handling broader structural context. 

– A 4-layer attention influence function with an expanded squeeze-excitation [7] bottleneck (64 →16 →64) (here the numbers denote the number of neurons in the respective layers of the network), enabling deep nonlinear feature weighting without normalization layers. 

– A 3-layer MLP [12] noise embedding (1 →32 →64 →64) that conditions every stage on the noise severity allowing a single unified model to handle varying noise levels. 

– A two-phase training strategy combining stage-wise optimization with joint end-to-end refinement for co-adaptation of all parameters including the PDE ones. 

– Comprehensive evaluation showing that Hybrid++ improves strongly over PDE, BM3D, and TNRD baselines, while approaching DnCNN [17] specif-ically in the severe-noise setting L=1. # 2 Proposed Method: Hybrid++ 

2.1 Multiplicative Gamma Noise Model 

Let u denote the clean grayscale image and f the noisy observation. The multi-plicative gamma noise model is: 

f = u · n, n ∼ Γ (L, 1/L ), (1) where Γ is the gamma distribution, L is the number of looks, yielding E[n] = 1 

and Var (n) = 1 /L . Low L (e.g., L=1 ) corresponds to severe noise, while higher 

L (e.g., L=10 ) represents milder noise levels. 

2.2 Architecture Overview 

Hybrid++ is built directly upon the Trainable Nonlinear Reaction Diffusion (TNRD) framework [2], preserving its core mathematical ideation - a sequence of reaction-diffusion stages, each comprising learnable convolution filters, a non-linear influence function and a data-fidelity reaction term while systematically upgrading them for suiting multiplicative gamma noise and giving better per-formance. Hybrid++ is not a purely hand-crafted PDE with all parameters fixed analytically. Rather, the denoising trajectory remains organized as explicit reaction-diffusion updates. The PDE initialization, multiscale diffusion filters, bounded influence function, stage-wise fidelity weights λt, and noise-conditioned reaction term each have identifiable roles in the restoration dynamics. This dif-fers from a standard feed-forward CNN such as DnCNN, where intermediate feature maps do not directly correspond to a known iterative restoration pro-cess. Key enhancement: Unlike classical PDE [11] where α and β are fixed scalars decided by grid search, Hybrid++ makes these learnable, allowing the model to jointly optimize the PDE alongside the convolutional stages using back-propagation. 2.4 Multiscale Filter Banks with Skip Connections 

At each stage t, the network receives a 2-channel input formed by concate-nating the current estimate ut−1 with the original noisy observation f . This skip-connection [6] provides access to high-frequency information that otherwise would have been lost through successive diffusion steps. This input is processed by two parallel and independent learnable filter banks: 

– 32 filters of size 3×3×2: A 3×3 kernel operating on the 2 channel input (the concatenation of the current estimate ut−1 and the original noisy image 

f ) to capture fine-grained local textures and sharp edge transitions. Title Suppressed Due to Excessive Length 5

– 32 filters of size 5×5×2: A 5×5 kernel to capture broader spatial context and large structural patterns. The filter responses are concatenated along the channel dimension, produc-ing a 64-channel feature map. This design is essential for multiplicative noise where noise patterns overlap a lot with structural high-frequency information. The dual-scale representation allows the network to separate noise from tex-ture at multiple spatial frequencies simultaneously. All filters are initialized with Kaiming-style scaling [5] and are fully learnable throughout training. 

2.5 Dynamic Noise Level Embedding 

To enable blind denoising across varying noise levels, a 3-layer Multi-Layer Per-ceptron (MLP) [12] maps the scalar noise strength parameter to a 64-dimensional conditioning vector: 

e = W3 · ReLU (W2 · ReLU (W1 · σL + b1) + b2) + b3, (3) where σL = 1 /√L is the noise strength derived from the gamma distribution’s coefficient of variation, bi’s are biases and W1 ∈ R32 ×1, W2 ∈ R64 ×32 , W3 ∈

R64 ×64 .This 64-dimensional embedding e is broadcast and added to the 64-channel filter responses at every stage. This makes the feature representation biased based on noise level. This design allows Hybrid++ to use a single unified noise-conditioned model for both L=1 and L=10 . In contrast, the TNRD and DnCNN baselines in our experiments are trained separately for each noise level. Thus, Hybrid++ is evaluated in the more flexible setting: it must learn one conditional mapping across noise severities rather than a separate optimum for each fixed L.

Design choice: The original TNRD [2] uses a simple 1-layer per-channel in-fluence function. By expanding this to 4 layers with an intermediate SE-attention [7] block, Hybrid++ can learn highly complex nonlinear response mappings. The 

tanh activations ensure bounded outputs to stay consistent with the physical dif-fusion formulation. Three prominent differences emerge relative to TNRD [2]: 

– Cross-channel interaction. The SE block [7] computes a global channel descriptor via average pooling and uses it to re-weight each of the 64 channels based on their global importance. Filters aligned with dominant image edges are amplified and the ones aligned with noise patterns are suppressed. A per-channel TNRD cannot perform this selection. 

– Input-dependent asymmetric shaping. Because the SE channel weights depend on the global average of the current feature map, the effective re-sponse curve of Φ changes from region to region: texturally complex patches activate a different channel mix than flat smooth regions. 

– Sharper edge-stopping via depth. Hybrid++ has four successive tanh 

compositions that raise the effective degree of the approximated penalty function as compared to a single tanh in original TNRD [2]. Fig. 2 illustrates the effective 1-D response along a single filter axis. Hy-brid++’s Φ shows a sharper transition and a lower saturation plateau demon-strating stronger selective suppression of small noise-like responses while better preserving large-magnitude edge responses. The reaction term derives from the MAP formulation for multiplicative gamma noise [1]. 

2.8 Two-Phase Training Strategy 

We use a two-phase optimization strategy: 

Phase 1: Stage-wise Initialization. Each of the 3 stages is trained se-quentially for 100 epochs. When training stage t, all prior stages are frozen and only stage t’s parameters alongwith the PDE [11] and embedding parameters are changed. This ensures each stage provides a meaningful refinement step. Train-ing uses Adam with lr = 10 −3 (learning rate), early stopping with patience 10 and gradient clipping at max norm 1.0. 

Phase 2: Joint End-to-End Refinement. After stage-wise initialization, 

all parameters across the 3 stages, the PDE module [11] and the noise embedding are unfrozen and jointly optimized for 50 epochs. This phase uses Adam with lr = 10 −4 and CosineAnnealingLR [9] ( Tmax =50 , ηmin =10 −6), allowing the entire network to co-adapt and fine-tune the inter-stage interactions. We use Mean Squared Error (MSE) as the loss function for both phases. The purpose of the two-phase strategy is not to keep the stages indepen-dent after training, but to provide a structured initialization before global co-adaptation. The stage-wise phase encourages each reaction-diffusion block to learn a meaningful refinement role. The later joint phase then fine-tunes the al-ready structured initialization across the PDE module, noise embedding, and all diffusion stages. Therefore, joint training does not cancel the stage-wise phase; it refines an initialization in which the stages have already learned progressively useful denoising roles.

3.1 Dataset 

We use the FoE dataset [13] containing 400 grayscale images that are split into 80% training, 10% validation, and 10% testing (40 test images) with a fixed random seed of 42. For qualitative evaluation on real-world data, we additionally test on real SAR images from the Sentinel-1&2 dataset. 8 M. Jetta, S. Dutta 

3.2 Baselines 

We compare Hybrid++ against four models including classical baselines, learning-based architectures and deep learning approaches: 

– BM3D [3]: Non-local patch-based method applied using homomorphic (log-domain) transform with noise variance σ2 = ψ1(L) (trigamma function). 

– PDE Baseline [11]: Classical nonlinear smooth diffusion PDE (Eq. 2) with hand-tuned parameters ( α=1 .0, β=2 .0). 

– TNRD [2]: Original 8-stage trainable reaction-diffusion. We trained separate models for each noise level. 

– DnCNN [17]: 17-layer deep CNN with batch normalization (approx. 556K parameters) for the black-box deep neural network architecture. We intentionally use DnCNN as the representative black-box deep CNN base-line rather than benchmarking against every recent transformer or diffusion restoration model. Recent restoration models are often designed as large general-purpose backbones for multiple degradation types, while our goal is to evaluate a compact reaction-diffusion extension specialized for multiplicative gamma noise. The selected baselines therefore isolate the main comparison axis of this work: classical PDE priors, non-local denoising, trainable reaction-diffusion, and deep CNN restoration. All learning-based models are trained with identical MSE loss, Adam optimizer and dataset split as our Hybrid++ architecture for fair com-parison. 

Remark on log-domain vs. direct-domain processing. BM3D [3] is an additive-noise denoiser. To handle multiplicative gamma noise we apply it via a homomorphic transform: the log of the noisy image is computed, BM3D is ap-plied with standard deviation σ = pψ1(L) (where ψ1 is the trigamma function, the log-domain noise variance of a Γ (L, 1/L ) variate), and the result is exponen-tiated. All other models - PDE [11], TNRD[2], DnCNN[17] and Hy-brid++ are applied directly in the original image domain without any log transformation. BM3D is evaluated in its strongest available configuration for gamma noise (log-domain) so it is fair comparison with the learning-based methods which either use a direct gamma prior (PDE, Hybrid++) or a purely data-driven mapping (TNRD, DnCNN).

# 6 Conclusion 

This paper presented Hybrid++, a physics-informed architecture for multiplica-tive gamma noise removal that bridges the divide between classical hand-crafted PDE-based methods and modern deep learning. By expanding the TNRD frame-work with learnable PDE initialization, 64-channel multiscale filter banks, 4-layer SE-attention influence functions, 64-dimensional noise embeddings and a two-phase training strategy, Hybrid++ achieved 20.86 dB at L=1 and 25.02 dB at L=10 on the FoE benchmark while only requiring to train a single unified model. Most significantly, a 44,408-parameter model closed the gap to the 556K-parameter DnCNN to within just 0.23 dB at severe noise levels. These results suggest that principled physical priors when augmented with targeted learnable components, can close a substantial part of the gap to black-box CNNs in the se-vere multiplicative-noise regime while preserving a structured reaction-diffusion interpretation. Future work should extend the comparison to larger transformer and diffusion restoration models, broader SAR datasets, and real-noise training protocols.
