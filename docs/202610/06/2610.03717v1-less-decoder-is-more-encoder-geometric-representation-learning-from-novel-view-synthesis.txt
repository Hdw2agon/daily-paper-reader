Abstract
This paper examines the role of Novel View Synthesis (NVS) in geometric represen-
tation learning. In principle, NVS should reason about 3D scene structure, thereby
enabling transferable multi-view geometric representations. Yet, existing encoder-
based NVS methods yield poor representations. This is not because of a lack of
supervisory signal, but rather due to inconspicuous architectural choices: spatially
expressive decoders that dilute representational capabilities of the scene encoder,
and low-level pixel-space targets that hinder feature learning. We present SNAP, a
self-supervised encoder-decoder transformer that addresses both through a pose-
conditioned local decoder and a latent-space reconstruction objective. SNAP is
task agnostic, and we show that it is competitive with special-purpose geometry-
supervised methods. SNAP also performs competitively against self-supervised
representations across five tasks: visual localization, pose estimation, point corre-
spondence, depth estimation, and robot manipulation. Remarkably, SNAP’s patch
features exhibit emergent viewpoint invariance that approaches heavily supervised
models despite lower compute and data budgets. Under camera shifts where stan-
dard 2D representations collapse, SNAP degrades more gracefully, revealing that
restricting decoder expressivity actively prevents the suppression of transferable
geometric structure. https://snap-nvs.github.io

Introduction
Geometric understanding from images is a foundation of computer vision. Methods like DUSt3R [1],
MASt3R [2], and VGGT [3] demonstrate that transformers can produce rich, transferable geometric
representations, when trained on large-scale annotated 3D data. This supervision requirement is a
fundamental bottleneck since dense 3D ground truth is expensive to collect at scale.
Novel View Synthesis (NVS) offers a compelling alternative [4–7]. These models must reason about
3D scene structure to predict unseen viewpoints, providing a rich geometric supervision signal only
from posed images, which are abundantly available across videos, robotics platforms, and structure-
from-motion pipelines. However, representations produced from feed-forward NVS methods transfer
poorly to downstream geometric tasks. We argue this poor transfer is not a signal failure, but rather
an architectural failure, driven by two identifiable and independently addressable choices that dilute
signal.
First, spatially expressive decoders tend to absorb the representational load of the task. When a
decoder has sufficient capacity to reconstruct a target view from any encoder output, the encoder is
not pressured to maintain structured, locally discriminative features. While the reconstruction task is
solved, the latent representations are not geometrically grounded. Second, pixel-space reconstruction
targets require modeling high-frequency aliasing statistics that are weakly related to 3D structure and
semantics. Consequently, the reconstruction loss produces gradients that encourage the representation
encoder to preserve fine-grained, image-specific statistics that are not directly informative about the
underlying 3D structure or semantics.

We present SNAP, which addresses both failure axes with two targeted interventions. First, a pose-
conditioned local decoder built on PROPE [8] restricts the decoder’s receptive field via an explicit
BlockNN mask applied to queried-view self-attention. This constraint forces the geometric structure
to reside within the encoder’s scene representation rather than being an effect of the generative
capacity of the decoder. Second, SNAP operates directly on frozen DINOv3 ViT-B patch features to
provide a richer supervision signal than RGB reconstruction. This structured grounding is jointly
aware of appearance, geometry, and semantics and produces higher quality representations.
Our main contributions are:
1. SNAP, a self-supervised multi-view spatial reasoning backbone that builds upon frozen 2D
semantic latent spaces with an asymmetric NVS prediction objective, and latent feature targets to
learn geometrically transferable representations without dense 3D supervision.
2. Mechanistic analyses isolating the independent impact of decoder expressivity and prediction
space on geometric transfer, via receptive field sweeps and prediction space landscape experiments.
3. Empirical evaluation across five tasks matching or exceeding self-supervised baselines and
competitive with geometry-supervised methods that require large-scale annotated 3D data.

Related Work
SNAP sits at the intersection of self-supervised representation learning, novel view synthesis, and
geometric foundation models. Detailed discussions are provided in Appendix A.
Self-Supervised Representation Learning. Masked Image Modeling (MIM) has evolved from pixel-
reconstruction (MAE [9], SimMIM [10]) toward latent-space prediction (I-JEPA [11], V-JEPA [12]).
In multi-view regimes, CroCo [13] and Muskie [14] leverage cross-view completion to learn implicit
3D priors. SNAP extends this trajectory by utilizing frozen DINOv3 [15] features as opposed to
pixel space targets used in Muskie [14], which act as semantically structured latent distillation targets
to provide a cleaner supervision signal for optimization.
Novel View Synthesis (NVS). Feed-forward NVS was established by architectures such as SRT [16]
and RUST [17], recently scaling to large-view models such as LVSM [4], RayZer [6], with contem-
poraneous works LagerNVS [18] and SVSM [7] continuing to extend the paradigm. However, most
of these variants utilize expressive decoders that absorb the representational load of the task, leading
to ungrounded latents. By constraining the decoder’s receptive field via a BlockNN mask, adopting
PROPE [8], and targeting a latent feature space, SNAP ensures its representations prioritize 3D
structural awareness over reconstruction statistics.
Geometry-Supervised Models. Recent foundation models, including DUSt3R [1], MASt3R [2],
VGGT [3], and D4RT [19], achieve state-of-the-art performance but rely on massive 3D-annotated
datasets. Similarly, π3 [20] and Depth Anything 3 [21] provide robust spatial priors via explicit
3D supervision. Conversely, SNAP approaches the geometric transferability of these models while
remaining self-supervised and training on significantly less data and compute.

Method
SNAP operates in two stages, as illustrated in Figure 1. First, a pose-free multi-view encoder
aggregates frozen DINOv3 patch features from N reference views into scene tokens Z via full
cross-view self-attention. Notably, no camera parameters are provided at this stage. Second, a
pose-conditioned patch decoder then queries Z using PROPE-rotated cross-attention to synthesize the
target view’s feature map. The target and context camera intrinsics and extrinsics are fed into PROPE
at this stage. Two design choices jointly force geometric structure into the encoder: a BlockNN
mask restricting the decoder’s spatial receptive field (Sec. 3.3), and a latent reconstruction objective
against frozen DINOv3 teacher features rather than RGB pixels (Sec. 3.4). We train SNAP on
scenes from RealEstate10K [22], DL3DV [23], and Co3Dv2 [24]; comprehensive implementation
and architectural details are deferred to Appendix B

Analysis: Decoder Expressivity and Prediction Target
Our objective is to learn scene representations that transfer across geometric tasks, not simply to
optimize novel-view reconstruction. These goals can diverge: an encoder-decoder NVS model [4, 18]
may reconstruct target views accurately while placing little transferable spatial structure in the multi-
view encoder. We identify two key design factors that influence whether the learned multi-view
representation encodes transferable spatial structure: decoder expressivity and the prediction target.
4.1
Axis 1: Decoder Expressivity Degrades Geometric Transfer
Decoder expressivity. In encoder-decoder NVS models, the decoder’s capacity dictates the balance
between its own learned 2D priors and the encoded conditioning signal. A highly expressive decoder
acts as a strong unconditional prior, utilizing local spatial context to reconstruct or smooth images
independently. While this improves raw synthesis quality, it creates an optimization shortcut: the
strong prior weakens the model’s reliance on the conditioning signal, relieving the encoder of the
need to capture precise, multi-view geometry. Consequently, the downstream utility of the learned
representation degrades. In transformer-based architectures, the strength of this prior is primarily
controlled by the number of decoder layers and the receptive field of target-view self-attention.
Prior NVS models commonly use highly expressive decoders with full self-attention in the recon-
struction of the target-view [4, 18]. We study the influence of decoder expressivity by varying the
receptive field of these self-attention blocks.
Experiment To isolate this factor, we train SNAP variants with varying decoder receptive fields,
RF ∈{1×1, 2×2, 4×4, 8×8, 16×16}. We control the receptive field using a BlockNN mask, which
restricts the decoder’s self-attention to non-overlapping k×k spatial windows. Expanding the mask
increases the amount of target-view spatial context the decoder can aggregate, while leaving the
decoder architecture otherwise unchanged. At the strictest setting, RF = 1×1, spatial communication
between target queries is eliminated, reducing the decoder to a patchwise residual MLP. At the
broadest setting, RF = 16×16, the window spans the full feature map, giving the decoder full
self-attention in target-view, as in [4, 18].
Figure 2 shows that increasing decoder receptive field improves the reconstruction objective but
degrades downstream geometric transfer. As RF grows, the decoder is able to use more target-
view spatial context, leading to lower feature reconstruction error. However, the frozen encoder
representation becomes less useful for geometry: point correspondence error, visual localization, and
pose estimation all degrade. This inverse relationship suggests that reconstruction quality alone is
a poor proxy for representation quality in encoder-decoder NVS models. The qualitative results in
Figure 3 show the same trend. With RF = 1×1, similarity maps are sharply localized around the
corresponding point and remain stable across viewpoints, while with complete self-attention (RF =
16×16), the maps do not always track the ground truth correspondence. These results demonstrate
limiting target-view spatial communication prevents the decoder from absorbing reconstruction priors,
encouraging transferable spatial structure into the multi-view encoder, motivating our patch decoder.
4.2
Axis 2: Prediction Space Determines Representation Quality
The prediction target determines what information the model is pressured to encode. Recent NVS
models typically supervise reconstruction in pixel space [4, 18], which can emphasize low-level
appearance, texture, and illumination rather than transferable geometry. We instead use latent feature
prediction, where earlier ViT layers remain closer to RGB while deeper layers yield high-level
structural abstractions that are robust to non-geometric artifacts like lighting and aliasing.
We sweep both the DINOv3 layer that is used as the featurized input and the layer used as the
prediction target, asking which feature spaces are optimal for novel-view prediction, both at the
input and at the target. We evaluate the resulting encoders on RE10K and DL3DV (val subset),
reporting feature reconstruction error, nearest-neighbor point correspondence (PCK@0.1), patch
retrieval (MRR), and intrinsic dimensionality using the TwoNN estimator [32].
Figure 4 shows that the easiest reconstruction targets do not necessarily produce the best spatial
representations. Lower-layer targets are closer to RGB and are easier to predict, with the best
reconstruction error obtained by the (input: 9, target: 2) pairing. However, downstream geometry
improves when predicting higher-layer DINOv3 features, suggesting that more semantic latent targets
provide a better supervisory signal for transferable spatial structure. The sweep also reveals a trade-

memory footprint than alternative models (Appendix E). By operating on 256×256 images with
a patch size of 16, our encoder processes a sequence length of only 256N + 8 tokens (including
registers). In contrast, VGGT processes 518×518 images at a patch size of 14, resulting in 1369N +5
tokens; it would require more than 12 views for our model to remotely approach the attention cost of
these baselines. Combined with Grouped Query Attention (GQA) configured with 16 heads and 8
groups, SNAP alleviates KV costs, operating efficiently even under limited compute budgets.
5.1
Point Correspondence
Point correspondence tests spatial discrimination at the pixel level: a match is correct only if the
feature at one pixel is more similar to its true correspondent than to all other image pixels. Ground-
truth correspondences for evaluation are supplied by a state-of-the-art dense matcher (RoMa v2 [26])
acting as a correspondence oracle. As Table 1 shows, SNAP outperforms self-supervised baselines
on RealEstate10K across all pixel thresholds. Remarkably, SNAP achieves this higher sub-patch
precision despite processing inputs at half the resolution of VGGT (256×256 vs. 518×518), under-
scoring the spatial sharpness of our learned representations. On DL3DV, where scene complexity is
higher, SNAP performs competitively with VGGT despite utilizing strictly no dense 3D supervision,
approaching its performance within fractional margins across all distance thresholds. NVS baselines
(ERayZer, LVSM, RayZer) consistently underperform, confirming that high reconstruction fidelity
does not imply spatial feature discriminability.

5.3
Relative Pose Estimation
Relative pose estimation uses a direct regression head over the full encoder latent, including register
tokens, with early-stage cross-view fusion. On RealEstate10K (Table 2), SNAP achieves high
rotation accuracy among NVS-based methods and is competitive with other self-supervised and NVS
methods on translation accuracy. The result confirms that SNAP’s encoder retains global geometric
reasoning simultaneously with the local discriminative structure demonstrated in Table 1 — the local
decoder bottleneck does not degrade large-scale geometric inference.
5.4
Robot Manipulation Under Viewpoint Shift
Beyond static benchmarks, we evaluate whether SNAP can transfer effectively to online decision-
making. We compare SNAP against a representative suite of baselines, including semantic backbones
(DINOv3, ResNet), geometry-supervised encoders (VGGT), and special-purpose pipelines (Adapt3R
[34]) on robotic manipulation under out-of-distribution viewpoint shifts. We evaluate on three
MimicGen tasks under camera shifts of 0.0 to 1.0 radians using frozen encoder backbones. Despite
not being trained on any robotics-related data, SNAP is competitive across the board.
As Figure 5 shows, semantic encoders (DINOv3, ResNet) collapse rapidly beyond 0.2 radians.
Arguably, 2D contrastive training suppresses the cross-view spatial variations which the policy
requires to infer 3D configuration. Conversely, SNAP’s multi-view latent reconstruction objective
induces strong viewpoint invariance out-of-the-box. Notably, SNAP outperforms its own frozen
DINOv3 teacher, generally outperforms task-specific baseline Adapt3R, and tracks closely with the
fully geometry-supervised VGGT baseline across all tasks and shift magnitudes.

Conclusion
While our current pretraining relies on posed video sequences and static, textured scenes, and our
findings are bound by a fixed compute and data budget that has yet to reveal the full scaling laws of
the architecture, SNAP demonstrates that feed-forward novel view synthesis (NVS) is a legitimate
powerhouse for self-supervised geometric pretraining. Extending this paradigm to dynamic video
remains a critical next step in establishing this approach as a foundation model. More broadly, our
results show that NVS’s rich geometric signal is most available when two architectural pathologies
are corrected: decoders expressive enough to absorb spatial reasoning that should belong to the
encoder, and pixel-space targets that contaminate gradients with low-level statistics.
Restricting decoder receptive field via a BlockNN mask biases spatial reasoning back into the scene
tokens; replacing pixel targets with last-layer DINOv3 features further redirects optimization toward a
structured, geometry-privileged manifold. The resulting backbone rivals geometry-supervised models
across five tasks without dense 3D annotations, and transfers robustly to robot manipulation, where
viewpoint shifts break standard 2D semantic models. Moreover Appendix D suggests that these
improvements are not restricted to a single teacher, improving correspondence among DINOv3, SAM,
and MAE alike. Most strikingly, SNAP demonstrates NVS’s geometric signal is best leveraged
by restricting decoder capacity, not by optimizing reconstruction, underscoring that representation
quality does not always correspond with reconstruction fidelity.

Supplementary Material
A
Related Work
A.1
Feed-forward novel-view synthesis
Novel-view synthesis has traditionally been addressed through per-scene optimization of explicit
or implicit 3D representations, including neural radiance fields and 3D Gaussian splatting [35–37].
These methods produce high-quality renderings, but require optimizing a new scene representation
at test time. A parallel line of work instead relies on reconstruction priors learned from data, using
feed-forward models for generalizable view synthesis. Early examples condition radiance fields or
image-based rendering networks on sparse input views [38–40], while recent methods directly predict
feed-forward 3D Gaussian scene representations [41–43]. More recently, large transformer-based
NVS models have moved further away from 3D representations, synthesizing target views directly
from learned multi-view representations or scene tokens [4, 6, 5, 44, 18, 7]. These models are
typically evaluated by image reconstruction quality, but their learned intermediate representations
have also been proposed as general-purpose multi-view scene representations. Our work investigates
aspects of NVS pretraining to improve the learned representation for downstream tasks, rather than
only for reconstructing novel views.
A.2
Multi-view representation learning
Beyond novel-view synthesis, several works learn multi-view representations explicitly for geometric
reasoning. CroCo introduced cross-view completion as a self-supervised pretraining objective for
two-view 3D vision [13]. This representation was later used as the foundation for DUSt3R and
MASt3R, which used pointmap prediction to solve many diverse tasks [1, 2]. Recent multi-view
generalist models such as VGGT, CUT3R, π3, MapAnything, D4RT, and Flow3R further extend
this direction, predicting camera parameters, depth, pointmaps, tracks, or dynamic scene structure
from arbitrary image collections [3, 45, 20, 46, 19, 47]. Many of the strongest geometric foundation
models rely on explicit 3D supervision, such as depth, pointmaps, camera poses, or tracks, which
limits the scale and diversity of usable training data. Self-supervised alternatives such as Muskie and
MuM [14, 48] revisit masked modeling in the multi-view setting. Our work is complementary: rather
than using masked reconstruction as the pretext task, we study novel-view prediction as a scalable
self-supervised signal, and identify how decoder expressivity and prediction target determine whether
NVS produces transferable spatial representations.
A.3
3D awareness in video and multi-view models
Recent work studies whether pretrained 2D, video, and multi-view foundation models encode
useful 3D structure. [49] probes frozen 2D visual foundation models for single-view geometry
and cross-view surface consistency, finding that some self-supervised image features encode non-
trivial 3D information. Feat2GS [50] further probes visual foundation models by reading out 3D
Gaussian attributes from frozen features, using novel-view synthesis to evaluate geometry and texture
awareness. VidFM3D [51] shows that video foundation model features also contain 3D-aware
information extractable with lightweight geometry heads. Our work asks a complementary question:
what makes NVS pretraining produce transferable spatial representations? Rather than only probing
for 3D information after pretraining, we isolate two design factors that shape the learned encoder
representation: decoder expressivity and the prediction target. We also evaluate our representation on
a range of geometric and robotics tasks.
Among these, the architectures closest to ours are SVSM [7], LagerNVS [18], and D4RT [19], all
concurrent works with distinct objectives. SVSM and LagerNVS both follow prior work in pairing
the scene representation with an expressive decoder, which is the main architectural difference from
our approach; as we show in Sec.
4.1, this design choice likely limits how well their learned
representations transfer to downstream multi-view tasks. Within this pair, SVSM focuses on the data
scale required for pretraining, while LagerNVS instead focuses on distilling priors from geometry-
pretrained models such as VGGT. D4RT, in contrast, adopts a lightweight cross-attention decoder
similar to ours, but trains it with supervised geometry targets, whereas we rely on a purely self-
supervised NVS objective.

B
Implementation Details
B.1
Pretraining Setup
Datasets and Splits. We train SNAP on a large-scale mixture of multiview video datasets totaling
approximately 117,000 sequences. This training set consists of the RealEstate10K training partition
(∼67,000 sequences), DL3DV-10K (∼10,000 sequences), and Co3Dv2 (∼40,000 sequences). For
the DL3DV and Co3Dv2 datasets, we hold out 20% of the available sequences for validation, and we
use the preprovided test split for validation on RE10K. During training, we randomly sample up to
N = 6 reference views and one target view per sequence. All images are center-cropped and resized
to 256 × 256 pixels. We use 16 layers of self attention for the multi-view encoder with embedding
dimension of 1024 and 8 blocks of alternating PROPE [8] cross-attention and masked self attention.
We leverage grouped-query attention [52] operating with 16 query heads and 8 KV-groups.
Optimization. Training was conducted for 40,000 steps using an effective batch size of 256 across
multiple classes of GPU hardware using Distributed Data Parallel (DDP). We employ the AdamW
optimizer with a peak learning rate of 1 × 10−4 and a weight decay of 0.02. The learning rate follows
a linear warmup for the first 2,000 steps, followed by a cosine annealing schedule that decays toward
zero. We apply gradient clipping with a maximum norm of 0.5 to maintain training stability.
Exponential Moving Average (EMA). To stabilize the learned representations, we maintain an
Exponential Moving Average of all trainable parameters with a decay rate of 0.999. The EMA scope
encompasses every component of the architecture, including the pose-free multi-view encoder and
the pose-conditioned local decoder, which serves as the latent feature reconstruction predictor. All
empirical evaluations and downstream geometric probes reported in the main text utilize these EMA
weights.
B.2
Point Correspondence
Label Generation. Training labels are generated using image pairs from the train splits of RealEstate
10K, DL3DV-10K, and Hypersim. We employ two distinct strategies depending on the dataset:
• RE10K and DL3DV: We derive pseudo-ground-truth correspondences from a frozen RoMa v2
matcher. To ensure high-quality supervision, we filter for image pairs containing at least τmin = 8
confident matches.
• Hypersim: We compute exact ground-truth correspondences via cross-projection using provided
depth maps and camera poses.
Architecture. The point correspondence head employs a three-stage mechanism: (1) symmetric
cross-attention layers that map the encoder’s latent tokens to a canonical 16 × 16 spatial grid; (2) a
source-view cross-attention block to localize the discriminative feature at query coordinate cA; and
(3) a matching stage that computes angular similarity between the query and target-view features via
L2 normalized dot products.
Specifically, the decode stage (1) consists of 3 cross-attention layers, and the localization stage (2)
consists of 3 cross-attention layers. In the final matching stage (3), independent linear projections
are applied to the queries and keys to prevent gradient interference. The L2 normalized dot product
is scaled by a learnable, log-parameterized temperature scalar (initialized to 1.0) before calculating
the similarity heatmap. A differentiable spatial expectation over the resulting similarity heatmap
produces the final predicted coordinate.
Optimization. The correspondence head is trained for 20,000 steps with an effective batch size of 128.
We utilize the AdamW optimizer with a peak learning rate of 2 × 10−4, a 2000-step linear warmup,
and a cosine annealing schedule. As specified in our evaluation protocol, all views are encoded under
identity camera-to-world poses with pixel-unit intrinsics to isolate the encoder’s intrinsic geometric
awareness from external pose signals. Evaluation occurs on the held-out validation splits on all
datasets.
B.3
Visual Localization
Architecture. The visual localization head is designed to regress an absolute target pose by localizing
an unposed query image against a reference multi-view context. The target pose is parameterized
using 9 parameters: a continuous 6D rotation representation and a 3D translation.

The regression head utilizes alternating transformer layers of self-attention (processing the target
sequence) and cross-attention (reading from the reference context). A learned CLS token is prepended

the tokens through the transformer blocks, the refined CLS token is extracted and passed through a
final multi-layer perceptron to output the 9 pose parameters. To ensure stable early training, the final
linear layer’s biases are initialized to represent an identity transformation.
Optimization. We train this head using the RealEstate 10K and DL3DV train splits, and evaluation
is conducted on their respective held-out validation splits. Optimization uses a peak learning rate
of 2 × 10−5 with a cosine annealing schedule and 2,500 steps of linear warmup. The network
minimizes a 9D pose regression loss using a SmoothL1 penalty on both the predicted rotation Rpred
and translation tpred.
B.4
Pose Estimation
Architecture. The relative pose estimation head predicts the pose sequence for all N input views
simultaneously in a single forward pass. Similar to the visual localization head, this module uses
transformer layers alternating between self-attention and cross-attention. It queries the multi-view
context using N learned view queries (one corresponding to each view) equipped with 1D RoPE.
The output tokens are processed by a regression MLP (Linear →GELU →Linear) to predict the 9D
pose (6D rotation and 3D translation) for each respective view. Per-view validity indicators mask out
padded entries during the loss computation.
Optimization. The sequence head is trained on the RealEstate 10K and DL3DV train splits and
evaluated on their held-out validation splits. Training utilizes a peak learning rate of 2 × 10−5,
governed by a cosine annealing schedule with 2,500 warmup steps. The model is optimized using a
9D pose regression loss based on the Smooth-L1 error of the rotation and translation predictions.

Robot Manipulation

Tasks. – We evaluate on three MimicGen manipulation tasks: STACK THREE-D1, SQUARE-D1, and

size 128, and gradient clipping at 100 for 350 epochs. Models use embedding dimension de=512,
chunk size 8, and action horizon 4. Results are reported over 400 rollouts per task per policy.
Image Resolution and Augmentation. – We use a common training resolution of 256×256 for
DINOv3, SNAP. For VGGT and Muskie we used a resolution of 224×224. ResNet and Adapt3R are
trained at 128×128 resolution (following a similar spirit as Adapt3r’s training resolution). The same
lightweight augmentation is applied to every encoder: per-batch color jitter (brightness, contrast,
saturation, hue) followed by a random translation crop of ±8 pixels.

D.2
Comparison with V-JEPA
SNAP and V-JEPA share the use of feature-space prediction rather than direct pixel reconstruction,
but they differ fundamentally in the type of representation they are designed to learn. V-JEPA [12] is
a video representation learner that predicts latent features across video observations, while V-JEPA
2.1 [58] extends this paradigm toward dense video features. In contrast, SNAP is explicitly a multi-
view encoder: it learns a shared scene representation from multiple posed images and trains that
representation to predict features at arbitrary target viewpoints. Thus, the comparison separates the
benefit of feature-space prediction from the additional geometric constraint introduced by explicit
multi-view representation learning.

latent prediction between video observations, whereas SNAP learns a shared scene representation
from multiple posed images and requires that representation to support feature prediction across
viewpoints. The stronger correspondence performance of SNAP across Hypersim, RealEstate10K,
and DL3DV indicates that this explicit multi-view geometric constraint provides a distinct advantage
beyond feature-space prediction alone.
E
Inference Speed
We evaluate the inference efficiency of SNAP against Muskie [14] and VGGT [3] to characterize
the computational overhead of our architecture. As shown in Table 9, SNAP demonstrates superior
scalability and memory efficiency as the number of input views (V ) increases.
Memory and Parameter Efficiency. – SNAP is significantly more memory-efficient than both
baselines. At a high-resolution regime of V = 100, SNAP requires only 1.03 GB of peak GPU
memory, representing a 40.5% reduction compared to Muskie (1.73 GB) and a 69.8% reduction
compared to VGGT (3.41 GB). This efficiency is underpinned by SNAP’s compact parameterization;
with only 233.38M parameters including the DINOv3 backbone, SNAP is approximately 1.3×
smaller than Muskie and 3.9× smaller than the geometry-grounded VGGT.
Temporal Performance. – While SNAP consistently outperforms VGGT, achieving a 2.9× speedup
at V = 100, it is roughly 36% slower than Muskie in the high-view regime (0.245s vs. 0.180s).
We attribute this latency gap to Muskie’s architecture, which utilizes fewer multi-view self-attention
blocks compared to our design. However, for practical deployment, SNAP offers a more accessible
memory profile: at V = 100, SNAP requires only 1.03 GB of VRAM, whereas Muskie consumes
67.9% more memory. For practitioners working with limited GPU memory or ultra-dense view
counts, SNAP provides a viable alternative that prioritizes a smaller memory footprint over raw
inference throughput. Notably, SNAP provides a different tradeoff of performance, speed, and
memory.

G
Landscape Plots
We expand the sweep methodology from Section 4.2 to evaluate a broader set of metrics: mAP/MRR
(reflecting bag-of-words correspondence retrieval), cycle consistency, and variance ratio (the ratio
of within-scene to across-scene variance, indicating how well features cluster by scene) (Figure 8).
Interestingly, we observe a distinct trade-off. While pairing mid-layer inputs with last-layer prediction
targets yields higher raw correspondence, this configuration results in lower cycle consistency
compared to initializing with the last layer of DINO. This performance valley for mid-layer inputs
is consistently prominent across mAP/MRR, cycle consistency, and the variance ratio. Ultimately,
this highlights that optimizing for raw local correspondence does not necessarily yield the most
structurally robust, 3D-grounded representations, justifying our use of deeper semantic layers to
enforce global multi-view consistency.

Qualitative Feature Visualizations
To qualitatively assess what these models learn, we visualize their high-dimensional feature maps
projected into RGB space. As illustrated in Figure 9, standard 2D priors like DINO group scenes
into flat, semantic regions but lack fine-grained 3D geometric structure. In contrast, SNAP clearly
learns a representation distinct from its DINOv3 teacher: it introduces smooth, continuous spatial
gradients across physical surfaces (e.g., ground planes), indicating an emergent understanding of 3D
geometry while preserving fine-grained, discriminative object boundaries. Alternative multi-view
baselines fail to achieve this geometric abstraction. Muskie heavily encodes raw appearance and
texture rather than underlying structure, resulting in representations that simply mimic the RGB input.
Meanwhile, LagerNVS suffers from severe, high-frequency grid artifacts, disrupting the continuous
spatial coherence required for robust multi-view reasoning along with feature anisotropy.
