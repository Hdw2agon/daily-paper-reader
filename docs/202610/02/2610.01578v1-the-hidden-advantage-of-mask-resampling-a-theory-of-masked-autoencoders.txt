# The Hidden Advantage of Mask Resampling: A Theory of Masked Autoencoders 

Jorge Medina Moreira †

, Lorenzo Bardone, Lenka Zdeborová 

Statistical Physics of Computation Laboratory, École Polytechnique Fédérale de Lausanne (EPFL), CH-1015 Lausanne, Switzerland 

Abstract 

Why can masked prediction learn useful representations that unmasked reconstruction misses? We study this question in a high-dimensional model of a masked autoencoder (MAE) trained on data with shared latent structure and heterogeneous noise. We prove that masked linear reconstruction can recover the latent feature at linear sample complexity in regimes where unmasked linear reconstruction, equivalent to PCA, fails. The analysis also quantifies the statistical advantage of mask resampling, an established ingredient of masked pretraining. By introducing a fixed collection of K masks per sample, we characterize its effect on feature recovery and downstream performance, identifying regimes where greater mask diversity lowers sample complexity. Guided by this prediction, we find that random cropping and flipping in standard image-training pipelines can obscure the advantage of mask resampling by renewing the prediction task even when the patch mask is fixed. Removing these transformations reveals a downstream advantage for dynamic over static masking in CNN autoencoders and vision transformers. A complementary BERT pilot finds benefits from greater mask diversity on downstream language tasks. Our results separate the benefit of the masked prediction objective from that of mask diversity, and show how a tractable theory can guide experiments that uncover advantages hidden by standard training practices. Code available at: https://github.com/SPOC-group/mask-resampling 

# 1 Introduction 

Why can predicting missing parts of an input produce more useful representations than reconstructing the entire input? Building on denoising autoencoders [1], Pathak et al. [2] predicted missing image regions from visible context and obtained more transferable representations than a standard autoencoder trained without input masking, a paradigm that now underlies masked language and image modeling [3–6]. Masked learning therefore couples two ingredients: a prediction objective that specifies what structure is rewarded, and an augmentation procedure that controls how many distinct prediction tasks a given sample ever presents. Despite its empirical success, our theoretical understanding of how these ingredients shape learned representations remains incomplete. We develop a high-dimensional theory that separates their effects and connects them to latent-feature recovery and downstream performance. Motivated by the observation that unmasked, i.e., the standard autoencoder, reconstruction 1 can favor high-variance nuisance directions over downstream-relevant structure [7], we consider a data model with a shared latent direction and heterogeneous coordinate-wise noise. The latent direction carries the downstream signal, while heterogeneity allows nuisance variance to dominate the leading principal component. Masking changes this competition by requiring hidden coordinates to be predicted from the visible ones, rather than rewarded for their own variance. We prove that a linear masked autoencoder recovers the latent direction with n = O(d)

samples where unmasked linear reconstruction, equivalent to PCA, fails (Sec. 3). Analyzing this model clearly reveals a second notable effect: the number of distinct masks available per sample controls how many underlying training samples are needed for latent-feature recovery. dynamic masking, commonly used in practice [4, 8], in which the mask is independently resampled each time a sample is used during pretraining. More generally, assigning each sample K independent masks and reusing them thereafter interpolates between these two regimes at fixed number of underlying samples. This allows us to isolate mask diversity from data diversity. Our analysis predicts that increasing K lowers the recovery threshold, with corresponding improvements in downstream performance in the settings studied. This prediction prompted us to revisit how the contribution of mask diversity is assessed empirically. In language modeling, dynamic masking has been reported to provide only modest gains over reusing a fixed collection of masks [8], but their static baseline already uses K = 10 pre-generated masks per sequence rather than a single fixed mask. Data2Vec 2.0 shows substantial improvements in image classification accuracy by increasing the number of masked views per sample within each training update [9]. Furthermore, in vision, self-supervised image learning commonly uses random cropping, flipping, and color transformations to generate changing views of the same sample [10, 11]. In MAE specifically, standard pretraining combines random cropping and flipping with mask resampling [4]. Under a newly drawn crop, for example, the same patch mask can hide different content from the original image. We therefore hypothesize that stochastic image augmentation adds another source of prediction-task diversity, obscuring the effect of resampling the mask itself. Guided by this hypothesis, we compare static and dynamic masking with and without stochastic cropping and flipping. Without these transformations, dynamic masking improves downstream accuracy in CNN autoencoders on CIFAR-10 and ViT-Base on ImageNet-100 (Fig. 1 (b,c)). In the ViT experiments, reintroducing cropping and flipping substantially narrows the observed static–dynamic gap, mainly because static masking improves (Fig. 5). Finite-K experiments further show progressive improvements as each image is exposed to more masks (Fig. 4). A complementary BERT-Medium pilot finds improvements with increasing mask diversity on GLUE tasks, extending the qualitative evidence beyond vision (Sec. 5). Together, these results distinguish two benefits of masked representation learning: the prediction objective can favor useful correlations over nuisance variance, while renewing the masks can lower the sample complexity required to learn those correlations. By identifying this distinction, the theory motivates a controlled test of its relevance beyond the solvable model. The main contributions of this paper are the following: • Under suitable heterogeneous noise, we prove that a linear masked autoencoder recovers the latent direction for all sufficiently large finite sample complexity α = n/d , whereas unmasked linear reconstruction (PCA) fails for every fixed α > 0. Replica calculations complement this guarantee by predicting the recovery threshold (Sec. 3). • We formulate a tractable high-dimensional model of a two-stage learning protocol: self-supervised learning (SSL) through masked reconstruction, followed by supervised linear probing with a frozen encoder (Sec. 2). For linear and nonlinear autoencoders, we derive replica predictions for feature recovery and downstream 2accuracy as functions of the masking ratio and mask diversity, showing the benefit of resampling the masks (Sec. 4). • Guided by our theoretical predictions, we test the role of mask diversity in vision and language. Dynamic masking improves downstream performance without stochastic cropping and flipping; adding these trans-formations substantially narrows the observed static–dynamic gap in the ViT setting, consistent with overlapping sources of prediction-task diversity. A BERT-Medium pilot provides complementary evidence for the benefit of mask diversity in language modeling (Sec. 5). 

## Further related work 

We outline the broad lines of related work here; App. A provides an expanded comparison with the closest references and clarifies the differences in setting, analytical scope, and contribution. 

Statistical-physics theory of neural networks. Our replica analysis builds on high-dimensional empirical risk minimization [12–14], shallow-autoencoder dynamics [15], and denoising autoencoders [16]. Conde Mendes et al. [17] prove an advantage of nonlinear autoencoders over PCA. Heterogeneous noise has also been studied in supervised learning [18, 19]. 

Theory of masked learning. Existing work connects masked autoencoding to contrastive learning and downstream guarantees [20], and establishes latent identifiability in hierarchical models [21]. Bisulco et al. [22] characterize linear MAEs with full reconstruction from masked inputs, including visible coordinates rather than only hidden targets. Their analysis relates patch size and masking ratio to the spatial correlations learned. Dong et al. [23] analyze decoupled coordinate predictors through rigorous ridgeless-regression risk formulas, accounting for both corrupted covariates and the samples supplying a selected prediction target. Single-coordinate prediction also connects to factored attention [24]. Wortsman Zurich et al. [25] characterize a spiked-model recovery transition in the joint matrix of single-coordinate ridge predictors and establish prediction-risk advantages over PCA in structured covariance settings. Cabannes et al. [26] distinguish sample variability from augmentation variability in SSL generalization bounds; additional views can reduce the latter without removing the former. 

Spiked models and heteroskedastic noise. Our linear baseline connects to spiked covariance models [27] and PCA recovery transitions [28, 29]. Hong et al. [30] characterize PCA under heteroskedastic noise. Spectral methods addressing heteroskedasticity include weighted PCA [31], diagonal correction through HeteroPCA [32], and optimal estimators for doubly heteroskedastic noise [33]. We study how masking changes the reconstruction objective to promote recovery, without supplying the coordinate variances or their distribution.

# 3 A provable advantage of masked reconstruction 

We first isolate the effect of the prediction objective. Unmasked autoencoders learn a compressed representation by reconstructing the full input through a low-dimensional bottleneck. The bottleneck forces the learner to select which variations in the data to preserve, with reconstruction error providing the criterion for this selection. Unmasked reconstruction is therefore a natural baseline for asking whether the features most useful for reconstructing the input also capture its shared latent structure.

Having established why the masked objective can favor useful structure, we next ask how its performance changes when each sample supplies only a finite collection of masks.

Mask diversity can change the learning mechanism. Beyond the weak-signal expansion, replicas reveal a qualitative difference between static and dynamic masking. In the linear case, at the weak recovery transition, static masking does not preferentially use cleaner coordinates to identify the feature: its threshold reflects noise heterogeneity only through the aggregate moment κ2. Dynamic masking can instead find and favor cleaner coordinates as recovery begins, implicitly learning which coordinates provide more reliable information. At suitable masking ratios, this adaptation provides an additional benefit not captured by the leading weak-signal law (7). App. D.4 derives this distinction and compares noise distributions with identical static but different dynamic thresholds. 

Interaction with the masking ratio. The finite-K penalty in (7) is smallest at balanced masking and grows towards either endpoint. Near these extremes, each view offers relatively few visible or hidden coordinates, and a small collection of masks provides few distinct prediction tasks. Increasing K reduces this penalty. The shallow-model sweep in Fig. 4(a) illustrates the associated improvement in accuracy and the broader range of useful masking ratios as K increases. At finite signal strength, the best downstream masking ratio also depends on the activation and the learned representation’s noise, so it need not minimize the leading threshold alone.

# 6 Discussion 

Our analysis separates two benefits of masked learning: masked prediction can favor shared structure over nuisance variance, while mask resampling can reduce the sample complexity required to recover that structure. We establish the first benefit rigorously in the linear model and quantify the second through replica calculations that agree with simulations. The CNN and ViT experiments, together with the BERT pilot, support the relevance of mask diversity across architectures and data modalities. This perspective connects masked reconstruction to theories of data augmentation. Analyses based on transformation groups explain statistical gains through invariance and averaging [38, 39]; in joint-embedding self-supervised learning, multiple views can reduce augmentation variability while retaining the variability associated with the original observations [26]. Our analysis gives a complementary account for masked reconstruction: changing K preserves both the underlying dataset and the distribution of individual masks, yet changes the predicted recovery threshold. The weak-signal law quantifies the additional sample requirement at finite K and the diminishing gains from increasing mask diversity. The ViT comparison suggests that geometric augmentation can partly compensate for mask reuse. Apredictive theory of augmentation should therefore explain when geometric transformations and mask resampling offer complementary benefits, and when one reduces the benefit of the other. Our replica analysis also identifies a qualitative change in learning: dynamic masking can exploit cleaner coordinates at the recovery transition, while static masking lacks this adaptation. Whether resampling similarly changes which information is learned in larger models remains an open question. Better representations need not require more data; they can come from a greater diversity of prediction tasks on the same observations.

The main text outlines the broad lines of related work. Here we expand on the closest references to clarify both their settings and what their analyses establish. We compare data models, prediction objectives, and masking protocols, as well as the observables and guarantees studied. These distinctions locate our contributions: recovery under heterogeneous noise for the exact mask-averaged objective, and replica predictions for mask diversity and downstream performance. 

Reconstruction and useful features. The reconstruction–perception mismatch identified by Balestriero and LeCun [7] motivates our separation: a direction can explain substantial input variance without carrying useful latent information. Their closed-form linear denoising analysis shows how corruption, including masking, can improve this alignment. In our spiked model, coordinate-dependent noise makes the obstruction explicit, and hidden-target prediction admits a recovery guarantee at linear sample complexity: every global minimizer of the exact mask-averaged linear objective weakly recovers at linear sample complexity while ordinary PCA fails (Theorem 3.1). 

Full reconstruction from masked inputs. The linear MAE analyzed by Bisulco et al. [22] has untied encoder and decoder matrices, whereas our bottleneck uses tied weights. More crucially, their mask-averaged analytical loss reconstructs all coordinates from masked inputs, including visible coordinates; ours scores only hidden targets. The visible-coordinate rewards retain a contribution from reproducing input variance, changing both the loss landscape and its masking-ratio dependence. Their characterization of critical points and of the spatial correlations selected by patch size and masking ratio is therefore closely relevant, but its spectral solution does not directly solve our objective. Our statistical analysis follows how the structure favored by hidden-target prediction translates into recovery at finite sample size and under finite mask reuse. These are different objectives and constraints, rather than a general performance ordering. 

Single-token prediction and factored attention. Rende et al. [24] connect factored attention to generalized Potts conditionals and use replicas to predict generalization in a Gaussian relaxation. There, predicting one coordinate from all the others probes a full-rank random precision matrix. This differs from our low-rank signal with diagonal heterogeneous noise both in correlation structure and in the number of targets per mask: a positive limiting fraction is hidden in our model. The shared tied bottleneck couples these prediction tasks, so its alignment with the planted feature, and the downstream utility of that alignment, become explicit observables as the number of reused masks varies. 

The joint matrix of single-coordinate predictors. Single-coordinate prediction can itself reveal latent structure: Wortsman Zurich et al. [25] analyze the joint matrix of self-supervised ridge predictors, rigorously characterizing its spectrum, prediction risk, and spiked-model BBP recovery transition. They also establish prediction-risk advantages over PCA in structured covariance settings. Coupling an extensive set of hidden targets through a shared tied bottleneck changes the estimator in our work; mask diversity is an additional statistical control. Our analysis follows its effects on recovery and downstream performance via replicas, alongside a rigorous recovery separation under heteroskedastic noise for the exact mask-averaged objective. Nevertheless, it is important to stress that recovering latent structure through the data correlations exploited by masked prediction is already established in their work. 

Masking ratio and a selected prediction target. Dong et al. [23] start from a loss over all hidden coordinates, but decouple their linear predictors and solve a stylized regression for one selected target, y = Xβ + ϵ

in their notation. Their isotropic and spiked covariance assumptions describe the remaining covariates X; the target coefficient and residual noise are specified separately. Consequently, this target need not be statistically representative of the other coordinates of the augmented sample (X, y ). The resulting risk formulas rigorously quantify how masking both corrupts covariates and selects the samples supplying this target. In our common-factor model, every coordinate follows the same generative construction without a designated response, and all hidden targets train the same tied bottleneck; the analysis then follows collective feature recovery. Their fixed-ratio theory motivates randomized masking ratios, with downstream benefits assessed across modalities; our finite-reuse calculation keeps the ratio fixed and links representation alignment to a finite-label probe, with replica rather than rigorous guarantees for the general learning curves. 16 Contrastive connections and downstream guarantees. The connection between masked reconstruction and useful representations is also explicit in Zhang et al. [20]: reconstruction induces alignment on a mask-based augmentation graph, supporting downstream guarantees and an analysis of masking ratio and dimensional collapse. Our recovery thresholds address a more specific statistical question: how reusing a finite collection of masks changes the learned alignment at a fixed sample-to-dimension ratio. The downstream calculation then tracks the effect of that alignment on a probe trained with finitely many labels. 

Identifiability in hierarchical models. Under the hierarchical generative and invertibility assumptions of Kong et al. [21], masking ratio and patch size determine which latent variables are shared between visible and hidden regions and can be identified. In our additive-noise model, the relevant observable is partial alignment with a planted direction at finite sample-to-dimension ratio. The recovery transition and its dependence on mask diversity concern statistical estimation under these different data and encoder assumptions, rather than an alternative identifiability statement for their hierarchy. 

Nonlinear recovery beyond PCA. Conde Mendes et al. [17] show that nonlinear autoencoders recover higher-order structure invisible to PCA, and that reconstruction loss can misalign with representation quality. Our signal is covariance-visible, but heterogeneous variances obstruct ordinary PCA. Hidden-target prediction changes which covariance structure the objective rewards, yielding a separation that does not require nonlinearity. Complementary high-dimensional work studies how pretraining sample size and representation dimensionality jointly control downstream linear-probe generalization in spiked models [40]. 

Noise-aware spectral alternatives. HeteroPCA [32] and whitening-based methods [41] address the same nuisance of heterogeneous noise through spectral correction. Fig. 8 compares such estimators on the same observations: our separation from ordinary PCA does not imply a separation from all spectral methods. The comparison locates masking among several routes to recovery. It changes which structure the reconstruction objective rewards, without supplying the autoencoder with the coordinate variances or their distribution. 

Bayes-optimal inference and the role of prior information. Our Bayes analysis builds on the low-rank matrix-estimation framework of Lesieur et al. [35, 42], which connects matched priors and output channels to approximate message passing and state evolution. We specialize this framework to coordinate-dependent Gaussian noise to obtain the reference learning curves and AMP updates in App. F. For Gaussian latent priors, the continuous recovery threshold is αBO  

> c

= [ β2Eγ (γ−2)] −1 when the inverse second moment is finite (App. F.8). Its dependence on inverse variances highlights the information available in low-noise coordinates. Zhang and Mondelli [33] provide an independent rigorous foundation: their analysis of doubly heteroskedastic matrix denoising yields this weak-estimation threshold under their Gaussian-prior and compact, positive covariance-support assumptions, and a covariance-aware spectral estimator attains it under their additional technical condition. Thus the Bayes reference connects our reconstruction-based learners to an established optimal-inference benchmark, rather than introducing a new information-theoretic limit. The distinction in available information matters for interpreting the comparison. Our Bayes reference uses the matched latent priors and the realized coordinate variances, whereas the autoencoders receive neither the variances nor their distribution. A remaining gap to Bayes therefore reflects both the reconstruction objective and the additional information used by matched inference. Increasing mask diversity improves how that objective uses the observations; it does not supply the learner with the noise model. The variance-consistency calculation in App. F discusses learning the noise levels from the samples, separately from the known-variance benchmark used here. 

Augmentation multiplicity and finite reuse. Prior work has studied the benefit of drawing multiple augmented views of the same example. Fort et al. [43] show empirically that averaging several freshly sam-pled augmentations per image can improve supervised generalization, while Cabannes et al. [26] analyze the corresponding multi-view setting theoretically and separate variability due to sampling original examples from variability due to sampling their augmentations. These works vary the number of views used for an example while preserving the underlying augmentation distribution. We study a different axis: finite reuse . Each training sample is assigned K masks that are held fixed throughout learning, so changing K changes the finite collection of prediction tasks available to the learner rather than the number of views averaged within an update. Replica 17 predictions quantify how this finite mask diversity changes feature recovery and downstream accuracy, including the weak-signal excess sample requirement in (7). The masks contain no additional information about the planted feature; we show that their benefit comes from how a richer collection of prediction tasks allows the objective to extract information already present in the observed samples. 

Mask resampling and other augmentations. Dynamic masking in RoBERTa [8] and multiple masked views per update in data2vec 2.0 [9] already exploit variation in prediction tasks. The interaction with image augmentation matters when interpreting its benefit: even fixed patch positions expose changing content under stochastic cropping and flipping. Our vision comparisons isolate this interaction, while the BERT pilot provides complementary evidence for finite mask diversity. Group-based accounts of augmentation through invariance and averaging [38, 39] offer another perspective; here the controlled variable is the reuse of prediction tasks from each original observation. These experiments test the qualitative role of mask diversity beyond the solvable model, without assuming that its quantitative recovery law transfers to these architectures and datasets. 18 B Model, notation, and effective output channel

## D.4 Mask diversity changes how the learner uses heterogeneous noise 

The weak-signal law in (7) describes an additional sample requirement caused by mask reuse. At finite signal strength, the replica equations reveal a further distinction: static and dynamic masking can use the coordinate noise profile differently when learning first becomes possible. Static masking has a noise-independent response to an emerging feature at its regular linear recovery transition. Dynamic masking can instead give cleaner coordinates a larger response, lowering the threshold through an adaptation that the static critical solution lacks. Thus the protocols differ not only in how many samples they require, but also in how they extract the feature from those samples. ## H.1 Masking and augmentation protocols 

Mask diversity and training budget. For finite K, each example has K independently drawn masks, fixed throughout pretraining. Static masking is K = 1 ; dynamic masking draws fresh masks on repeated presentations. The number of underlying examples is unchanged. In the shallow experiments, each update averages over the fixed mask collection, or uses one fresh mask for dynamic training (App. H.12). The CNN training schedule is specified below. 

CNN data and evaluation. Fig. 1(b) and Fig. 4(b) use CIFAR-10 [54] with five independent encoder-initialization and data-split seeds. Each seed uses a balanced 40 ,000 -image training subset for both pretraining and labeled frozen probing. Masked pretraining and linear probing use 10 ,000 held-out images for validation. The separate official 10 ,000 -image test set supplies final accuracy. One probe is fitted per encoder; points report mean test accuracy and bars show ±1 SD across the five seeds. The masked comparisons share the split and initialization within each seed. 

CNN architecture and preprocessing. All CNN conditions use the same 854 ,563 -parameter autoencoder. Images are scaled to [0 , 1] and standardized using channel statistics from the pretraining subset. The encoder receives the three masked image channels and a binary mask channel, with hidden pixels set to zero after standardization. Its four convolutions have output widths (32 , 64 , 128 , 128) , kernel sizes (3 , 4, 4, 4) , strides 

(1 , 2, 2, 2) , padding one, and ReLU activations. The decoder uses three stride-two, kernel-four transposed convolutions with widths (128 , 64 , 32) , padding one and ReLU, followed by a kernel-three, padding-one convolution to RGB. There are no skip connections or normalization layers. Global spatial averaging of the encoder’s 

128 × 4 × 4 output gives the frozen representation. Images contain 64 nonoverlapping 4 × 4 patches; each mask hides round(64 ρ) patches chosen uniformly without replacement. 

CNN optimization. For the main CNN sweeps in Figs. 1, 4, 14 and 15, we use AdamW [55] with batch size 512 , learning rate 10 −3, weight decay 10 −4, and gradient norm clipping at 5. A validation-based plateau scheduler halves the learning rate, with patience five epochs and a minimum of 10 −5.For finite K, each epoch traverses the fixed collection of 40 ,000 K image–mask pairs. Dynamic masking draws a fresh mask at every presentation throughout training. We group 32 presentations per image into one epoch for validation and scheduling, corresponding to 2,500 optimizer updates. Under dynamic masking, each image therefore receives 32 E independent mask draws over E epochs. Validation uses one fixed mask per held-out image after each epoch. We retain the checkpoint with the lowest validation reconstruction MSE and stop after 15 epochs without improvement, with a minimum of 20 and a ceiling of 460 epochs. Under these parameters, our setting is always early-stopped and not undertrained. 

CNN linear probes. Probes receive unmasked, unaugmented images and use the frozen 128 -dimensional representation, standardized coordinatewise using the probe training set. A linear ten-class head is fitted with cross-entropy, AdamW, batch size 512 , constant learning rate 10 −2, and zero weight decay. Classifier initialization and minibatch-shuffling seeds are fixed across encoders. The head with lowest validation cross-entropy is retained; training stops after 25 epochs without improvement, with a minimum of 20 and a ceiling of 200 epochs. All reported masked-model probes satisfy this stopping criterion. The unmasked checkpoint sweep uses the same probe settings with a 300 -epoch ceiling (App. H.8).

## H.9 Mask renewal and image augmentation in CNNs 

Fig. 14 complements Fig. 5 using the CNN autoencoder on CIFAR-10 from Fig. 1(b). We compare static and dynamic masking with and without stochastic cropping and flipping, averaging over five seeds. Within each seed, the four conditions share the data split and initial autoencoder weights. The architecture, optimizer settings, validation-based checkpoint selection and stopping rule, and frozen linear-probing protocol follow App. H.1. Cropping and flipping substantially improve static masking across the sweep, bringing its mean accuracy close to dynamic masking at lower masking ratios. At larger masking ratios, dynamic masking retains an advantage even with augmentation. The CNN comparison therefore supports the same interpretation as the ViT experiment: geometric transformations can partly compensate for mask reuse by changing the content to be predicted. The remaining gap shows that the extent of this compensation depends on the masking ratio and experimental setting.
