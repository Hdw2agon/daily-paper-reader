Abstract 

Robust tensor completion recovers multidimensional data from partial observations corrupted by sparse gross errors. Existing methods typically promote low rank through tensor nuclear norms, whereas convolutional lifting enforces low rank on a matrix of translated data copies, capturing translation-invariant structure beyond conventional tensor ranks. Constructing these copies requires a boundary condition, and convolutional models conventionally adopt circular continuation for this purpose, in which every shift is a permutation. For a finite nonperiodic tensor, however, circular continuation connects opposite sides of the tensor, creating wrap-around neighborhoods that do not exist in the data. We propose reflective convolution nuclear norm minimization (RCNNM), which replaces circular shifts with endpoint-nonrepeating reflection for robust tensor completion. Reflection breaks the permutation structure of circular lifting, so entries near the boundary occur with nonuniform multiplicities and the lifting obeys the weighted Gram identity A ∗ 

> ref

Aref (X) = D ⊙ X , with D recording these multiplicities. Using this weighted geometry, we show that RCNNM recovers the underlying tensor and the sparse gross errors exactly with high probability under random sampling and sparse corruption. For bounded dense perturbations, the recovery error remains linear in the perturbation level. The same diagonal structure keeps the optimization e ffi cient by giving the two-block ADMM a closed-form entrywise update, while singular-value thresholding is evaluated through a small eigenproblem on the right Gram matrix instead of a full SVD of the lifted matrix. Across color and multispectral image experiments, RCNNM achieves average boundary-PSNR gains of up to 3.26 dB over its circular-lifting counterpart while remaining competitive globally. Because reflection changes the neighborhoods that encounter the finite boundary, the boundary-dominant gain is consistent with the continuation rule, and boundary-restricted metrics are reported in addition to full-image averages. 

Keywords: tensor completion, convolutional low-rankness, reflective boundary condition, nuclear norm minimization 

1. Introduction 

Recovering multidimensional data from incomplete observa-tions is a fundamental problem in signal and image processing. Without additional structure the problem is ill posed, and low rank is a natural prior for data whose intrinsic dimension is much smaller than their ambient dimension. In the matrix case, nuclear norm minimization provides a tractable convex surrogate for rank minimization and admits exact-recovery guarantees under suitable sampling and incoherence conditions [1–3]. Low-rank– sparse matrix recovery, including robust principal component analysis and recovery from corrupted or erased entries, explicitly models sparse gross errors in addition to low rank [4–6]. Tensor representations preserve multiway structure across modes with-out fixing a particular matrix unfolding [7]. Tensor completion and robust tensor recovery exploit this structure to reconstruct missing and corrupted multidimensional data [8–14]. Formally, let X = Rm1×···× md be the space of order-d tensors with index set I = Qda=1{1, . . . , ma}, let Ω ⊆ I be the set of ob-served indices, and let PΩ be the coordinate projection onto Ω.

Since such rank functions are nonconvex and their direct minimization is gener-ally intractable, practical methods replace them with tractable surrogates, and the choice of rank notion and surrogate distin-guishes the tensor completion models discussed below. Unlike the matrix case, tensor completion has no unique no-tion of rank, and low-rank structure on a tensor can be character-ized through di ff erent tensor decompositions, including Tucker and CP decompositions [7] and the tensor SVD [15]. Each of these decompositions induces a di ff erent surrogate for the rank term in (1), and the completion models built on them all impose low rank directly on the tensor X. Imposing low rank on the tensor itself is natural for globally low-rank data, but translation-invariant structure, such as a pattern that repeats across the tensor, is captured by low rank only after translated copies are aligned. None of them translates the tensor, so no boundary condition is involved. The boundary condition becomes decisive once the tensor is mapped into a lifted matrix, because translating a finite tensor requires choosing how it continues beyond its boundary. arXiv:2610.04979v1 [cs.CV] 4 Oct 2026 That choice determines the lifting operator, its geometry, and the low-rank structure the model captures. The tensor comple-tion models induced by these decompositions therefore do not address the boundary issues created when low rank is imposed on a matrix of translated copies. Lifting-based models pursue this alternative, imposing low rank on a larger structured matrix derived from the tensor X

rather than on X itself. The lifted matrix can take a Hankel, Toeplitz, patch, or convolutional form, and the low rank of each encodes a di ff erent kind of structure. Annihilation-based Hankel lifting has proven e ff ective for image inpainting [16]. Low-rank models built from overlapping patches likewise come with recovery guarantees [17]. The last of these constructions is convolutional lifting, which is the focus of the rest of this paper. It stacks translated copies of the tensor as columns of a matrix [18]. Let ka ≥ 1 be the kernel length along mode a,and collect the kernel o ff sets in K = Qda=1{0, . . . , ka − 1}, so that 

K = |K| = Qda=1 ka is the number of shifts. Given a family of translation operators {Tt : t ∈ K} mapping X into itself, and an enumeration t(1) , . . . , t(K) of K, the lifted matrix is 

AT (X) = [vec( Tt(1) (X)) , . . . , vec( Tt(K) (X))] ∈ RN×K , (2) where vec stacks the entries of a tensor into a column vector and 

N = Qda=1 ma is the number of entries of X. The rank of the lifted matrix in (2) defines a convolutional rank for the tensor X. A small convolutional rank means that many convolution kernels annihilate the tensor, so the columns of the lifted matrix are strongly linearly dependent. This prior is natural whenever local structure repeats across the data. Periodic data with su ffi ciently sparse spectral structure can yield an exactly low-rank lifted matrix, while locally smooth data can give approximately low-rank lifted matrices with many near-duplicate columns and rows. In the convolutional setting, convolution nuclear norm mini-mization (CNNM) builds the lifted matrix from shifted versions of the tensor X [18]. CNNM was proposed for tensor comple-tion with arbitrary sampling, a problem that covers time-series forecasting as a special case. In many applications, missing en-tries may form coherent structures such as scratches, occlusions, or sensor failures, patterns that are not covered by guarantees derived under uniform random sampling. To handle series with trends and nonseasonal dynamics, CNNM has been extended with a learnable orthonormal transform [19]. Convolution princi-pal component pursuit (CPCP) extends this idea to tensor recov-ery with sparse corruptions [20]. Convolutional low-rankness generalizes Fourier-domain sparsity. For a full kernel, the cir-cular convolution matrix is diagonalized by the discrete Fourier transform, and its nuclear norm is proportional to the ℓ1 norm of the Fourier coe ffi cients, with the proportionality determined by the DFT normalization [18]. Together, these results show that translation-induced low rank captures structure that conventional tensor ranks do not describe directly. The convolutional lifting used in CNNM and related models is the special case of (2) in which the translation operators are circular shifts. Circular continuation suits periodic tensors, but for a finite nonperiodic tensor it connects opposite sides of the tensor, creating wrap-around neighborhoods that do not exist in the data. Because the continuation rule fixes which entries are treated as adjacent within each translated copy, the low-rank model must fit a periodicity absent from the observed data, which can bias the recovered entries near the boundary. Reflective boundary conditions have long been used in image restoration to avoid such artifacts of periodic continuation [21– 23], since mirror extension preserves the local smoothness of the tensor across the boundary. Reflection has also appeared in tensor models, for instance in cosine-transform-based ten-sor products [24], DCT-based robust completion within the M-product framework [25], and reflective block-convolution tensor decompositions [26]. In these models, however, reflection is inherited from the transform or factorization rather than cho-sen for how it treats the finite boundary. CNNM and its robust CPCP extension both use circular shifts in their convolutional liftings [18, 20]. We instead study how convolutional low-rank lifting and its recovery geometry change when circular shifts are replaced by endpoint-nonrepeating reflection under sparse corruption and missing entries. Unlike those transform-driven constructions, reflection changes the lifting operator itself and hence its Gram geom-etry. When the translation operators are instead reflected shifts, which continue the tensor beyond its boundary by endpoint-nonrepeating reflection, the resulting lifting is denoted Aref .Shifts are then no longer permutations, and the multiplicity of an entry of X depends on its distance to the boundary. This departure from the circular case is captured by the weighted Gram identity

Since the zero o ff set always contributes a column equal to vec( X), the reflective lifting is injective and the induced reflective convo-lution nuclear norm is a genuine norm rather than a seminorm. On this basis, we formulate reflective convolution nuclear norm minimization (RCNNM) for robust tensor completion. The precise optimization model and its special cases are treated in Section 3.2, following the definition of reflective lifting and its induced norm. The main contributions are as follows. 

• We introduce an arbitrary-order endpoint-nonrepeating re-flective convolutional lifting. Its nonuniform multiplicity geometry is captured by the weighted Gram identity (4), which also shows that the induced reflective convolution nuclear norm is a genuine norm. 

• We formulate the RCNNM model and prove deterministic identifiability with strict dual-certificate conditions, high-probability exact recovery under random sampling and sparse corruption, and stability under bounded dense perturbations. 2• We derive a two-block ADMM whose diagonal Gram struc-ture gives a closed-form entrywise update, while singular-value thresholding is evaluated through a small eigenproblem on the right Gram matrix. Experiments on synthetic and real image data show that the improvement over the circular-boundary version is most pronounced near the boundary, where the continuation rule has its direct e ff ect. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the notation and preliminaries. Section 3 defines the reflective lifting and the RCNNM model. Recovery guarantees are developed in Section 4, and the optimization method is presented in Section 5. Section 6 reports the numerical results, followed by the conclusion in Section 7.

6.6. Multispectral Images 

We further evaluate RCNNM on Cloth, Superballs, and Clay from the CAVE database [39]. Each 512 × 512 × 31 scene is resized to 256 × 256, and the first ten spectral bands are retained. We set γ = 0.2, SR ∈ { 0.5, 0.6, 0.7}, and use a [9 , 9, 5] reflective kernel. The three missing patterns are generated as in the RGB experiments. For each degradation setting, λ is selected once on a reference scene and then fixed across the three test scenes. Tables 5 and 6 report mean global and boundary metrics, respectively. Figure 7 gives a representative spatial–spectral comparison on Clay at SR = 0.6 and γ = 0.2, including the recovered spectrum at a selected pixel over the first ten bands. The global results in Table 5 show that RCNNM improves the circular-boundary version by an average of 0 .76, 0 .42, and 0 .38 dB under random, scratch, and grid missing, respectively. The corresponding BPSNR gains in Table 6 are larger: 1 .87, 0 .72, and 0 .82 dB. Under grid missing, RCNNM also improves the circular-boundary version by 0 .0118 in SSIM and 0 .0167 in BSSIM on average. Across all baselines, GTNLN and TTNN yield slightly higher global PSNR at SR = 0.7 for random and scratch missing, respectively; RCNNM nevertheless attains the highest BPSNR and BSSIM in both cases. Under random missing, its BPSNR gain over the circular-boundary version increases from 1 .18 dB at SR = 0.5 to 2 .47 dB at SR = 0.7. The CAVE results therefore reproduce the main trend observed on RGB images: replacing circular continuation by reflection produces modest but consistent global improvements and a larger gain near the spatial boundary. 

7. Conclusion 

We proposed RCNNM, a robust tensor completion framework based on endpoint-nonrepeating reflective convolutional lifting. Reflection changes the Gram geometry from A ∗

> circ

Acirc = KI

to A ∗ 

> ref

Aref = D, which supports both the recovery analysis and the closed-form tensor update. Under random sampling and random-sign sparse corruption, we established exact recovery guarantees and stability to bounded dense perturbations. The convex model is solved by a two-block ADMM, with singular-value thresholding implemented through the smaller right Gram matrix. Experiments on synthetic tensors, BSDS color images, and CAVE multispectral images show that reflective lifting consis-tently outperforms the circular counterpart, with the clearest gains in boundary-restricted metrics. Future work will consider practical parameter selection and nonconvex low-rank regulariza-tion. Replacing the nuclear norm with a tighter nonconvex spec-tral surrogate may better approximate rank and reduce shrinkage bias on dominant singular values, but would require correspond-ing optimization, convergence, and recovery analysis.

Appendix C.5. Scope of the Reflection-Specific Assumptions 

Theorem 4.1 uses two distinct reflection-specific compati-bility requirements in addition to coherence. A positive lower bound on χT excludes lifted perturbations from V⊥ that are nearly aligned with the linear space T, while an upper bound on cref ensures that correcting the pullback mismatch does not exhaust the spectral-norm margin of the dual certificate. Neither condition follows from low rank alone. The preceding propo-sitions relate χT to a subspace angle and multiplicity distortion and bound cref through the reference-lift mismatch. They are su ffi cient geometric conditions for the present proof and are not claimed to be necessary. The parameter μb likewise remains in-stance dependent; the present analysis does not provide a general low-rank-only bound for it. The probabilistic recovery theorem also uses independent Rademacher corruption signs in the sparse-certificate concentra-tion step. This is a condition of the present recovery analysis rather than an input required by the RCNNM optimization model (22).
