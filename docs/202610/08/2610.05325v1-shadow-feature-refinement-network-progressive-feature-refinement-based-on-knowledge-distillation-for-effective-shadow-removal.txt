Abstract 

In the field of deep learning, has seen significant advance-ments; however, shadow removal remains a persistent chal-lenge owing to the variable sizes and colors of shadows influenced by lighting conditions. This study proposes a novel shadow feature refinement network (SFR-Net), which leverages supervised learning, feature refinement loss, and knowledge distillation to enhance shadow removal perfor-mance. A dedicated post-processing algorithm is further in-troduced to restore natural color consistency in the generated shadow-free images. We evaluated our method on two public datasets: the adjusted image shadow triplet dataset (ISTD+) and the shadow removal dataset (SRD), which demonstrate strong generalization capabilities under diverse conditions. On ISTD+, our model achieved a root mean square er-ror (RMSE) of 3.4627 and structural similarity index mea-sure (SSIM) of 0.9382 across the entire image. On SRD, it recorded an RMSE of 4.3781 and an SSIM of 0.9341. These comprehensive results show that our approach per-forms competitively across both shadow and non-shadow regions while setting a promising direction for robust and perceptually natural shadow removal. Code is available at https://github.com/DongHyun99/SFRNet. 

## Introduction 

A shadow forms when light is obstructed by an opaque object, preventing it from reaching a surface. It is a com-plex phenomenon determined by various environmental fac-tors, such as object shape, lighting conditions, and reflec-tions [46, 26, 24]. Shadows are often disruptive in image processing tasks, such as image segmentation and object de-tection [11, 2, 29, 42], prompting several studies to propose new methods for shadow removal [46, 26, 25, 22, 13, 36]. Nonetheless, shadow removal is complex and challenging, for several reasons. First, shadows cause nonuniform lighting in certain regions because of light obstruction. Therefore, re-moving shadows by adjusting the brightness or contrast of an image is challenging [1]. This is because non-uniform light-ing induces changes in the texture and colors of shadow re-gions, resulting in the loss of image information. Second, it is difficult to distinguish shadows from actual objects [45], par-ticularly in complex scenes, making accurate detection even more difficult. Moreover, shadows consist of two regions:

the umbra, where the light source is completely blocked, and the penumbra, where light source is only partially blocked. The size and sharpness of the penumbral region vary de-pending on the intensity and distance of the light source, which makes difficult to define the shadow boundary accu-rately [46]. The challenge is compounded in cases involving overlapping shadows formed by multiple light sources and reflections. The key to shadow removal lies in estimating and restor-ing the colors of shadowed regions based on those of non-shadowed regions. Various methods have been introduced in recent years. Classical shadow removal approaches remove shadows based on prior information regarding the lighting or gradient of an image [34, 15, 30]. However, they suffer from high computational costs and design complexity [13]. Recent advances in deep-learning-based generative models have en-abled significant progress in shadow removal. In particular, supervised learning approaches integrate paired shadow and ground truth (GT) images, thereby allowing the model to learn the differences between the two images [32]. These methods [46, 24, 25, 22, 13, 36, 32, 6, 37, 41, 28, 10] have demonstrated superior performance and accuracy than con-ventional methods. However, several limitations remain. First, constructing diverse and accurate shadow datasets is labor-intensive and time-consuming [25]. In outdoor images captured under sun-light, minute differences often exist in pixel values of the non-shadowed regions across image pairs [20]. Because of the characteristics of shadow removal models that restore shadowed regions through non-shadowed regions, such dif-ferences can pose a significant hindrance to learning. How-ever, it is difficult to obtain images where there is almost no difference between paired images. Consequently, there are limitations in reconstructing images through a simple com-parison of the two images. Second, as previously mentioned, it is difficult to construct a shadow dataset. Therefore, there is limited diversity in the objects and environments that can induce biased learning [20]. The trained model does not flex-ibly generalize to new shadow images, because the shapes and types of objects that create shadows are similar, and the extent of shadows or locations where images can be captured is limited [19]. Image inpainting is the task of restoring masked-out re-gions based on the information gathered from the surround-

Similarly, because the shadow removal task also restores the shadow region to a state where there is no shadow, the two tasks are common in that they both deal with visual imperfections. However, while image inpainting generates entirely new content by utilizing the surrounding informa-tion, shadow removal can use ample information, including objects, textures, and colors, from both the surrounding re-gions and the shadow region. Hence, shadow removal can be seen as a specific form of image inpainting in a narrow sense. Therefore, this study proposes a supervised learning-based shadow removal model, referred to as the Shadow Feature Refinement network (SFR-Net), which uses the image in-painting concept. We incorporate image inpainting technol-ogy and also utilize the knowledge distillation approach to remove shadows. Our hypothesis related to this approach is as follows: In some cases, the model is trained such that the final image generated by removing shadows from the input image is sim-ilar to the GT image of the input image. Further, in other instances, the model is trained such that the features gener-ated during the shadow removal process are similar to the features generated when the GT image passes through the image inpainting network. In these cases, it is possible to achieve a more realis-tic image with shadows removed. Therefore, the SFR-Net is composed of two image generation networks that are derived from the image inpainting network model: guiding network (GI ) and guided network ( GS ). The GS receives shadow im-age ( IS ) and the shadow region of the shadow image, which is shadow mask ( MS ), as input to remove shadows. The GI

is an image generation model with the same structure as the 

GS and utilizes the GT image ( Igt ) and an empty shadow mask ( Mgt ) as input to reconstruct the GT image. In this process, the features generated at each layer play the role of supervising the training of the corresponding layer in the guided network. To verify the effectiveness of the technique we proposed, we conducted extensive experiments using the ISTD+ [25] dataset. The experimental results indicate the SFR-Net yielded a competitive performance, both quantita-tively and qualitatively, compared to recent techniques, in-cluding Shadow Former [22]. The main contributions of this study are as follows. 

• This paper proposes a supervised learning-based shadow removal model that combines image inpainting and knowledge distillation techniques. To achieve this, we present two image-inpaining-based image generation model architectures and loss functions for efficient learn-ing. 

• We trained our model using the correlations between pixels in the non-shadow regions of the shadowed and shadow-removed images. By incorporating this informa-tion, we correct the pixel values for the entire shadow-free image. This post-processing operation can help restore the overall colors of the generated images to closely match those of the original images and enhance shadow removal performance. 

• We conducted extensive experiments using the ISTD+dataset, which is a public dataset, and demon-strated that the proposed SFR-Net can achieve a perfor-mance similar to that of the state-of-the-art methods. 

## Related Works 

Previous studies on shadow removal primarily used physics-based methods [7–9]. Physics-based methods remove shad-ows based on prior environmental information, including lighting and space. Since the inception of deep learning, many shadow-removal techniques based on generative mod-els, such as generative adversarial networks (GANs) [5] and diffusion models [17], have been proposed in recent years [46, 26, 25, 22, 13, 36]. Qu et al. [32] proposed a multi-context model that extracts shadow-matte images. After using a global localization network (G-Net), appearance modeling network (A-Net), and semantic modeling network (S-Net) to extract context, matte images were generated through a 1 × 1

convolution to remove shadows. Cun et al. [6] used a hierar-chical attention aggregation node that utilized an attention mechanism to hierarchically aggregate multiple contexts. They designed two nodes to generate shadow-free images and masks. In addition, they used a shadow-matting GAN to generate a shadow matte to augment and use the shadow image. Le et al. [25] interpreted shadow removal from the perspective of image decomposition. They proposed SP+M Net, which extracts a reliable image based on a physical light-ing model for images (SP-Net) and then generates a shadow matte image (M-Net) by utilizing it. Moreover, in a follow-up study, they introduced SP+M+I Net, which enhanced the overall performance by applying an image inpainting net-work (I-Net) to generate shadow-removed images [26]. They also released the ISTD+ dataset, which was generated by per-forming color restoration preprocessing on the GT to trans-form the nonshadowed regions of the GT images to be highly similar to those of the shadowed images. From a model architectural perspective, recent shadow-removal methods have increasingly adopted transformer- and diffusion-based learning frameworks. HomoFormer [43] em-ployed a homogenized transformer architecture to efficiently capture long-range dependencies in shadowed regions. CR-Former [37] introduced region-aware cross attention between shadow and non-shadow patches and applied RefineNet to re-cover information lost during the shadow removal process. Guo et al. [13] proposed a model that leverages both spa-tial and channel-wise information using shadow interaction and channel attention modules. ShadowGAN-Former [18] reweighted self-attention mechanisms using shadow masks to better model both the global context and local shadow structures within a GAN-transformer hybrid architecture. Simultaneously, diffusion-based approaches have gained attention because of their ability to generate high-quality shadow-free images. DeS3 [22] used a pretrained DINO-ViT [3] to extract features during the reverse diffusion process and incorporated them into the loss function. Similarly, Shad-owDiffusion [14] integrated diffusion models with degrada-tion priors to enhance restoration performance. Mei et al. [31] proposed a latent feature-guided diffusion model that learns shadow-free priors using a two-stage training approach to address the challenge of texture preservation in shadowed re-gions. The main limitation of most previous studies is that they could not address the differences in color between shadowed and GT images. Regarding human perception, the sense of incongruity can be greater when shadows are removed from images with simple colors and textures than when they are removed from complex images. This problem is accentuated by the difference in color between the shadow image and the GT image when the shadow removal model is trained from scratch. This issue may be attributed to both the limitations related to colors and the fact that the boundary of the shad-owed region was unclear when the shadow was removed. Consequently, the shadow was not properly removed, result-ing in a greater sense of incongruity. Therefore, if a shadow removal model is trained without considering these factors, it may generate images in which the boundary between the shadowed and non-shadowed regions is unclear, or there is a loss of color. The proposed approach incorporates feature-level knowl-edge distillation between the shadow image and the GT image to minimize the degradation of the shadow region during the shadow removal process and trains the model to generate shadow-removed images that look more natural and resem-ble GT images. Moreover, our approach can restore images with minimal loss to the non-shadow region of the original shadow images by utilizing a pretrained image inpainting model in the shadow removal process. 

## Methodology 

This section introduces the proposed shadow removal model, referred to as SFR-Net. The SFR-Net utilizes a deep-learning-based generative model to generate a shadow-removed im-age, Isf , using the shadow image IS and binary mask MS

for the shadow region of the shadow image, which are pro-vided as inputs. The shadow mask MS was obtained from the ground-truth mask annotations provided in the ISTD+ dataset during the training and testing phases. For datasets without official mask annotations, such as the shadow removal dataset (SRD), commonly used predicted masks from models such as DHAN [6] were used as input masks. Subsequently, the designed postprocessing was applied to the shadow-removed image to generate the final shadow-removed image Isf -pp ,wherein the colors were restored. Figure 1 shows the struc-ture of the SFR-Net. 

Shadow Feature Refinement Networks 

The SFR-Net is a generative adversarial network (GAN)-based generative model that consists of two image genera-tors and one discriminator. As mentioned previously, the two generators were designed based on the hypothesis that more realistic images can be generated by comparing the differ-ences between the GT and predicted images, as well as the features of the intermediate layers. These two generators are referred to as the guiding network GI and guided network 

GS . The GS uses the shadow image IS and paired mask image MS as inputs to remove shadows. Simultaneously, the pretrained GI takes Igt , which is the GT image for IS , and the empty mask image Mgt as input to reconstruct I′

In this study, we used LaMa [35] for GI and GS among the various image inpainting models because it can learn rich contexts using fast Fourier convolution (FFC) [4]. As shown in Figure 2, the fast Fourier convolution (FFC) module applies a spectral transform to process global and lo-cal features separately and efficiently in parallel. This design enables the network to learn rich contextual representations essential for complex tasks such as shadow removal. The spectral transform is composed of two core components: the Fourier unit (FU) and local Fourier unit (LFU). FU pro-cesses global features by applying a fast Fourier transform (FFT) to project spatial features into the frequency domain, where long-range dependencies and global structures can be captured more effectively. By contrast, LFU focuses on en-hancing the local detail by dividing the feature map into four quadrants, processing each separately with FFT, and fusing them via channel-wise concatenation. It further applies spa-tial shift operations to strengthen the interactions between neighboring regions. These local features are then fused with global features, thereby allowing the model to preserve both broad contex-tual relationships and fine-grained details simultaneously. Figure 2 illustrates this architecture, including the overall structure of the FFC block and the internal workflows of the FU and LFU. The proposed guided network GS is con-structed using an FFC-based architecture. Specifically, the 

GS consists of four DownScale layers based on FFC, two FFC layers, 18 FFCResNetBlocks using skip connections, and four UpScale layers, which are also based on FFC. In addition, the discriminator uses the patch-based discrimina-tor used in the Pix2PixHD model [39]. The shadow-removed image Isf , which is generated by the guided network GS ,is subjected to a post-processing algorithm that restores the overall colors degraded during the shadow removal process and becomes the final shadow-free image Isf -pp . Loss Function 

The SFR-Net uses adversarial loss ( LAdv ) for generator GS

and discriminator D, high receptive field perceptual loss (LHRF P L ) [35], which is a ResNet-based perceptual loss, and discriminator feature matching loss ( Lf m ) [16]. In ad-dition, to improve the image reconstruction performance, SFR-Net uses mix loss ( Lmix ) [44], which combines MS-SSIM [40] loss and L1 loss as the reconstruction loss func-tion. Finally, it uses feature refinement loss ( Lf r ), which uses knowledge distillation between the guided network ( GS ) and guiding network ( GI ) to improve the shadow removal per-formance. Adversarial loss. SFR-Net is a type of GAN that uses an adversarial loss function. It further calculates the loss func-tion separately for the shadowed and non-shadowed regions. The non-shadow region of the image generated by GS of the SFR-Net (it is the inverted mask region and is referred to as 

1 − MS ) should be identical to that of the shadow-free im-age ( Igt ). Hence, the discriminator is trained to classify the generated non-shadowed region as a real image. Therefore, it is preferable for the pixel values in the non-shadow region to remain unchanged. The discriminator is trained to assign high confidence scores to these regions, as they should match the target (i.e., ground-truth) distribution. By contrast, the shadow region is expected to be altered by the generator, and the discriminator is trained to detect these changes accord-ingly. In our patch-based discriminator approach, each image is divided into N spatial patches, and the loss is computed on a per-patch basis. Each patch is supervised differently depending on whether it belongs to the shadow region, as in-dicated by the shadow mask. Specifically, patches within the shadow regions are expected to be modified by the generator and should be distinguished from the ground truth, whereas patches outside the shadow region should be consistent with the original image content. Feature refinement loss. In general, perceptual loss is used to supplement pixel-level losses such as L1 and L2

losses in image translation tasks. It also enables the com-parison of high-level perceptual losses using a pretrained model [23]. Post-processing for Color Restoration 

SFR-Net combines five different loss functions to generate natural shadow-removed images. However, the main limita-tion is that the overall color of the generated images can be altered. This problem occurs because, in most shadow re-moval datasets, the data pair of a shadow image and a GT image exhibits a difference in color between the two im-ages owing to differences in conditions, such as when the image was captured and lighting conditions [20]. As the training of the shadow removal model progresses, it dis-covers and learns changes in shadows as well as changes in colors owing to lighting conditions between the shadow and GT images. Consequently, the overall color of the im-ages was altered. To overcome this problem, Le et al. in-corporated a linear regression algorithm to create an ISTD+ dataset with a reduced color loss [25]. By contrast, we in-corporated a post-processing algorithm to restore the colors of the shadow-removed image to match those of the input image. Here, IS is an image in which shadows are cast on 

Igt . Therefore, based on the assumption that IS and Igt have no color or pixel differences in the non-shadowed region, the shadow-free image Isf should have no color differences in the non-shadowed region. Therefore, we learned a model (e.g., regression) for color differences in the non-shadowed region between IS and Isf and applied it to the entire area of 

Isf to restore the altered colors in Isf . Various methods can be used to determine these color differences. For example, if linear regression analysis is applied, the equation can be represented as follows:

Fur-ther, β0 and β1 denote the intercept and regression coefficient (slope) for the linear regression, respectively. β0 and β1 are applied to all pixels of Isf through linear regression trained for each image via Equation (14) to calculate the final output (Isf -pp ). Here, ⊙ denotes element-wise multiplication. The prediction of each pixel was performed separately for R, G, and B. 

## Experiments 

Experimental Setup 

Datasets. The performance of the proposed method was ver-ified using two publicly available shadow-removal datasets, ISTD+ and SRD. The ISTD+ dataset is a shadow-removal dataset obtained by postprocessing and adjusting the ISTD dataset. This dataset resulted in a slight improvement in the color differences between shadowed and shadow-free im-ages [25]. The ISTD+ dataset is a large-scale shadow-removal dataset consisting of 1,870 triplets, where each triplet is com-posed of a shadow image, a shadow-free image, and a mask image. The resolution of the images was 640 × 480 , and the dataset consists of 1,330 triplets for the training set and 540 triplets for the test set [38]. The SRD dataset comprises 3,088 pairs of shadowed and shadow-free images; however, it does not provide ground-truth mask annotations and has no fixed resolution. To ensure a fair comparison with ISTD+, we resized all the SRD images to 640 ×480 pixels. Moreover, because our method and the baseline models require triplet data, including shadow masks, we adopted the widely used approach of generating masks using the DHAN model [6] for both the training and evaluation of the SRD. 

Metrics. As in previous studies, we quantitatively measured the root mean square error (RMSE) for the GT and shadow-free images that were transformed into the LAB color space. We also measured the peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) and structural similarity index measure (SSIM) for RGB space. Each of these metrics is evaluated for shadowed, non-shadowed, and entire regions. Smaller RMSE values are ideal, whereas higher PSNR and SSIM values indicate a bet-ter image quality. The equations for each evaluation metric are as follows:

Implementation details. The proposed model was devel-oped using PyTorch 2.0 (CUDA 11.8) and PyTorch Lightning 1.2.9, and was trained using an NVIDIA Tesla A100 GPU. SFR-Net utilizes LaMa [35], which possesses state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance, as the base model for knowledge distillation using an image inpainting model. The model used had four downsampling layers, 18 FFC residual blocks, and four upsampling layers. We used the Pix2PixHD-based dis-criminator proposed by Wang et al. [39]. We set the batch size to four and used Adam and Lion optimizers. The learn-ing rate was set to 0.001 for the generator and 0.0001 for the discriminator. The mixing coefficient in Equation (11) was set to γmix = 0 .025 , consistent with Section . The weight λf r , which is multiplied by Lf r , varies for each model. The experimental outcomes indicated that the best performance was achieved when λf r = 100 . Similarly, in Equation (1), we used α = 10 , β = 30 , γ = 100 , δ = 10 , and 

ζ = 0 .001 as the weight values that are multiplied by each loss. These values were selected based on the experiments because they yielded the best performance.

The greater the color difference in the non-shadowed region, the greater is the enhancement effect. Therefore, for practical applications, linear regression in the BGR space provides an excellent tradeoff between performance and ef-ficiency. ## Conclusion 

In this study, we propose a new shadow-removal model called SFR-Net. The SFR-Net learns based on the knowledge dis-tillation of feature maps during the shadow removal process. Specifically, the GT image was input into the pretrained im-age inpainting network, and the features were extracted ac-cordingly. These features were then compared with those of the shadow removal model. Regarding the loss function, we enhanced the shadow removal performance using MS-SSIM, 

L1 loss, and feature refinement loss. We also incorporated a method for color restoration through linear regression-based postprocessing. The experimental results indicated that SFR-Net can effectively reconstruct images for shadow removal. However, the proposed method has several limitations. First, because the model was trained on the ISTD+ and SRD datasets, it may face challenges in generalizing to unseen environments, particularly those with significantly different lighting conditions, object types, noisy backgrounds, or com-plex overlapping shadows. Second, although knowledge dis-tillation is inherently suitable for label-efficient learning, our model still depends on paired data because of the supervised nature of shadow-removal tasks. Third, although effective, the postprocessing step remains separate from the core model and cannot be optimized jointly. To address these limita-tions, future work will explore shadow removal models that incorporate self-supervised or weakly supervised learning schemes to reduce the dependence on paired datasets. We also aimed to develop an end-to-end architecture in which postprocessing for color correction was integrated into the network and optimized jointly, thereby improving both ro-bustness and visual coherence. In addition, we plan to eval-uate the model using more diverse datasets and challeng-ing conditions, including varying lighting, sensor noise, and structural inconsistencies to further validate its generaliza-tion capabilities in real-world scenarios. Finally, although preliminary interpretability is provided through feature and residual visualizations, future studies may explore model at-tribution techniques to improve the transparency and explain-ability of the decision-making process.
