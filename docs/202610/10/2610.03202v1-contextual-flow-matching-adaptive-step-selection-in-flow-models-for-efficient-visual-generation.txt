## Abstract 

Flow Matching enables high-quality visual generation via continuous-time dynam-ics, but inference remains costly due to multiple sequential function evaluations. Existing acceleration methods reduce the number of function evaluations but of-ten introduce additional training overhead, degrade quality, or fail to account for input-dependent variability. We propose C OFLOW , an inference-time method that adaptively selects the step counts each generation based on the prompt features. Our context-aware C OFLOW is trained online with an unsupervised reward that balances inference efficiency and generation fidelity. Our method is plug-and-play, requiring no retraining of the underlying generative model. It generalizes to image and video generation, achieving over 2.5× speedup while preserving perceptual and semantic quality. We further provide a theoretical analysis establishing an O(1 /K )

forward-Euler discretization error bound under standard regularity conditions. 

## 1 Introduction 

Flow Matching (FM) [1] has recently emerged as a powerful paradigm for visual generation [2]. In this framework, a time-dependent vector field governs the evolution of samples, and integrating this dynamics produces high-quality outputs along smooth trajectories. Compared to diffusion-based approaches [3], FM can achieve comparable or superior perceptual fidelity with fewer function evaluations (steps), making it an attractive alternative for both image and video generation. Despite these advantages, inference efficiency remains a central challenge [4, 5, 6]. Generating a single sample requires sequential numerical integration of the learned dynamics, which involves multiple forward passes of the model over time. This inherent serial processing becomes increasingly expensive as model capacity scales and tasks demand higher spatial resolutions or longer temporal horizons, resulting in substantial inference latency in practical deployments. A range of methods have been proposed to reduce inference latency in visual generations. These methods include distillation [4, 5, 7], step truncation [8, 9], and consistency-based training [10, 11, 12]. While these approaches effectively reduce the number of function evaluations, they introduce non-trivial trade-offs, including additional training costs and reliance on teacher models or large-scale datasets. Such training overhead can offset the computational benefits gained at inference time. Several training-free methods have also been proposed that rely on heuristic step-skipping [13, 14, 15] based on intermediate signals. However, these training-free methods require accurate, expensive

The difficulty of a generative task and associated computational requirements can vary substantially. For example, a simple prompt “A photo of a cat" requires less computation than a prompt like “A photo of a cat traveling to the moon in a UFO" (see Fig. 1) due to the need for fine-grained and prolonged trajectory refinement of the generative process. The complexity of prompts can then influence the number of steps. AdaDiff [16] adapts to such input-dependent complexity by using a pre-trained policy that decides the number of steps required to complete the generative task. However, pre-training such a policy is expensive, and the trained policy can be sensitive to distribution shifts at inference time. Our goal in this work is to develop an input-adaptive online strategy that eliminates the need for pre-training. We introduce C OFLOW : Contextual Flow Matching, an inference-time input-adaptive step selection method for Flow Models, thereby reducing inference cost. C OFLOW uses the contextual bandit [17] framework to learn the optimal number of steps for each generation task in an online fashion. It treats prompt-specific features, like semantic embeddings, complexity cues, and prior observations from similar inputs as context, and uses a neural network to predict the number of steps required. Once the number of steps is decided, it executes the corresponding full generation trajectory. The neural network is trained on the fly by optimizing a reward that balances computational cost and trajectory refinement. The reward is motivated by the observation that complex prompts induce larger variations in the velocity field across timesteps (see Fig 1 and 6). COFLOW is fully task-agnostic, adapts to multiple existing solvers (see Appendix A.9), and applies seamlessly to image generation, image editing, and video generation while maintaining high-fidelity outputs. We further provide theoretical grounding by deriving an upper bound on the discretization error induced by suboptimal step selection. Together, these components establish a principled adaptive inference framework that reduces unnecessary computation while preserving generation quality. We summarize the key contributions of this paper as follows: • We propose a novel adaptive inference paradigm that predicts an input-dependent step budget using a neural contextual bandits algorithm, conditioned solely on prompt-specific features. • We design an unsupervised reward mechanism based on trajectory dynamics, leveraging variations in the learned velocity field to balance computational cost and generation fidelity. • We provide a theoretical analysis establishing an O(1 /K ) discretization-error bound, high-lighting the role of input-dependent flow dynamics in adaptive inference. • Experimental results across image and video benchmarks show that our method achieves inference acceleration ( > 2.5×) while maintaining quality and outperforming baselines. 

## 2 Related Work 

Flow Matching (FM) [1, 18, 19, 20] has emerged as a strong alternative to diffusion models [3, 21, 22, 23]. FM achieves high-quality generation enabling applications such as image inversion [20], editing [24], and video synthesis [25, 26]. Recent advances further improves scalability and performance 2through multimodal extensions [27, 28], large-scale flow models such as FLUX [19], and hierarchical approaches such as pyramidal flow matching [29] for video generation. Despite these advances, iterative integration remains a fundamental bottleneck, limiting real-time and interactive deployment. Existing acceleration methods can be broadly categorized into retraining-based and inference-time approaches [30]. Retraining-based methods, including distillation ap-proaches such as InstaFlow [31], LeDiFlow [32], and Diff2Flow [33], compress multi-step generation into one or a few steps by transferring knowledge from pretrained models. Other methods, such as PeRFlow [5], instead modify the training objective to produce straighter transport trajectories. Although effective, these approaches require additional training, large-scale data, or specialized objectives, resulting in substantial computational overhead. Recent works further combine adversarial and consistency objectives to improve high-quality few-step generation [10, 11, 34]. Inference-time methods reduce computation by modifying the sampling procedure. Techniques such as TeaCache [13] and FastFlow [15] skip redundant evaluations using trajectory-based signals, while FlowCast [14] adapts speculative decoding to flow matching models. While effective, these approaches typically rely on heuristic signals or alter trajectories designed for fixed discretization schedules, which can introduce inconsistencies and degrade generation quality. Our setup is closely related to AdaDiff [16], but the solution approach differs in several fundamental aspects. The policy training in AdaDiff requires generating multiple images at various step budgets and assess their quality in each round of training, which require offline-data and introduces overheads. In contrast, our method is fully online and leverages intrinsic model dynamics (trajectory-level signals) as a proxy for generation complexity, thereby avoiding any additional generations and external evaluation. This yields a plug-and-play framework that preserves the consistency of the underlying discretization schdules of flow models resulting in efficient and high-quality visual outputs. 

## 3 Methodology 

We begin with a brief review of Flow Matching (FM) and describe how our approach builds on it to enable adaptive inference. 

3.1 Flow Matching Overview 

Let π0 and π1 denote probability distributions over Rd, corresponding to a simple source distribution and a complex target data distribution, respectively. Flow Matching formulates generation as learning a continuous transformation that maps samples from π0 to π1 via a time-dependent dynamical system. Specifically, a velocity field v : Rd × [0 , 1] → Rd defines the evolution of a sample xt through the ordinary differential equation (ODE) 

dx t

dt = v(xt, t ), x0 ∼ π0, t ∈ [0 , 1] . (1) The velocity field, typically parameterized by a neural network, is trained such that integrating this dynamics transports the initial distribution π0 to π1 at t = 1 . This induces a flow map that traces a continuous trajectory for each sample, ultimately producing data-consistent outputs. 

Inference. Since the learned dynamics generally do not admit closed-form solutions, generating requires numerical integration of the ODE. In practice, simple solvers such as the forward Euler method are commonly used due to their efficiency. The time interval [0 , 1] is discretized into K steps 

{t0 = 0 , t 1, . . . , t K = 1 }, and starting from x0 ∼ π0, the sample is iteratively updated as 

xtk+1 = xtk + ∆ tk · v(xtk , t k), ∆tk = tk+1 − tk. (2) This procedure approximates the continuous trajectory using a sequence of discrete updates, where the number of steps K directly controls the trade-off between computational cost and generation quality. A finer discretization of the interval leads to lower Euler discretization error, denoted as 

E(K) and vice versa. For a given K, the discretization points are fixed beforehand by the schedule of the solver. A key observation motivating our approach is that different conditional inputs can induce trajectories with different levels of variation and, consequently, different sensitivities to temporal discretization. Thus, a fixed step budget may be unnecessarily conservative for some inputs while insufficient for others, motivating an adaptive mechanism that selects the step budget conditioned on the input. 33.2 Algorithm Design 

To accelerate generation, our goal is to determine an input-dependent integration step budget during inference. Crucially, this decision should account for both the global data distribution and input-specific context, as samples from the same distribution can exhibit different trajectory dynamics. It uses a lightweight neural network ( d ∼ 30 K parameters), with the covariance inverse updated incrementally using rank-one Sherman–Morrison updates. The resulting forward, backward, and uncertainty computations are small relative to evaluations of the 6generative flow model, and their latency is included in all reported inference times. ## 4 Experiments 

We evaluate our method across three settings: text-to-image generation, image editing, and text-to-video synthesis. We first describe the datasets, followed by the baselines used for comparison. 

Datasets. For text-to-image generation, we use the GenEval benchmark [35], consisting of 553 

prompts designed to evaluate compositional reasoning. The prompts test capabilities such as object presence , spatial relations , attribute binding , and counting , providing a fine-grained measure of semantic alignment. For image editing, we adopt GEdit [36], which contains 606 real-world editing instructions spanning 

object manipulation , appearance changes , layout modifications , and style transfer . This enables evaluation of both localized and global transformations. For video generation, we use VBench [37] and construct a subset of 80 prompts by uniformly sampling 5 prompts from each of its 16 evaluation dimensions. This ensures balanced coverage of factors such as motion consistency , object persistence ,

camera dynamics , and scene composition .

Baselines. We compare against representative methods spanning full-generation, training-based acceleration, and inference-time approaches. Full Generation performs standard sampling over the complete trajectory without approximation, serving as an upper bound on fidelity, while TeaCache 

[13] improves efficiency by caching intermediate representations across timesteps at the cost of minor approximation error. 

InstaFlow [31] compresses multi-step generation into one or few steps via distillation, achieving large speedups but reduced visual and compositional quality. PeRFlow [5] enforces piecewise-linear velocity trajectories through additional training for stable few-step generation. FlowCast [14] uses speculative decoding to predict future steps in parallel, reducing wall-clock time but increasing NFEs. 

FastFlow [15] skips evaluations using a bandit-based smoothness heuristic, while AdaDiff [16] formulates step selection as reinforcement learning guided by external quality metrics, introducing overhead and potential misalignment with semantic fidelity. In contrast, COFLOW uses a neural contextual bandit to predict an input-adaptive step budget before sampling and executes the full trajectory accordingly, enabling context-aware computation while preserving discretization consistency; results include all samples encountered during exploration. All baselines are evaluated using their official implementations and hyperparameters (see Appendix 12). Table 1 reports results under released settings for all the baselines. We set μ = 1 / max α∈A α

to normalize the computational-cost term relative to the available step budgets. The parameter μ

controls the trade-off between trajectory variation and computational cost; its effect is examined in Figures 3 and 4. All the experiments were performed on a set of 2 NVIDIA A6000 GPUs. 

Models. To demonstrate the versatility of our approach, we evaluate across multiple state-of-the-art models: for image generation, we use BAGEL [28], Flux-Kontext [19], PeRFlow [5] and StableDif-fusion models [38]; for image editing, we use BAGEL and Flux-Kontext; for video generation, we use the HunYuanVideo model [25].

Summary. Across image generation, editing, and video synthesis, C OFLOW consistently reduces inference computation while maintaining competitive output quality. The method selects an input-dependent integration budget and then executes the corresponding solver trajectory without modifying the pretrained generative model. Ablations further show that conditioning the policy on input context improves the observed quality–efficiency trade-off relative to a non-contextual variant (Appendix A.7). A finer-grained analysis further examines the contributions of MiniLM embeddings and handcrafted prompt features. Additional online ablations show that the policy stabilizes over the interaction sequence and is robust across prompt orderings, while uncertainty-aware UCB exploration improves both the surrogate objective and computational efficiency relative to an otherwise identical greedy policy (Appendix A.7). Finally, C OFLOW can be combined with existing inference-time acceleration methods such as FastFlow and FlowCast for additional runtime reductions (Appendix A.4). 

Limitations. While C OFLOW is lightweight and fully online, it requires a brief exploration phase to learn the contextual policy; however, note that all reported results already include exploration samples. Its effectiveness also depends on the expressiveness of the prompt features, which are intentionally designed to be simple and efficient. More expressive features could further improve adaptation quality, but at the cost of additional computational overhead. In practice, both the exploration phase and lightweight feature design stabilize quickly and have minimal impact on overall performance. 

## 5 Conclusion 

We introduced C OFLOW , an online framework for input-adaptive step selection in flow-based generative models. C OFLOW formulates step-budget selection as a neural contextual bandit, using lightweight prompt representations and an intrinsic trajectory-variation surrogate to adapt computation without retraining the underlying generative model. Across image generation, image editing, and video synthesis, C OFLOW substantially reduces inference computation while maintaining competitive semantic and perceptual quality across multiple backbones. We further provide a forward-Euler analysis establishing an O(1 /K ) discretization-error dependence under standard regularity conditions, together with empirical evidence that the proposed velocity-variation statistic tracks within-trajectory sensitivity to temporal discretization. Our results demonstrate that online contextual step selection provides a practical mechanism for adapting inference computation across inputs while remaining compatible with pretrained flow models and existing inference-time acceleration techniques. 9References

Discussion of the assumptions. The result requires standard regularity conditions for forward Euler: Lipschitz continuity of the velocity field in the state variable and a uniform bound Mi on the derivative of the velocity field along the exact trajectory. The constant Mi captures input-dependent variation in the conditional flow dynamics. The theorem characterizes numerical integration error independently of the surrogate reward used by C OFLOW for adaptive step selection.

A.4 Complementarity of Our Approach 

Table 11 evaluates C OFLOW in combination with existing inference-time acceleration methods. The two mechanisms can therefore be applied jointly. For each combination, C OFLOW first selects the integration budget, after which the corresponding acceleration method is applied during generation. As shown in Table 11, combining adaptive step selection with TeaCache, FastFlow, and FlowCast further reduces runtime relative to using each acceleration method alone, although the magnitude of the gain and its effect on generation quality vary across methods. These results demonstrate that adaptive step-budget selection can be combined with existing inference-time acceleration techniques, providing an additional mechanism for reducing computation without requiring modifications to the underlying generative model.

A.6 L1-Relative Velocity Change as a Surrogate for Trajectory Variation 

We use the L1-relative velocity change in Eq. 3 as a lightweight, model-internal measure of variation along the generated flow trajectory. Larger values of ˆv(x, α ) indicate stronger changes in the learned velocity field across integration timesteps and may therefore reflect greater sensitivity to coarse temporal discretization. Importantly, ˆv(x, α ) is not a direct estimate of discretization error or perceptual quality, but an observable trajectory-level surrogate that requires neither a reference solution nor an external quality model. Figure 6 illustrates the velocity-change profiles across different prompts and integration budgets. To more directly examine the relationship with discretization sensitivity, we additionally compare generations obtained with step budgets {5, 10 , . . . , 50 } against a 100-step reference generated from the identical initial latent. We measure reference deviation using the ℓ2 distance between terminal latent states. Across eight representative GenEval prompts, ˆv is strongly associated with reference deviation as the integration budget varies, with a mean within-prompt Spearman correlation of 0.924 

(median 0.970 ); pooled across all prompt–budget pairs, the correlation is ρ = 0 .822 (p < 10 −16 ). A similar association is observed using the perceptual LPIPS distance (Spearman ρ = 0 .676 ,

p < 10 −11 ), indicating that the trend is not specific to latent-space distance. Both ˆv and reference deviation vary systematically with the integration budget, so these correlations should be interpreted as evidence that ˆv tracks within-trajectory discretization sensitivity , rather than as evidence that it independently ranks generation difficulty across prompts. Indeed, when controlling for the step budget and comparing prompts at fixed α, the mean cross-prompt Spearman correlation is 0.255 in this pilot. B.1 Choice of Context Representation as MiniLM 

A key component of our approach is the construction of a lightweight yet expressive context represen-tation for each prompt. We employ a pretrained SentenceTransformer (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) to obtain semantic embeddings, which are combined with handcrafted complexity features. 

Why SentenceTransformer (MiniLM)? We use all-MiniLM-L6-v2 as a compact semantic rep-resentation of the prompt.
