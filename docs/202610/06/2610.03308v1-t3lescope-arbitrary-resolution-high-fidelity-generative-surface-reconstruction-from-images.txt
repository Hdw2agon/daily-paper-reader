ABSTRACT
We reconstruct high-fidelity 3D scene meshes from posed multi-view images
without per-scene optimization, across scales ranging from single objects to large
outdoor scenes. Per-scene optimization methods lack the learned 3D prior needed
when observations are sparse or surfaces are glossy or transparent. Existing gen-
erative methods leverage such priors to complete geometry in sparsely observed
regions, but typically operate at a fixed resolution over a limited spatial extent,
trading spatial coverage against detail. Reconstructing a large scene therefore of-
ten requires partitioning it into independently processed overlapping local regions,
making it difficult to maintain global geometric consistency. To address these
issues, we propose T3lescope, which applies a single fixed-resolution generator
across scene scales in an inference-time coarse-to-fine cascade. A coarse level es-
tablishes the scene layout, and finer levels perturb and denoise geometry inherited
from the coarser level within progressively finer spatial cells to recover surface
detail. The model is trained on individual cells at multiple scales and shares its
weights across all levels, so no hierarchy is fixed during training, and the num-
ber of levels, cell scales, and cell locations are determined at inference time. On

INTRODUCTION
Reconstructing high-fidelity 3D meshes from posed multi-view images is important for a broad
range of real-world applications, including robotic perception, simulation, digital twins, and immer-
sive environments. These applications require both fine surface detail and globally consistent scene
geometry, across targets ranging from single objects to large outdoor scenes. Existing approaches
broadly fall into three categories: per-scene optimization, feed-forward prediction, and generative
reconstruction. Optimization-based methods fit a scene-specific 3D representation to the input im-
ages and can recover accurate surfaces from dense observations (Wang et al., 2021; Huang et al.,
2024; Gu´edon et al., 2025). However, they require optimization for every scene and, lacking a
learned 3D prior, fail under sparse views, occlusion, or glossy, reflective, and transparent surfaces.
Feed-forward methods address the cost of per-scene optimization by learning priors across many
scenes and directly predicting geometry such as depth or point maps (Wang et al., 2024; Lin et al.,
2026; Keetha et al., 2026). However, their outputs are typically image-aligned and must be fused
across views to form a complete mesh, where cross-view inconsistencies can introduce noise, holes,
or duplicate surfaces. Generative reconstruction addresses both issues by conditioning a learned 3D
generator on the input views. Like feed-forward methods, it avoids per-scene optimization, while
its learned prior directly predicts a complete 3D representation, including weakly constrained sur-
faces (Xiang et al., 2025; 2026). Its main limitation is scale: current 3D generators typically operate
on a fixed voxel grid at a single scene scale, trading spatial coverage against geometric detail. Gen-
Recon (Schmid et al., 2026), the closest prior work, extends such a fixed-resolution generator to large
scenes using overlapping local cells with MultiDiffusion (Bar-Tal et al., 2023). While this increases
spatial coverage, distant regions interact only indirectly through overlaps, making global geometry
difficult to maintain. Coarse-to-fine 3D generators offer another solution, but existing approaches
fix their hierarchy, spatial extent, and output resolution during training (Ren et al., 2024a;b). These
limitations motivate a different question: can a single fixed-resolution generator be composed across
scene scales at inference time, from global structure to progressively finer detail?
To this end, we propose T3lescope, an inference-time coarse-to-fine cascade that reuses the same
generator across multiple scene scales. A coarse level reconstructs the full scene and establishes its
global structure, while each finer level refines inherited geometry by halving the cell size, perturbing
it with noise, and denoising it to recover finer surface detail. The same weights are shared across all
levels and trained on individual cells at multiple scene scales, without paired coarse-to-fine super-
vision. Thus, cell size, layout, and cascade depth determine spatial coverage and output resolution
at inference time. This flexibility, however, makes image conditioning nontrivial: cells vary in po-
sition and physical scale across scenes and cascade levels, while each level requires image features
at a different resolution. To address this, we precompute reusable multi-resolution image features
and condition each voxel using its projected location and projected size. This makes conditioning
independent of absolute scene position and scale, allowing cells to be placed at arbitrary positions
and scales while providing the detail required at each cascade level.
We evaluate T3lescope on two public benchmarks, ScanNet++ and Tanks and Temples, and on our
own city-block dataset, under both sparse- and dense-view settings. It outperforms feed-forward
and generative baselines and is competitive with per-scene optimization. It recovers fine structures
as well as glossy and transparent surfaces. Results also show that our method generalizes across
diverse scenes, view counts, and image resolutions (Figures 1 and 3). Ablations validate both parent
initialization and image conditioning. Our contributions are:
• We propose an inference-time coarse-to-fine cascade that reuses one fixed-resolution 3D
generator across scene scales, with coverage and resolution chosen at inference time.
• We develop a cell-independent multi-scale conditioning scheme that reuses image features
across cells, levels, and sampling steps while allowing flexible cell placement and scale.

RELATED WORK
Mesh reconstruction. Mesh reconstruction from posed images has traditionally relied on multi-
view stereo, which matches pixels across images and fuses the recovered geometry (Sch¨onberger
et al., 2016). Neural implicit and Gaussian-based methods instead optimize a 3D representation
for each scene (Wang et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025; Gu´edon
et al., 2025; Gomez et al., 2026), with recent methods scaling to city-scale scenes (Liu et al., 2025).
These approaches recover accurate geometry from dense views and can be regularized with learned
monocular or geometric cues. However, they require per-scene optimization and tend to degrade
under sparse observations or with challenging glossy and transparent surfaces. Feed-forward meth-
ods instead learn scene priors that generalize to unseen scenes and predict image-aligned depth or
point maps in a single forward pass (Wang et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2026; Keetha et al., 2026; Guo
et al., 2025). While they avoid per-scene optimization, their predictions must be fused across views,
making the resulting mesh sensitive to cross-view inconsistencies. In contrast, our method directly
generates the mesh with a 3D generator, without either per-scene optimization or multi-view fusion.
Generative 3D reconstruction. Recent 3D generative models directly produce high-resolution
meshes through sparse structures and structured latents (Xiang et al., 2026). Pixel-aligned image
conditioning improves geometric fidelity via explicit 2D–3D correspondence (Li et al., 2026a). Gen-
Recon extends an object-level 3D generator to scene-scale reconstruction by tiling the scene with
local overlapping cells and jointly sampling them with MultiDiffusion (Schmid et al., 2026; Bar-Tal
et al., 2023). This extends generation beyond the model’s native spatial extent, but each denoiser
operates on a local cell, and distant regions interact only indirectly through neighboring overlaps.
Instead, our cascade propagates global structure from the coarse reconstruction to each finer cell.
Hierarchical generation.
Coarse-to-fine 3D generators reach high resolution with hierarchies
whose levels and scales are fixed during training, often with a separate model at each level (Ren
et al., 2024a;b; Meng et al., 2025). Methods for larger scenes scale spatial extent by progressively
extending the generated region (Wu et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2025; Joshi et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026b;
Meng et al., 2026). In 2D, cascaded diffusion models similarly train a separate model for each res-
olution (Ho et al., 2022), while training-free methods reuse the same model to progressively refine
upsampled samples (Meng et al., 2022; Du et al., 2024). We adapt this idea to 3D. At each finer
level, we reduce the cell size while keeping the generator resolution fixed. This allows the same
model to be reused across scales, with the hierarchy configured at inference time.

METHOD
We reconstruct a mesh M from posed multi-view images with a 3D generative model, without per-
scene optimization. Since the generator has a fixed resolution, we reconstruct the scene coarse-to-
fine. The coarsest level captures the scene, and finer levels refine it (Section 3.2). At each level, we
partition the scene into overlapping cells (Section 3.1) and apply the generator with corresponding
image features (Section 3.4). We train a single model across scales (Section 3.3) and configure the
hierarchy and spatial scale at inference time. Figure 2 shows the pipeline.
3.1
SINGLE-LEVEL SCENE GENERATION

structure. We refer to these two steps as the SS and mesh stages. The input is a set of posed images

3.2
INFERENCE-TIME COARSE-TO-FINE CASCADE
To overcome the limited context of single-level generation, we introduce a coarse-to-fine cascade.
The coarsest level covers a large spatial extent and establishes the global structure, while each sub-
sequent level refines the inherited geometry to recover finer surface detail. Each level halves the cell
size, with cell size at level l given by sl = s0/2l for l = 0, . . . , L −1 (Figure 2(a)).
At every level, we derive the child sparse structure from the parent mesh decoder Dshape. The
decoder upsamples the parent shape latent through subdivision stages, each doubling the spatial
resolution. We use the output at resolution 2R, where a child cell with half the parent edge length
corresponds to an R3 block of voxels, and take this block as the child’s sparse structure. Because
the parent geometry is estimated at a coarser resolution, subdivision alone leaves the surface coarse
and misaligned with the finer child grid. This provides a noisy initialization, which we refine with
the SS flow model at the child resolution (Figure 2(c)).

The parent also determines the child-cell layout by retaining only cells that intersect its predicted
geometry, restricting generation to relevant regions. To initialize the cells of the coarsest level and
per-cell view selection, we use the point cloud from Depth Anything 3 (DA3) (Lin et al., 2026). We
use the depth only for these two choices, and never fuse it into the output geometry (Appendix D.3).
3.3
MULTI-SCALE TRAINING OF A SHARED MODEL
Since the parent prediction is used only to initialize the next level at inference time via Equation 1,
training does not require paired coarse and fine examples. We train one SS flow and one mesh flow
on individual cells, using standard conditional flow matching (Lipman et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023).
We map cells of several edge lengths s to the same canonical coordinates and R3 grid and condition
the model on the images that observe each cell. Cells at different scales therefore differ only in
voxel spacing ∆= s/R. Neither s nor ∆is given to the model, so the same weights apply across
scales. Unlike XCube and SCube (Ren et al., 2024a;b), our hierarchy is not fixed during training,
so its depth, cell layout, and physical scales can be chosen at inference time without retraining.
Appendix E provides more details about training.
3.4
CELL-INDEPENDENT MULTI-SCALE IMAGE CONDITIONING
In the MultiDiffusion formulation above, every cell is evaluated at every flow step of the SS and
mesh stages, making feature reuse important for efficient inference. Pixel-aligned conditioning (Li
et al., 2026a; Schmid et al., 2026) projects each voxel into the input views, bilinearly samples one
feature per view, and aggregates them before the 3D generator. However, the DINOv3 token grid is
coarse, so the sampled features can lose the information needed for detailed surface reconstruction.
Pixal3D (Li et al., 2026a) addresses this by upsampling features to image resolution, but the upsam-
pled maps become costly to cache as image resolution and view count increase, and must then be
recomputed at every flow step. Aggregating before the generator also keeps the view weights from
depending on the current voxel state. We address these with voxel-queried image attention over
compact cached features and a cell-independent tile bank that provides each cell with scale-matched
image features without per-cell encoding.
Voxel-queried image attention. For a voxel p, we gather the DINO tokens within a w × w window
around its projection in each view, and let the current voxel feature hp attend to all of them with
a single softmax over tokens and views. Instead of bilinear sampling, we apply relative positional
encoding to the continuous offset from the voxel projection to the token center to preserve sub-
token position and local image context. As the query is the current voxel feature, the attention
weights adapt to the evolving 3D state across transformer blocks and flow steps. We also encode the
projected voxel size and ray direction (Li et al., 2025) (Appendix D.1). For finer detail, the mesh
stage also samples compact multi-stride ConvNeXt features (Liu et al., 2022) at the same projected
location. The DINO features remain on their native token grid, while the ConvNeXt features are
lightweight, allowing both to be cached for all views and reused across cells and flow steps.
Cell-independent tile bank. The base generator (Xiang et al., 2026) is pretrained on 512 × 512
images that frame the target region at a particular image scale. To match this pretraining setup, each
view should provide a crop that covers the cell projection, with pixel spacing matched to the cell’s
voxel spacing. GenRecon (Schmid et al., 2026) instead resizes each full image to a single resolution.
In wide scenes, where an image covers a large area, this can leave a fine cell with only a few pixels.
Instead of center-cropping the images around each cell projection, which would require separate
image encoding for every cell–view pair and dominate the cost, we build an isotropic pyramid for
each input image and divide each level into a fixed grid of 512 × 512 tiles (Figure 2(b)). Each tile is
defined by its source image, pyramid level, and crop origin, independently of the 3D cells. For each
view, a cell selects the level that matches its voxel spacing and the tiles covering its projection, so

4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Models and training data. We train two models (Appendix E): a general scene reconstruction
model and a city-block reconstruction model. We initialize both from a pretrained TRELLIS.2 (Xi-
ang et al., 2026) model, first fine-tune it for multi-view conditioning on TexVerse (Zhang et al.,
2025), and then fine-tune the two models independently on their respective datasets. The general
model is trained exclusively on synthetic data: the single-room datasets SAGE-10K (Xia et al., 2026)
and 3D-FRONT (Fu et al., 2021), together with our procedurally generated multi-room houses and
grass scenes. The city-block model is fine-tuned on our dataset of photographs captured in Japanese
cities and their paired building information models (BIMs). The exterior geometry of the BIMs
defines the training target and contains no people or vehicles (Figure 4; Appendix B.1).

Metrics. We report Chamfer distance (CD) in meters, F-score, and normal consistency (NC). F@τ

city blocks, the BIM meshes model surfaces unseen from the street, such as roofs, so we evaluate
whole blocks, cropping only the prediction to the ground-truth footprint. See Appendix B for details.

ABLATIONS AND ANALYSIS
Parent initialization. Each finer level starts its flow sampling from the parent’s prediction noised to
ts instead of pure noise (Section 3.2). We compare our default ts = 0.8 with ts = 0.9 and ts = 1 at
every finer level (Figure 5(a)). At ts = 1, the finer levels start from pure noise and inherit only the
cell placement. On the large ScanNet++ scenes with 32 views, lowering ts improves every metric
and reduces the Chamfer distance by 80%. Results with 256 views are in Appendix G.1. Figure 5(b)
shows a city block. Overlapping cells keep neighboring surfaces continuous, but information from
distant cells barely reaches a cell, and the roof is not visible from the street. Without the parent, the
roof breaks into fragments above the building. With the parent, all cells start from the same coarse
building, and the roof stays in place.
Image conditioning. Figure 6 compares, at the finest level, the GenRecon-style conditioner, our
voxel-queried attention (Section 3.4), and the latter with the ConvNeXt branch, which adds pixel-
level detail. The baseline bilinearly samples DINO features at projected voxel centers, which loses
detail finer than a token. On ScanNet++, voxel-queried attention alone is on par with the baseline,
and the ConvNeXt branch substantially improves every metric. On the human capture, the baseline
over-smooths the hair, the nose, and the shirt wrinkles. Voxel-queried attention recovers strands of
hair, and the ConvNeXt branch sharpens them further and adds the shirt buttons. Appendix G.2
shows further qualitative results.
Inference time. On large ScanNet++ scenes, a single H200 takes 17 (L1) and 89 (L2) minutes per
scene, comparable to per-scene optimization but slower than feed-forward methods (Appendix H).
5
CONCLUSION AND LIMITATIONS
We have presented T3lescope, a generative reconstruction framework that applies a single fixed-
resolution 3D generator across scene scales through an inference-time coarse-to-fine cascade. The
coarse level establishes a globally consistent layout, finer levels recover surface detail, and a cell-
independent multi-scale image conditioning gives every cell images at the resolution it needs. On
ScanNet++, Tanks and Temples, and city blocks, T3lescope outperforms the generative baseline and
is competitive with or better than feed-forward and per-scene optimization methods.

Since T3lescope does not fit the input images pixel by pixel, its surfaces can be slightly offset from
the observations, which lowers the F-score at tight thresholds when views are dense. Its cost grows
with the number of cells and cascade levels, so sampling large scenes at high resolution remains
slow, and it assumes posed images. Moreover, it does not work on distant objects in unbounded
scenes (Appendix A.5). In future work, we plan to reduce the sampling cost by distilling the flow
models into few-step samplers and to extend T3lescope to unbounded scenes.

B
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP DETAILS
B.1
DATASETS
ScanNet++.
We evaluate the 50 validation scenes of ScanNet++ (Yeshwanth et al., 2023) and
divide them by the volume of the ground-truth bounding box into a small and a large split of 25
scenes each. We use the undistorted DSLR images at 1752 × 1168 as inputs and choose the 32-
and 256-view sets by farthest-point sampling of the camera centers among the training frames not
flagged as bad. For the scenes with fewer than 256 frames, 12 in the small split and 9 in the large
split, we use all of them. The ground truth is the mesh reconstructed from the laser scans, with its
anonymized regions removed.
Tanks and Temples.
We evaluate six scenes of Tanks and Temples (Knapitsch et al., 2017). As
for ScanNet++, the 16-, 32-, and 256-view sets are chosen by farthest-point sampling of the camera
centers. truck has only 251 images and uses all of them in the 256-view setting. We extract the
frames from the 4K videos provided by the dataset and undistort them to match the official COLMAP
reconstruction, which gives images of approximately 3920 × 2180 (4K). For a fair comparison with
the baselines, the main paper uses these images downsampled by two to approximately 1960×1090
(2K), and Appendix A.2 reports additional results with the 4K images. The ground truth is the
official laser scan with its crop volume and alignment.
City blocks.
Our city-block dataset contains 41 blocks with 248 buildings and 139,893 posed
images at 9504 × 6336. We split it geographically into 38 training blocks with 220 buildings and
126,068 images, and three held-out evaluation blocks with 28 buildings and 13,825 images. The
blocks range from 42 × 22 m to 156 × 137 m in horizontal extent, and the buildings are 23 to 172 m
tall. All images are captured with a handheld camera at walking height, about 1 to 2 m above the
ground.
We register the images using RealityScan and an in-house SfM pipeline, and align the reconstruction
to a laser scan of the block to obtain metric scale. The exterior BIM of each building is modeled
in the coordinate frame of the same scan. The cameras and the BIM are therefore aligned without
any further registration. We use the mesh derived from the BIM as the ground truth. It represents
building exteriors, vegetation, and street furniture down to signs and traffic cones, but not people or
vehicles. Each image is accompanied by masks of the sky, people, and vehicles. We will release the
evaluation split, including the images, a 3D Gaussian Splatting model of each block, and the BIM
meshes. For evaluation, each held-out block is reconstructed from 256 of its images, sampled at a
uniform stride over the capture sequence.
B.2
METRICS
3D metrics.
Let P and G denote the points sampled from the predicted mesh and the ground truth,
respectively, following each protocol (Appendix B.3). The Chamfer distance (CD) is the average of
the mean nearest-neighbor distances from P to G and from G to P, in meters and without truncation.
Precision and recall at a threshold τ are the fractions of points in P and in G whose nearest neighbor
in the other set lies within τ, and the F-score F@τ is their harmonic mean, with τ in centimeters.
Normal consistency (NC) is the mean absolute cosine similarity between the normal of each point in
P and G and that of its nearest neighbor in the other set, with pairs more than 20 cm apart counted
as zero.
2D metrics.
We follow the 2D metrics of GenRecon (Schmid et al., 2026). We render depth
and normal maps of the predicted and ground-truth meshes and compute the errors on the pixels
covered by both. We report the mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean squared error (RMSE) of
depth in meters, the absolute relative error (AbsRel) mean |ˆz −z|/z, and the squared relative error
(SqRel) mean(ˆz −z)2/z, where ˆz and z are the predicted and ground-truth depths. Ang is the mean
angular error in degrees between the rendered normals, which are interpolated from area-weighted
vertex normals. Completeness (Comp) is the fraction of pixels covered by the ground truth that are
also covered by the prediction. For depth, the main paper reports MAE and AbsRel, and Table 3
additionally reports RMSE and SqRel.

B.3
EVALUATION PROTOCOLS
ScanNet++.
Although ScanNet++ registers the camera poses to the ground-truth mesh, the reg-
istration leaves residual errors on some scenes that are not negligible at the F@2 threshold. We
therefore rigidly align the ground-truth mesh to the COLMAP sparse points of each scene with ICP
before evaluation. This alignment is shared by all methods.
We compute the 2D metrics following GenRecon (Schmid et al., 2026), rendering both meshes from
the cameras of the official test split, invalidating masked pixels, and pooling the errors over all pixels
of all frames. As its evaluation code is not released, we reimplement it based on the paper.
For the 3D metrics, the main paper uses the virtual-scan protocol of GaussianWrapping (Gomez
et al., 2026), which scores only the surfaces that the cameras see and the ground truth contains.
Unlike the original protocol, which uses all cameras of the capture, we use up to 300 held-out
cameras chosen by farthest-point sampling among the frames that are neither input views of the run
nor flagged as bad. We cast only the rays that hit the ground truth against the prediction, so a missing
surface lowers recall and a surface outside the ground truth is never scored.
Table 4 also reports the 3D evaluation protocol of GenRecon, which samples points uniformly over
the complete meshes after discarding predicted triangles farther than 15 cm from the ground truth.
We run its released code with four million points per mesh instead of 200,000 to reduce sampling
noise. Since the ground truth misses windows, mirrors, ceilings, and surfaces behind furniture, this
protocol cannot distinguish a hole in the ground truth from a hole in the prediction.
Tanks and Temples.
We use the virtual-scan protocol of GaussianWrapping. The protocol takes
the points seen from all cameras of the official trajectory, crops them to the official volume, and
aligns them to the ground truth by the released transform refined with ICP. F@τ uses the official
per-scene threshold τ of 3 mm for ignatius, 5 mm for caterpillar and truck, 10 mm for
barn and meetingroom, and 25 mm for courthouse. The main paper reports CD divided
by τ to average over scenes, and Table 5 reports the per-scene CD in meters. On both ScanNet++
and Tanks and Temples, all methods are scored by the same implementation. Since our meshes can
reach hundreds of millions of triangles, we implement a custom rendering and evaluation script that
handles such meshes without decimation.
City blocks.
As the BIM ground truth covers complete building exteriors, we compare the com-
plete meshes rather than only the surfaces seen by the cameras. We crop only the reconstructions
to the ground-truth footprint, the ground-plane projection of the ground-truth mesh, to remove their
surroundings outside the block. Since NC counts only pairs within 20 cm, on this dataset it mainly
reflects how much of the surface is recovered.
B.4
BASELINES
All baselines receive the same input views, camera poses, and intrinsics, and are evaluated on the
meshes they produce. We use the official implementations and checkpoints. On ScanNet++ and
Tanks and Temples, our method and the per-scene optimization methods take the input images at
full resolution on ScanNet++ and at 2K on Tanks and Temples. The feed-forward methods and Gen-
Recon resize them to the resolution expected by each model. The rows marked 4K in Table 5 instead
give our method the higher-resolution images of Tanks and Temples. On city blocks, our method
takes the original 9504×6336 images, whereas the baselines take undistorted images downsampled
to 1920 × 1280. All methods are also given the extent of the block from the ground truth.
2DGS (Huang et al., 2024) uses a depth ratio of 1 and a distortion weight of 10 on ScanNet++
and city blocks, and the released settings on Tanks and Temples, with the masks applied to the
photometric loss on city blocks. MILo (Gu´edon et al., 2025) uses the released fast configuration,
with its preset for indoor scenes on ScanNet++ and the released evaluation settings on Tanks and
Temples. GaussianWrapping (Gomez et al., 2026) uses the released training and mesh extraction
settings. CityGaussianV2 (Liu et al., 2025) uses the released street-scene configuration, which
trains a coarse model and fine-tunes 2 × 2 partitions before merging them, with depth supervision
from Depth Anything V2 (Yang et al., 2024) and the masks.

DA3 (Lin et al., 2026) uses DA3-GIANT-1.1 conditioned on the input poses and intrinsics and
processes all views in a single forward pass, so that one scale converts the depth to meters. On
ScanNet++, each image is split into four virtual cameras with narrower fields of view. MapAny-

released code in the ScanNet++ mode with the default settings, conditioning on all input views.
Mesh extraction. MILo, GaussianWrapping, and GenRecon output meshes directly. The other
methods produce depth maps, which we fuse by TSDF fusion in Open3D (Zhou et al., 2018) with
the ground-truth poses, keeping the 50 largest connected components. The voxel size, truncation,
and maximum depth are 1 cm, 4 cm, and 4.5 m on ScanNet++, following GenRecon, and 5 cm, 8 cm,
and 200 m on city blocks. On Tanks and Temples, we use the original per-scene settings of 2DGS.
For 2DGS on ScanNet++, we use the voxel size of 0.6 cm and truncation of 2.4 cm that its authors
use for large Tanks and Temples scenes. For CityGaussianV2, pixels with opacity below 0.5 are
discarded before fusion. On city blocks, we mask the depth and crop the volume to the ground-truth
extent enlarged by 10 m. Since Murre aligns each view to its own sparse depth, overlapping views
are not made consistent, and on city blocks its fused mesh covers only a small part of the ground
truth (Table 6).

ADDITIONAL METHOD DETAILS
The full inference procedure for one scene is as follows. The subsections below describe the gen-
erator (Appendix C.1), its training (Appendix C.2), the scene-wide blending (Appendix C.3), the
transfer between levels (Appendix C.4), and the cell sizes and extents (Appendix C.5).
1. For each level l = 0, . . . , L −1, with cell edge length sl = s0/2l:
(a) Place the cells. At level 0, they cover the scene extent; at finer levels, they cover the
surface predicted by the parent (Appendices C.5 and C.4).
(b) Select the conditioning views and tiles for each cell (Appendix D.3).
(c) Encode the tiles selected at this level into the tile bank, encoding each tile only once
even if selected by multiple cells (Appendix D.2).
(d) Run the SS stage on the scene-wide SS latent grid with MultiDiffusion, starting from
noise at level 0 and from the noised parent latent at finer levels, and decode the pre-
dicted occupancy bO(l).
(e) Run the mesh stage on the active voxels of bO(l) with MultiDiffusion.
(f) Unless l is the finest level, decode the shape latent up to resolution 2R and encode the
blocks of the child cells as their inherited SS latents.
2. Decode the shape latent of the finest level, and optionally of other levels (Appendix F.2),
into a mesh with margin-based chunk decoding (Appendix F.1).
C.1
TWO-STAGE 3D FLOW BACKBONE
Our generator follows the two-stage design of TRELLIS.2 (Xiang et al., 2026). It operates on one
cell in canonical cell coordinates (Appendix C.2) and is conditioned on the posed images I that
observe the cell (Section 3.1). The SS stage generates the occupancy O ∈{0, 1}R3 of the cell with
R = 64, where active voxels (O = 1) contain surface geometry. The mesh stage then generates
a shape latent on the active voxels, which the shape VAE decoder converts into a triangle mesh at
5123 resolution. Both stages are latent flow-matching models. The SS latent of an occupancy is
x0 = ASS(ESS(O)), where ESS is the SS VAE encoder and ASS normalizes latents using the mean
and standard deviation of the training latents. The shape latent is obtained from the ground-truth
mesh by the shape VAE encoder. We keep both pretrained VAEs frozen (Appendix E.1) and train
only the two flow models.

C.2
MULTI-SCALE TRAINING WITH SHARED WEIGHTS
For a cubic cell with center oc and edge length s, we map a world-space point p to canonical cell
coordinates (p −oc)/s and transform the ground-truth geometry and camera poses in the same
way. We therefore encode every cell at the same SS and shape resolutions regardless of its size,
and s changes only the world-space voxel spacing ∆= s/R. Small cells represent fine geometry,
whereas large cells cover more context with the same latent budget.
The general-scene model is trained with cell edge lengths from two ladders: {1, 2, 4} m and
{1.5, 3, 6} m (Appendix E.5). To expose the model evenly to different scene scales, we sample
the cell size explicitly: for each training example, we first choose a training data source with the
sampling share of Appendix E.3, then one of its edge lengths with equal probability, and finally a
cell of that size from one of its scenes. This avoids biasing training toward smaller cells, which are
more numerous under spatial sampling. Each stage uses a single set of weights shared across all
cell sizes. Each training example consists of only one cell; we do not train on parent–child pairs or
multi-level trajectories.

coarse level can still generate. Parent-guided placement is used even at ts = 1, where noise replaces
the inherited latent.

CELL SIZES AND EXTENTS
ScanNet++ and the city blocks use metric coordinates: in both, the camera poses are aligned to
laser scans (Appendix B.1). On these datasets, cell edge lengths are specified in meters: 4, 2, and
1 m on ScanNet++, and 80, 40, 20, and 10 m on city blocks. The poses for Tanks and Temples and
the three real-world captures (garden from Mip-NeRF 360, coffee martini from N3DV (Li

camera translations so that the median distance from the cameras to their centroid equals a target

capture. Cell sizes are defined in these normalized units: 4, 2, and 1 units on Tanks and Temples,
garden, and coffee martini, and 2.8, 1.4, and 0.7 units on the human capture.
The coarsest cells cover a horizontal extent and a height range. The height range is computed from

meetingroom (Tanks and Temples), and coffee martini. City blocks use the footprint of the
ground-truth mesh, while the remaining Tanks and Temples scenes and the human capture use the
convex hull of the camera trajectory. On garden, cascade levels cover nested squares with half-
widths of 16, 8, and 4 units around the camera centroid, and their meshes are combined as described
in Appendix F.2.

IMAGE CONDITIONING AND SCENE PREPARATION
D.1
VOXEL-QUERIED IMAGE ATTENTION
This section details the voxel-queried image attention introduced in Section 3.4. It replaces the im-
age cross-attention in every transformer block of both flows. In the mesh flow, each voxel attends to
two types of image features: DINOv3 tokens around its projection in each view, and ConvNeXt fea-
tures sampled at the projection. Below, we first contrast this design with pixel-aligned conditioning,
and then describe the DINO branch (projection, geometric encoding, and attention), the ConvNeXt
branch, and the variant used in the SS flow.
Difference from pixel-aligned conditioning.
Pixel-aligned generators sample one feature per
view at the voxel projection and combine views before the 3D network. This design has two limita-
tions in our setting.
The first is resolution. A DINO token covers a 16 × 16-pixel patch; thus, features sampled from
the token grid, even with bilinear interpolation, lack direct information finer than 16 pixels, which
is insufficient for reconstructing fine mesh details. Pixal3D (Li et al., 2026a) upsamples DINO
features to image resolution before sampling. For a 512 × 512 tile, the upsampled 1,024-channel
BF16 feature map requires
5122 · 1024 · 2 bytes = 0.5 GiB per view,
(6)
and 64 views require 32 GiB. MultiDiffusion evaluates every cell at every flow step, so features
exceeding GPU memory must be recomputed at every evaluation.
The second is view aggregation.
Pixal3D averages sampled features over views, and GenRe-
con (Schmid et al., 2026) replaces the average with a learned aggregator conditioned solely on
image features. In both approaches, the view weights are independent of the voxel feature.
Our attention addresses both limitations. For resolution, the attention score receives the offset from
the voxel projection to each token center, preserving the sub-token position without upsampling.
Shallow ConvNeXt feature maps provide image detail finer than 16 pixels at about 1% of the mem-
ory of upsampled DINO features. Both features are small enough to keep in memory for all views,
so we compute them once and reuse them across cells and flow steps. For aggregation, we keep the
tokens of all views separate until the attention step and use the current voxel feature as the query, so
that the view weights depend on the voxel feature. The attention score also incorporates the apparent
voxel size and the viewing ray.

D.3
DA3-BASED LAYOUT AND VIEW SELECTION
Running the generator on a new capture requires two decisions outside the model: where to place
the coarsest cells, and which tiles each cell reads. We make both decisions using depth from Depth
Anything 3 (DA3) (Lin et al., 2026), run with input poses and intrinsics as in the DA3 baseline
(Appendix B.4). We use DA3 solely for these decisions: we do not feed its depth or features to the
flow models, fuse them into the output mesh, or use them to optimize it.
Cell placement.
At the coarsest level, we discard the 10% of DA3 depths with lowest confidence,
unproject the remainder, and place cells over the volume that these points occupy. For some scenes,
we additionally use the camera hull or ground-truth footprint as in Appendix C.5. The volume is
covered by cells on a half-cell-stride lattice. At finer levels, candidate cells are derived from the SS
occupancy predicted at the parent level in world coordinates. We dilate this occupancy by one parent
SS voxel before intersecting it with the child lattice, so that a small miss at the coarse level does not
remove the region from all finer levels.
Visibility gate.
We determine which views observe each voxel center p of the 163 SS latent grid of
a cell. A view is considered to observe p if p projects within the image frame and is not occluded in
the DA3 depth map. The occlusion test tolerates larger depth discrepancies where DA3 confidence
is low. On city blocks, we ignore sky, people, and vehicles in this test, using masks predicted by an
off-the-shelf segmentation model. The generator still receives the unmasked images.

E
TRAINING DETAILS
We train two models sharing the same architecture: a general-scene model evaluated on ScanNet++,
Tanks and Temples, and real-world captures, and a city-scale model specialized for our city-block
dataset. Both models start from the same initialization (Appendix E.1) and shared multi-view pre-
training on TexVerse (Appendix E.2), differing only in their final scene-level fine-tuning data (Ap-
pendices E.3 and E.4). Both employ the same inference procedure (Appendix C). The remaining
subsections detail scale and rotation augmentations (Appendix E.5) and optimization settings (Ap-
pendix E.6).
E.1
MODEL INITIALIZATION
Both flows are initialized from the official TRELLIS.2 weights. The SS flow retains the original
30-block architecture. For the mesh flow, we prune the 30-block backbone down to 15 transformer
blocks by removing the blocks that affect its output the least, reducing the denoiser from approxi-
mately 1.3B to 655M parameters while preserving the hidden dimension, attention heads, and latent
resolution of the retained blocks. To recover from pruning, we fine-tune the pruned mesh flow
with the official single-image conditioning for 50k steps on 16 H200 GPUs with a learning rate of
3 × 10−5. The voxel-queried image attention and the ConvNeXt branch are subsequently trained
during image-conditioned multi-view training.
We freeze the pretrained SS and shape VAEs, which encode the clean SS and shape targets and
decode generated latents. We update only the two flow networks and their conditioning layers during
training.
E.2
SHARED MULTI-VIEW PRETRAINING
After initialization, we train both flows on multi-view renderings of TexVerse (Zhang et al., 2025).
This stage adapts the single-object generators, alongside the new conditioning modules, to posed
multi-view inputs. The resulting checkpoint serves as the starting point for scene-level fine-tuning
(Appendices E.3 and E.4). Training follows a curriculum over mesh resolution and the number of
randomly sampled conditioning views. The mesh flow is trained first with 4 views at mesh resolution
2563, then with 4 views at 5123, and finally with 16 views at 5123. The SS flow, whose occupancy
targets have a resolution of 643, is trained with 4 and subsequently 16 views. Conditioning images
are rendered or cropped to 512 × 512.
E.3
GENERAL-SCENE FINE-TUNING
Starting from the TexVerse checkpoint (Appendix E.2), we fine-tune both flows of the general-scene
model on multi-scale scene data. In addition to the single-room scene datasets SAGE-10K (Xia

E.5
SCALE AND ROTATION AUGMENTATION OF CELLS
We augment training cells in both scale and orientation.
39


Preprint
Scale.
We assign each room or house scene one of the two scale ladders {1, 2, 4} m and
{1.5, 3, 6} m and partition it into cells of those sizes, which gives six cell sizes across the dataset.
Because a scene yields far more small cells than large ones, we sample cells with the scale-balanced
scheme of Appendix C.2 to avoid over-representing small cells.
Rotation.
We rotate each cell independently of its scale in two ways. First, we rotate the cell about
the vertical axis by a uniformly sampled angle and tilt the vertical axis by up to 10◦. We sample
the tilt angle from a distribution concentrated near zero, which keeps most cells nearly upright
while making the model robust to camera poses that are not perfectly level. Second, we apply
rotations by multiples of 90◦about the vertical axis alongside reflection flips, yielding eight discrete
orientations per cell. The same transform is applied to the target geometry and the camera poses, and
reflections also flip the images. Because these eight discrete transforms only permute the voxel grid,
we apply them directly to the dual-grid representation of TRELLIS.2 (Xiang et al., 2026) instead of
recomputing it from the mesh. We still re-encode the SS and shape latents of each transformed cell,
because neither VAE encoder is equivariant to these transforms. Because the tile bank is independent
of cells (Appendix D.2), these augmentations leave the image processing unchanged: all cell variants
access the same tiles and differ only in their relative camera transforms.
E.6
OPTIMIZATION SETTINGS
Both flows are optimized using AdamW with a constant learning rate, weight decay of 0.01, β =
(0.9, 0.95), and ϵ = 10−8, with BF16 mixed precision and a per-GPU batch size of 8. The learning
rate is 10−4 during the 4-view phases of the mesh flow, 5 × 10−5 in other pretraining phases, and
10−5 during scene-level fine-tuning. For classifier-free guidance, the conditioning input is dropped
with probability 0.1. We set σmin = 10−5 in the flow path of Equation 2. TexVerse pretraining
(Appendix E.2) runs on 16 to 32 NVIDIA H200 GPUs for approximately 80k steps for the SS flow
and 200k steps for the mesh flow in total. Scene-level fine-tuning (Appendices E.3 and E.4) runs for
25,000 steps per flow on 8 H200 GPUs.
F
SCALABLE MESH DECODING
F.1
MARGIN-BASED CHUNK DECODING AND STITCHING
We decode each cascade level into a unified scene mesh. Decoding cells independently would
truncate the shape VAE receptive field at cell boundaries, producing visible seams. Instead, we
assemble the shape tokens across all cells into a single sparse tensor for the level. Because cells
overlap by 50% during MultiDiffusion (Appendix C.3), we select every other cell along each axis to
partition the volume without overlap.
Because decoding the entire level at once exceeds GPU memory, we process it in blocks of 43 cells.
Each block is padded with a 4-voxel latent margin from its neighbors, so that voxels near the block
boundary are decoded with their surroundings. Each block outputs only the mesh faces belonging to
its interior region, preventing duplicate faces between adjacent blocks. We merge the copies of each
boundary vertex produced by adjacent blocks into a single vertex, identifying them by their integer
voxel coordinates. The blocks thus join into one mesh without duplicate faces or open seams.
F.2
COMBINING CASCADE LEVELS
In object-centered captures such as Mip-NeRF 360 (Barron et al., 2022), cameras orbit a central
subject observed closely from many angles, while the background is seen only from afar. Because
the background appears too small in the images to show fine detail, we do not reconstruct it at the
finest resolution: we run the coarsest level over the full bounding volume and restrict successive
refinement levels to concentric squares centered on the subject. For the garden scene in Figure 1,
we employ three cascade levels, with each finer level covering half the horizontal extent of its pre-
decessor.
Each level yields an independent mesh, which we merge hierarchically. Because finer meshes pro-
vide higher fidelity in the central region, we remove coarse faces whose centroids fall within the
boundary of the next finer level, and combine the remaining outer ring with the finer mesh. This
40


Preprint
ring composition is used only for the nonuniform qualitative reconstructions; on ScanNet++ and
Tanks and Temples, all levels share identical spatial extents, and the finest level is evaluated directly.

G.3
TILE PYRAMID LEVEL SELECTION
For each view, a cell attends to the pyramid level matching its voxel spacing (Section 3.4). As
detailed in Appendix D.3, we choose this level so that one SS voxel covers approximately 8 pixels
(Equation 24). We compare this adaptive selection with two fixed levels shared across all cells:
Coarse selects the coarsest level whose image height is at least 512 pixels (584 pixels on ScanNet++
and approximately 546 pixels on Tanks and Temples), whereas Fine uses the full-resolution images.
Each configuration uses a separate model trained with the corresponding selection rule under the
same schedule. We report every cascade level with 32 input views, on ScanNet++ in Table 10 and
on Tanks and Temples in Table 11.
On ScanNet++ (Table 10), the images are 1168 pixels high, so Coarse halves them only once, to
584 pixels, and keeps most of their resolution. Coarse is therefore competitive on this dataset: it
achieves a slightly higher F@2 and a similar CD at levels 1 and 2. The adaptive selection still yields
the lowest MAE across all cascade levels and scene groups, and Fine underperforms the adaptive
selection in nearly every column.
In contrast, on Tanks and Temples (Table 11), the inputs are 4K images about 2180 pixels high, so
Coarse downsamples them by a factor of four, discarding high-frequency details needed by finer
cells. As a result, Coarse falls behind at level 2, with a mean F@τ of 0.2305 compared to 0.2937.
While Fine performs best on certain scenes (e.g., Barn), the adaptive selection achieves the best
mean F@τ and CD/τ across all cascade levels.

INFERENCE TIME
Table 13 reports the inference time for each cascade level on five large ScanNet++ scenes with 32
input views on a single NVIDIA H200 GPU. Because each level halves the cell size, the number of
cells and the total computation can grow by roughly 8× per level, and by more depending on the
cell layout. In practice, the number of cells grows from 31 to 470 to 2,490, and runtime increases
by 8.2× from level 0 to level 1 but only by 4.7× from level 1 to level 2, because finer levels allocate
cells only where the parent level detects surfaces. Reconstruction up to level 1 takes an average of 17
minutes per scene, and up to level 2 takes 89 minutes, which is comparable to per-scene optimization
methods. Flow sampling in the SS and mesh stages accounts for most of the runtime at all levels,
whereas view selection, tile preparation, and parent transfer require less than 3 minutes combined.
Peak GPU memory usage is 79 GiB at the finest level.
